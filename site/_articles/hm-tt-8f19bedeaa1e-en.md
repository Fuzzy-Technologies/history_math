In elementary geometry, the compass is one of the main tools for geometrical constructions in the plane, alongside the straightedge. Euclid's Elements already named the compass and straightedge as the basic tools for constructing geometrical objects. In geometry, both instruments are treated as idealized.

The drawing compass (from Latin circulus, “circle, circumference”) is an instrument for drawing circles and arcs and for measuring lengths.

An ordinary compass consists of two metal legs joined by a hinge. One ends in a point and the other in a pencil, or a ruling pen, chalk or felt-tip pen. Dividers have points on both legs.

A special set containing a compass together with accessories such as interchangeable leads and needles, other instruments such as calipers, dividers and a ruling pen for drawing ink lines, and a case with fitted recesses for them is called a drawing-instrument set.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The history of the compass spans several thousand years. The earliest depictions of circles date to prehistoric times. Surviving drawn circles suggest that the Babylonians and Assyrians already used compasses. The wheel was invented in Babylon at the same time. The earliest chariots also appear in excavations of rich tombs from the late 3rd millennium BCE and later.

An iron compass was found in a Gallic burial mound of the 1st century CE in what is now France.

In Yelizaryev's first Russian book, the compass is called a kruzhalnik. The name kruzhalo also occurs; in building, this means a wooden or metal arch-shaped support on which a vault or arch is built. Excavations in Veliky Novgorod uncovered a steel cutting compass used to make ornaments of small circles. Such patterns were widespread in Rus'.

Three main kinds of compass are known: the rope compass, the vernier caliper and the caliper.

A rope compass is a rope with distances marked on it, usually by knots, or rods attached at its ends. It is used where a large-radius circular arc is required, for example when marking out sports arenas, where a rigid compass is inconvenient.

The Egyptians already knew the surveying compass: a type of caliper forming a rigid structure of three sticks fixed so that the distance between the ends of two remains constant. This surveying instrument was also used in Rus'. Many other modifications of calipers were invented as well.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Calipers are compasses with curved legs for measuring solid objects. They measure the outside diameter of products and components.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

The vernier caliper is an improvement on the rope compass, used mainly for measurement. It measures the external and internal dimensions of an object and the depths of holes and projections.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

Proportional compasses existed in the Pythagorean school. They were intended to change the dimensions of a measured or designed object proportionally in a predetermined ratio. One such compass was found in the excavations at Pompeii.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

Scholars in Islamic countries developed compasses for constructing conic sections as well.

Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519) created a device called a parabolic compass.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-8" %}

Thus, the main types of compass are used for:

- drawing circles;
- taking and transferring linear dimensions, with marking compasses or dividers;
- changing the scale of a measured dimension, with proportional compasses;
- constructing parabolas and ellipses, with parabolic and elliptical compasses.

The compass has always been an indispensable aid to architects and builders. It is used beyond draughting, navigation and cartography, including in medicine: large and small spreading calipers measure the transverse dimensions of the human body and the dimensions of the skull respectively.

The compass even appeared on the coat of arms of the GDR, symbolizing the intelligentsia.

The origins and uses of such simple, familiar instruments as the compass are closely connected with the development of the sciences, great discoveries and humanity's overall progress.
