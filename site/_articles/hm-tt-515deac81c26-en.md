A sextant, from Latin *sextantis*, “sixth”, is a navigational angle-measuring instrument used to measure the altitude of celestial bodies above the horizon, or angles between visible objects, in order to determine the observer's geographical coordinates.

It measures angles when the observer's position is unsteady, as in maritime and air navigation. Its most important feature is that it allows the observer to bring the two objects between which the angle is measured together in the same field of view.

The sextant's scale spans 1/6 of a full circle, or 60°. Isaac Newton proposed the principle of its construction as early as 1699, but it was not put into practice during his lifetime. The quadrant was an early precursor of the sextant.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The sextant was first constructed in 1730 by John Hadley in England and independently by Thomas Godfrey in the United States. The first instruments had measuring arcs of 1/8 of a circle and were called octants. The arc was later enlarged to 1/6, giving rise to the name “sextant”.

The sextant's parts are mounted on a frame formed by two radii and an arc called the limb. A small mirror and filters are fixed to the left-hand radius. Half the small mirror's surface is transparent. At the vertex of the frame, a large mirror is attached to a moving radius called the alidade. At the other end of the alidade is a reading drum divided into 60 minute graduations. One turn of the drum moves the alidade through half a degree, while the image seen through both mirrors moves twice as far, through one degree. The limb's graduations account for this feature. For more precise alignment of the images, a telescope is fitted into a special support on the frame.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

The sextant eventually displaced the astrolabe as the principal navigational instrument. “Sextant” was also the name of an older astronomical instrument. It was this instrument, rather than the navigational one, that Johannes Hevelius commemorated in the sky with the constellation Sextans. Early sextants are preserved at the National Maritime Museum in Greenwich, London; the Maritime Museum in Amsterdam; and the Science Museum in London.

Today, the sextant is used as an alternative to satellite navigation.

In the school astronomy course, it is usually either absent altogether or mentioned only briefly. Although it is not an essential instrument in school practice, its widespread use among sailors, pilots and travellers makes it worthwhile to introduce students to both its construction and its theory through extracurricular club activities.
