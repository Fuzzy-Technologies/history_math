*Marking the 230th anniversary of his birth*

Nikolai Dmitriyevich Brashman (14 [25] June 1796–13 [25] May 1866) was a Russian mathematician, researcher in mechanics and outstanding teacher, and a corresponding member of the Imperial St Petersburg Academy of Sciences. He was one of the founders of the Moscow Mathematical Society and wrote university courses on mechanics and analytic geometry.

## The Path to Moscow University

He was born in Moravia, near Brno, and studied at the University of Vienna. Arriving in Russia in 1823, he first taught in St Petersburg, then was invited to Kazan University. From 1834 his life was tied to Imperial Moscow University, where he lectured on applied mathematics and mechanics.

## Mechanics and Teaching

As a teacher, he was renowned for an unusually systematic approach. Mechanics became a coherent science connected with practice, engineering problems and a clear mathematical language, rather than a collection of formulas. In the 1840s he developed a course in practical mechanics that joined theory with technical applications, strongly influencing Moscow’s teaching tradition.

His research centred on hydromechanics and the development of the principle of least action. He published studies and wrote textbooks, among the best known being *Theory of the Equilibrium of Solid and Liquid Bodies: Statics and Hydrostatics* and *Theoretical Mechanics*.

The award of the Demidov Prize for the manuscript of his course on analytic geometry was an important recognition. He was later elected a corresponding member of the Academy of Sciences in 1855 and became an Honoured Professor of Moscow University in 1859.

## The Mathematical Society and a Teacher’s Legacy

Building a scientific community was another significant part of his life. In 1864 he became the first president of the Moscow Mathematical Society, which grew out of regular meetings and discussions. *Matematicheskii Sbornik*, Russia’s oldest mathematical journal, began appearing in the year of his death.

He is remembered for more than his publications and dates. His lectures and seminars helped form the Moscow school of mathematics and mechanics in the middle of the nineteenth century. That is a rare outcome of a scholar’s life: knowledge continuing to work through pupils, books and university traditions.
