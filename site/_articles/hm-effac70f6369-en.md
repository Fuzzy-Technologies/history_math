The last great mathematician of the Hellenistic age.

He was born in Perga and worked in Alexandria. He became renowned as a geometer and astronomer.

He wrote the eight-book treatise *Conics*, in which the ellipse, hyperbola and parabola are defined as sections of a circular cone, whether right or oblique. We use Apollonius’s names for these curves. The names express one of their properties relating to areas: parabola means “application”, ellipse means “application with a deficiency”, and hyperbola means “application with an excess”.

His work *On Tangencies* discusses the celebrated problem of Apollonius: “Three objects are given, each of which may be a point, a line or a circle. Construct a circle that passes through the given points and is tangent to the given lines or circles.” Familiar school constructions of a triangle’s circumcircle and incircle are special cases.

In Apollonius’s treatment, geometric constructions are carried out using compass and straightedge. He also describes the defining relations for curves, sometimes called their “symptoms”, in the language of plane geometry. These can be seen as early equivalents of equations for curves.

The theory of conic sections exemplifies a “pure” mathematical theory created in advance of any practical need. Broad practical applications of these sections emerged much later. Important early modern examples include Kepler’s study of planetary motion in elliptical orbits and Galileo’s work on the parabolic trajectories of projectiles.
