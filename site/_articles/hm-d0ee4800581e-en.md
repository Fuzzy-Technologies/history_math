*Marking the 260th anniversary of his birth*

Semyon Yemelyanovich Guryev (10 [21] September 1766–11 [23] December 1813) was a Russian mathematician and researcher in mechanics, a full member of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences from 1798 and a member of the Russian Academy from 1800. He belonged to a generation at the turn of the nineteenth century for whom mathematics was a practical instrument of engineering, navigation and education as well as a theory.

## Engineer, Teacher and Academician

He studied at the Artillery School and Engineering Corps in 1778–1784, then served as an officer. In 1792 he was sent to England to study hydraulics, a characteristic episode in a period when Russian science and technology were actively learning from European experience. On his return, Guryev taught mathematics, artillery and navigation at several institutions in St Petersburg.

From 1798 his life was closely connected with the School of Naval Architecture, where he was professor of mathematics and later inspector. In his final years he also taught at the Institute of the Corps of Transport Engineers. Alongside this, he conducted research and prepared texts for higher education at the Academy of Sciences.

## Geometry and Textbooks

Guryev’s mathematical interests embraced geometry, analysis and mechanics. Encyclopedic accounts emphasize his early analytic derivation of the principal equations of plane curves in polar coordinates: describing a curve through the dependence of its radius on its angle. This apparatus mattered both to the theory of curves and to applied problems in mechanics.

His *Essay on Improving the Elements of Geometry* (1798) holds a special place in his legacy. More than a textbook, it is a philosophical guide to teaching: Guryev sets out the aims of school education and proposes a path from practical experience to theoretical knowledge. He was critical of classroom retellings of Euclid, considered different ways of arranging the material, and insisted that proofs be clear and concepts usable in action rather than merely memorized.

In the early nineteenth century he produced substantial courses and translations, chiefly for naval and engineering schools. These included *Naval Course, Part One, Containing the Foundations of Geometry* (1804), *The Science of Calculation* (1805), *Foundations of the Transcendental Geometry of Curved Surfaces* (1806), *Foundations of Geometry*, second edition (1811), and *Foundations of Differential Calculus* (1811). Guryev translated and adapted European textbooks for Russian readers, including Étienne Bézout’s works on navigation and Cousin’s course in differential and integral calculus.

## Mechanics and the Language of Science

His interests also included difficult questions of the period: he attempted to prove Euclid’s fifth postulate and studied the equilibrium of vaults. His works on dynamics appeared in the Academy’s publications. *Foundations of Mechanics* was published posthumously in 1815, an indication of how much manuscript material he had prepared during his lifetime.

Guryev also cared about organizing scientific exchange. He is credited with proposing one of the Academy’s first scientific collections in Russian, *Theoretical Investigations of the Imperial St Petersburg Academy of Sciences*, published in five volumes in 1808–1818. At a time when science was often written in Latin, French or German, this helped develop Russian scientific language and the habit of discussing mathematics in one’s native tongue.

Guryev is remembered for a rare combination of researcher, engineer and teacher. He could turn difficult material into a teaching text while demanding meaning and rigour from education. People like him did much to lay the foundations of the nineteenth-century Russian mathematical school.
