(For the 80th anniversary of the Great Victory)

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-1" %}

In the memoirs of the astrophysicist and academician V. V. Sobolev, who worked at the Yelabuga branch of Leningrad State University during the war, we read: *“Yelabuga taught people how to work.”* Most of the scientists who reached Yelabuga were, of course, able people who had undergone rigorous selection before evacuation. Yet many Leningraders gratefully recalled the “Yelabuga school”, which taught them to work energetically and selflessly. Those who had lived in Yelabuga continued to work just as successfully in later years.

The life and scientific career of Fyodor Dmitriyevich Klement (1903–1973) make a particularly strong impression, even on Leningrad scientists. He arrived in Yelabuga as head of the luminescence laboratory and a senior research fellow. Soon afterwards he was elected secretary of the branch's Party organization. On 10 May 1943, before the Academic Council of the Yelabuga branch, he defended his Candidate dissertation, “A Study of the Luminescence of Alkali-Halide Phosphors”. In the summer of 1943, the branch's director, Professor V. A. Ambartsumyan, left for Yerevan, and Klement was appointed in his place. He therefore had to carry out the very difficult and responsible task of returning the evacuated branch in the summer of 1944.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

> In 1944–1951 he worked at Leningrad State University as an associate professor in the Department of Optics.  
> In 1951–1970 he was rector of the University of Tartu.  
> From 1951 he was a professor and an academician of the Academy of Sciences of the Estonian SSR.  
> In 1954–1972 he was a member of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of Estonia.  
> In 1954–1966 he was a deputy of the Supreme Soviet of the USSR.  
> He received the Orders of Lenin, the Red Banner of Labour, the Badge of Honour and the October Revolution.  
> Hero of Socialist Labour (1969).  
> Chairman of the Estonian SSR Committee for Science and Technology (1967–1973).

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

This rise in Klement's scientific and administrative career can be explained by his strong personal qualities. He describes his life in the memoir Altars on the Paths of Science (Templid teaduse teedel. — Tallinn: Eesti raamat, 1983, in Estonian). His son, Roald Fyodorovich Klement, kindly provided us with a translation of the chapter devoted to the war years, for which we are very grateful. He also sent his book The History of Our Family (St Petersburg, 2003), much of it devoted to his father. Roald's childhood recollections of life in Yelabuga are very interesting. They lived at 44 Lenin Street, now Kazanskaya Street. He was 10–12 and attended grades 3–5 at the Kuibyshev School. He was in the same class as our celebrated fellow townsman, the writer S. T. Romanovsky, and shared his desk. Roald remembers many employees of the university branch and the Leningraders who lived in Yelabuga.

Fyodor Dmitriyevich Klement — Theodor, son of Didrich Klement — was born on 12 June 1903 in St Petersburg. He attended gymnasiums in St Petersburg and Narva. He had to start work in the difficult years for Russia and Estonia, 1918–1919. In 1919–1922 he taught at the village school in Shchepets, Gdov District. He became the first Komsomol member in Shchepets and joined the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) in 1923. In 1922–1926 he held management posts in public and political education. Thus he became a member of the administrative nomenklatura while still young. Such public, Party and administrative work greatly hindered his studies and scientific work throughout his life. *“He was talented and variously gifted in every sphere of life, and the Party nomenklatura ruthlessly exploited his talents for its own purposes. As a result, his administrative advancement far outpaced my father's scientific career,”* writes R. F. Klement.

In 1934 Klement graduated from Leningrad State University's Faculty of Physics. While still a student, he was elected deputy secretary of the university's Party organization in 1930. In 1933–1935 he was a postgraduate student there. In a reference, A. N. Terenin, corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences, wrote: *“...Comrade Klement really has every prerequisite to play a leading role in Soviet physics in the future...” In 1935–1951 he simultaneously held several scientific and administrative posts: scientific secretary of the Institute of Physics, editor of the university's Scholarly Notes and deputy editor-in-chief of the Bulletin of Leningrad University.* In 1937 he established an independent luminescence laboratory at the university's Institute of Physics.

He came to Yelabuga with this laboratory. There they worked on practical scientific applications, specifically the development of luminescent screens, together with physicists from USSR Academy of Sciences institutes based in Kazan, including academicians A. F. Ioffe and L. A. Artsimovich. He therefore had to visit Kazan repeatedly.

Klement recalled: *“The years behind the lines in Yelabuga were difficult for all of us in their own way, particularly the first, when we had only begun adapting to a new way of life. Food disappeared from unrestricted sale in 1941. The local population lived mainly from their vegetable gardens. We were evacuated from Leningrad at the end of the summer and received garden plots only in the autumn or for the following summer. Ration cards provided only bread. Market prices rose unbelievably. Our pay was enough only to buy potatoes. We boiled potatoes only in their skins... At the beginning of 1942, a food ration was introduced for scientific workers, which noticeably eased our lives... In Yelabuga we nevertheless managed to organize and adapt our domestic arrangements so that we lived better than in Saratov and Kazan...”*

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

R. F. Klement adds: *“In Yelabuga we lived in a wooden cottage with a large Russian stove, in which we cooked our meals. There was no electricity in the first year, and we lit the room with a kerosene lamp. Water had to be carried from a well three blocks away. Father usually did this and was very skilful at carrying two buckets of water on a shoulder yoke.*

*In winter, once the sledging route was usable, Father borrowed a horse from the institute and the whole family went to the forest for firewood. We felled extremely tall pines, cut them into logs, loaded them onto the sledge and took them home. Father shunned no peasant work: firewood in winter, and hay to be mown for the institute's horses in summer. The institute was allotted a large hayfield in the Kama meadows, and all the professors worked there with scythes and rakes. Father was an excellent mower; no one could keep up with him. His sweep was broad and he was tireless at work.”*

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

Klement's laboratory continued to function successfully after the war. It was later moved to Tartu. Through his efforts, the world-famous Tartu school of luminescence physicists was established. As rector of the university for 20 years, he earned the love and respect of everyone there, professors and students alike.

Klement died in 1973 and was buried in Tartu.
