{% include article-figure.html id="fig-1" %}

I am a mathematician, and I want to tell you the remarkable story of numbers. It is hard to imagine life without them. We need numbers at every turn.

A Russian saying goes: *“I know it like my own five fingers!”* Why do you think we say this? What does it mean?

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Could the expression come from the distant time when knowing that there were five fingers meant being able to count?

Prometheus, chained by the gods to a Caucasian rock for revealing to humans what should have remained the gods' knowledge, says:

*“Hear what I did for mortals…*

*I invented number for them*

*And taught them to combine letters…”*

(So wrote the Greek tragedian Aeschylus.)

The Greeks thought Prometheus had given them numbers. They also said he stole fire from heaven and gave it to humans. Of course, they were mistaken in attributing this achievement to heroes and gods.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

*“All is number,”* said the most famous mathematician of all times and nations.

Yes, the great Pythagoras once said *“All is number”*, measuring even harmony through numbers.

For you, the world of numbers is now as simple as “twice two is four”. Yet humanity had to learn for many thousands of years before reaching this understanding. The history of numbers is the oldest chapter in the history of science.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

People began learning to count in the most ancient times, taught by life itself. Even in the distant past, when they lived in caves and wore skins, they could not do without counting. Their first concerns were obtaining food and sheltering from bad weather. By observing the surrounding world, however, they gradually changed their living conditions. They learned to build dwellings and harvest stores, and to make pottery. In doing so, they learned to distinguish and compare quantities of objects.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

First, people learned to distinguish one from many: a single leader in a herd of deer, or the sun and moon in the sky. To name this quantity, they also used words denoting a single object, such as “moon” or “sun”.

You often hear the word “soloist”. What does it mean? “A performer who performs alone,” you will say. It comes from Latin *solus*, “one”. The Russian word *solntse*, “sun”, also resembles “soloist”. The explanation is very simple: when the Romans, who spoke Latin in antiquity, devised a name for the number 1, they began from the fact that there is always just one sun in the sky.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

People noticed that different groups of objects—animals, hunters, hands—could have the same number: two deer, two fingers. Repeated observation of paired objects—eyes, ears, hands, wings—led to the concept of 2. In some languages, the word for 2 still sounds like “eyes”, among Native Americans, or “wings”, among Tibetans.

Eventually people named the number 3, originally understood as “many”. Consider Russian sayings: *“To repeat the same thing three times”; “You wait three years for what is promised”; “In the thirtieth kingdom, in the thrice-ninth realm.”* The number 3 also plays a special role in Russian fairy tales, which usually feature three brothers.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

*An old man had three sons:*

*The eldest was a clever lad,*

*The middle neither good nor bad,*

*The youngest was a fool outright.*

(P. P. Yershov, *The Little Humpbacked Horse*)

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-8" %}

In Russian we say *odna loshad*, “one horse”; *dve loshadi*, “two horses”; *tri loshadi*, “three horses”; *chetyre loshadi*, “four horses”; but then *pyat loshadey*, “five horses”, and keep that form thereafter. Once, beyond 4 began “many”.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-9" %}

By studying the languages of different peoples and communities, scholars learn much about how numbers acquired their names. Try counting from 1 to 10 in Russian, Tatar and English. There are differences, but also things in common.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-10" %}

In the nineteenth century, Nikolai Nikolayevich Miklouho-Maclay, a Russian ethnographer, anthropologist, biologist and traveller who studied the indigenous populations of Southeast Asia, Australia and Oceania, described counting among the Papuans of New Guinea. As they bent their fingers, they made a particular sound, such as “be”: be, be, be, be, ibon-be; then on the other hand, be, be, be, be, ibon-ali; on one foot, samba-be; and on the other, samba-ali.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-11" %}

You have surely read how Robinson Crusoe marked the days and months spent on his uninhabited island with notches. He did not invent this way of recording numbers, of course. When the number of objects exceeded the fingers and toes, people used notched sticks, pebbles, bundles and knots in cords. Notches on a stick were the first method of recording numbers. Widespread among many peoples, it was nevertheless very inconvenient.

The Russian expression *“Zarubi sebe na nosu”*, literally “Notch it on your nose”, still survives. It says that to remember something important, one should make a notch. Here, *nos* derives from *nosit*, “to carry”. In earlier times, many people carried small sticks for making notches. They called them *nos*, and recorded a number by making the corresponding number of notches or marks on it.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-12" %}

Peoples in distant countries and different periods devised their own ways of recording numbers, yet these still had similarities. In Egypt, for example, the first numbers were represented by vertical strokes and later by hieroglyphs; in Babylon, by wedges.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-13" %}

The first human civilization on our planet arose in hot Africa. The Nile flows through this land into the Mediterranean. It is warm all year, and fertile valleys lie along its banks. This is why people settled the valley in ancient times.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-14" %}

They formed the state of Egypt around four millennia before the Common Era. The Egyptians built the famous pyramids, tombs of the pharaohs. Papyrus scrolls survived in these pyramids, bearing texts written by Egyptian scribes in hieroglyphs. About five thousand years ago, people realized that numbers could be recorded not only as notches representing units, but also by orders: units, tens and hundreds.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-15" %}

Another cradle of humanity was Mesopotamia. In Western Asia, the Tigris and Euphrates flow from north to south, giving the valley its name. This extraordinarily fertile land became a favourable place for settlement as early as the fourth millennium BCE. You may have heard of the Sumerians, who lived in the rivers' delta, and the Akkadians to the north. These peoples formed the powerful state of Babylon.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-16" %}

Before other peoples, the Sumerians devised a way to write words on clay: cuneiform. They pressed wedge-shaped symbols representing letters and numbers into clay tablets with a special stylus, then dried the tablets in the sun.

The Babylonians wrote numbers using just two wedges: a vertical one for a unit and a horizontal one for ten. The same vertical wedge represented both 60 and one. This means their numeral system was sexagesimal, or base 60.

Interestingly, we still sometimes use the Babylonian counting system. Why do you think an hour has 60 minutes and a minute 60 seconds? One suggestion is that we inherited this from Babylon. The division of a circle into 360 degrees is also known to have come from them.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-17" %}

The Chinese used a decimal system written in characters.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-18" %}

Greek numeral signs, called Herodianic and dating from the seventh–fifth centuries BCE, are initial letters of number names: Ι, one, in Greek *ios*; П, five, *pente*; Δ, ten, *deka*; Н, one hundred, *hekaton*; Х, one thousand, *khilioi* or *khilias*; and М, ten thousand, *myrioi* or *myriad*. These are the origins of words such as “pentagon”, “dean”, “hectare” and “kilogram”.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-19" %}

Scholars suggest that the Roman sign for five depicts a hand with five spread fingers, and the sign for ten two hands put together. Ten fingers have given humanity invaluable help, teaching people to count and perform simple numerical operations.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-20" %}

The modern numerals 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 0 were invented much later. They descend from numerals invented in India. India introduced both convenient digits and the method of writing them now accepted worldwide: the decimal positional system. It is the most convenient and economical for notation and calculation.

The Arabs borrowed the Indian notation. It reached Europe through Arab and Italian merchants.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-21" %}

In Russia, Indo-Arabic numerals first appeared only in the seventeenth century. Before that, Slavic alphabetic numeration was used, with numbers represented by letters bearing a small stroke, called a *titlo*, above them.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-22" %}

The invention of zero also has an interesting history. Many numeral systems managed without it, but it is a very important sign in the decimal positional system devised by the Indians. When a place value was absent, they put a dot. Thus 5.1 meant 501 and was read “five, sunya, one”. *Sunya* meant “empty”. Later, in the fifth century, a small circle replaced the dot, eventually becoming today's zero, 0. Arab scholars translated *sunya* as *sifr*, and Europeans later used *ciffra*. In Russian, people initially even said *shifr*, and only later *tsifra*. Thus the word now meaning “digit” originally meant only zero. The invention of zero is considered an important mathematical discovery.

The Russian word *nul*, “zero”, in its modern sense arose later, in the sixteenth century, from a Latin word meaning “none”. Now we call all ten signs used to write numbers “digits”.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-23" %}

Work and reason made humanity great and strong. People discover nature's laws and transform the world. To do this, they must know much and be able to count—accurately and quickly.
