Pi Day is an informal holiday celebrated by mathematics enthusiasts on 14 March at 1:59:26 in honour of the mathematical constant.

Larry Shaw, a staff member at the Exploratorium in San Francisco, first organized the celebration in 1988. He noticed that the US month/day date 3/14, followed by the time 1:59:26, matches the opening digits of $\pi=3.141592653589\ldots$.

The celebration can also be held at 13:59:26, or 1:59:26 p.m. on a twelve-hour clock. In twenty-four-hour notation, the matching time is 01:59:26.

People give speeches praising π and its role in human life, draw pictures of a world without π, bake and eat “pi pie” decorated with the Greek letter or the number’s opening digits, drink beverages and play games whose names begin with “pi”, solve mathematical puzzles and riddles, and even dance in circles around objects associated with the number. The Russian word *pirog*, meaning pie, supplies another pun.

Pi Approximation Day is also celebrated on 22 July, since $\pi\approx22/7$. Written as the fraction 22/7, this date approximates π more closely than 3.14 does. Archimedes obtained $22/7$ as an upper bound for π. The day/month date format is also much more widespread than the month/day format behind 14 March.

Historical note. The constant π is the ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter. Its constancy was known in antiquity. Pi is irrational: it cannot be expressed exactly as a ratio of natural numbers, and its decimal expansion is infinite and nonrepeating. The German mathematician Lambert proved its irrationality in 1761. Pi is also transcendental: it cannot be a root of any nonzero polynomial with integer coefficients. Ferdinand Lindemann proved this in 1882 while at the University of Freiburg, ending more than 2,500 years of attempts to square the circle.

The British mathematician William Jones is generally credited with first using the Greek letter π for the number in his *Synopsis palmariorum matheseos* (Synopsis of Mathematical Achievements, 1706). Leonhard Euler popularised the notation in 1737. The letter is thought to come from the initial of the Greek περιφέρεια, meaning circumference or periphery.

Calculating many decimal digits of π also has a long history and both theoretical and practical significance. By June 2022, its first 100 trillion decimal places were known. Such computations became possible only in the computer age.

People also compete in memorising digits of π. Many mnemonic devices exist, including poems whose word lengths give successive digits. This one is in Russian:

Это я знаю<br>
И помню прекрасно,<br>
Но многие знаки<br>
Мне лишни, напрасны...

A literal English gloss is: “I know and remember this perfectly, but many digits are unnecessary and of no use to me.” Count the letters in the Russian words, rather than in the translation: 3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9, 2, 6, 5, 3, 5, 8.
