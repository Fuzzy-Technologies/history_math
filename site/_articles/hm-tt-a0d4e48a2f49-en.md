A goniometer, from Greek γωνία, “angle”, and μετρέω, “I measure”, is an angle-measuring instrument.

Goniometry as a science with its own methods and instruments has been known in the literature since 1914.

By operating principle, goniometers are either contact instruments, applied directly to an object to measure it, or reflecting instruments, which measure using light reflected from its faces. Reflecting goniometers are more convenient and accurate.

The simplest contact goniometer combines a protractor and ruler with one or more moving parts.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The first goniometers were used in crystallography to measure angles between crystal faces. The French mechanic Arnould Carangeot is credited with creating the first contact goniometer in the XVIII century. The English chemist William Hyde Wollaston created the first single-circle reflecting goniometer in the early XIX century (1809).

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

In 1889, the brilliant Russian crystallographer E. S. Fedorov (1853–1919) built a two-circle reflecting goniometer based on the theodolite principle. German scientists S. Czapski and V. Goldschmidt developed other versions. These instruments measure through rotations about two perpendicular axes.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

In medicine, goniometers measure joint mobility to identify possible movement problems and select methods of rehabilitation. The simplest instrument is a contact goniometer, applied directly to the body: essentially a protractor with a movable arm or ruler.

Industrial goniometers measure angles between any surfaces capable of reflecting light.
