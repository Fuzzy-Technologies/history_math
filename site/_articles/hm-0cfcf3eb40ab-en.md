In the classical period, Athens became one of the principal centres of ancient Greek thought. Philosophers of different generations taught and debated there, including Anaxagoras, Hippias, Plato and Aristotle; Democritus, Theodorus and Hippocrates were also closely connected with its intellectual life. In the fourth century BCE, Plato founded the Academy and Aristotle established his school at the Lyceum, both important models for later scholarly and educational communities.

Aristotle was one of ancient Greece’s great philosophers and polymaths, a striking example of the universal scholar. His writings cover many subjects, including physics, biology, zoology, metaphysics, logic, ethics, aesthetics, poetry, music, rhetoric, psychology, linguistics, economics, politics, meteorology, geology and government. He founded the Peripatetic school of philosophy at the Lyceum.

At seventeen, Aristotle came to Athens and became Plato’s student. Early in his time at the Academy, he wrote dialogues. He remained there for twenty years, until Plato’s death, and then left. Some time later he was invited to tutor Alexander, the son of the king of Macedonia, who was then thirteen.

Several years later, after Alexander became king, Aristotle returned to Athens and opened his own school at the Lyceum, outside the city. The modern word *lyceum* derives from its name. Alexander did not forget his teacher. The Lyceum became a centre of wide-ranging research.

Aristotle divided all the sciences known to him into theoretical, practical and productive. Among the theoretical sciences he placed physics, mathematics and metaphysics.

Although he studied many subjects, his works on logic are of particular interest to us: the *Organon*, including *Categories* and *On Interpretation*. He is widely recognized as the founder of logic, the element needed for the deductive construction of mathematics.

Aristotle discovered that if a statement $A$ follows from $B$, the truth of $B$ must be established. The truth of $B$ may follow from $C$, that of $C$ from $D$, and so on. At some point, the chain $A\leftarrow B\leftarrow C\leftarrow D\leftarrow\cdots$ must stop, and its final statement must be accepted as an axiom.

Four kinds of elements can be distinguished in Aristotle’s account of demonstrative knowledge:

- basic concepts,
- axioms,
- definitions,
- theorems.

Modern axiomatic theories usually require consistency, while independence is treated as a desirable additional property; new statements are obtained by rules of inference. The deductive approach developed in ancient mathematics became one of its principal methods.

This is how the idea of mathematics as an axiomatic and deductive discipline took shape. Three classical logical principles are traditionally associated with the Aristotelian tradition:

- identity: if $A$, then $A$;
- non-contradiction: $A$ and not $A$ cannot both be true;
- excluded middle: either $A$ or not $A$ is true.

Aristotle’s achievements in logic also include his rules of inference, or syllogisms. A syllogism derives a new conclusion from two premises. Departing from its conditions sometimes produces a false conclusion, a device often used in sophisms.

Aristotle’s views profoundly influenced medieval scholarship through the Renaissance and were not systematically superseded until the Enlightenment.
