A “travelling puzzle” has survived from ancient Egypt to the present day: *“7 people each have 7 cats; each cat eats 7 mice; each mouse eats 7 ears of grain; from each ear, 7 measures of barley can grow. What are these numbers, and what is their sum?”* This is a problem from the *Papyrus of Ahmes*.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

We invite you to think about the following related questions and problems, which bring together history and mathematics teaching:

1\. What are these numbers, and what is their sum? Describe the mathematical objects used in this problem.

2\. In what other historical problems do these objects appear? Recall, for example, the legend of the chessboard in its Indian and Roman versions, or problems about merchants.

3\. Why do you think the problem speaks of exactly seven items of each kind? Name other historical examples of the use of 7: the seven wonders of the world, the seven days of Creation.

## Answers and historical background {#section-1}

1\. 7 people, 49 cats, 343 mice, 2401 ears of grain, 16807 measures of barley: 19607 in total. A geometric progression and the sum of its terms.

2\. Perelman, Ya. I. “The Legend of the Chessboard.” In *Living Mathematics*, 8th ed. Moscow: Nauka, 1967, pp. 87–91.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Here is one of the merchant problems: *“Two merchants agreed that, for one month, the first would give the second 10,000 dollars a day. In return, the second would pay the first one cent on the first day, two cents on the second, and so on, doubling the previous day's payment. The second merchant agreed and enjoyed his income for the first three weeks, but by the end of the month he was completely ruined, having given his entire fortune to the first.”* Try calculating how much each merchant spent. On what day did their expenditures become equal?

3\. Many ancient peoples regarded 7 as a special number. The Egyptians and Babylonians saw it as the sum of two “essential to life” numbers: three and four. Three people form the basis of life — a mother, a father and a child — while four is the number of cardinal points and wind directions from which rain arrives, its life-giving moisture making the land fruitful.

P. S. The ancient Egyptians loved cats and revered them as gods. This was connected with the development of agriculture in ancient Egypt, when harvests needed protection from rodents. Domesticated cats hunted rats as well as snakes.
