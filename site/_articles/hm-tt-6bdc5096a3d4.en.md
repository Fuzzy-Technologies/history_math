> “The seven liberal arts are the foundation of an education to be given not for practical benefit, but because it is worthy of a freeborn person and beautiful in itself.”  
> Aristotle, *Politics*

Traditionally, medieval education was confined to church schools. In time, society came to need educated administrators, lawyers, notaries, physicians and priests.

At the turn of the XII–XIII centuries, Western Europe’s education system changed radically: new institutions—universities, from Latin universitas, “community”—arose from older schools. The emergence of medieval universities was connected with the growth of towns.

Europe’s oldest universities have no precise foundation dates. The transformation of monastic and cathedral schools into universities was a lengthy process.

They first opened in Italian towns: Salerno (XI century), Bologna (1088), followed by Paris (1150, officially in 1200), Oxford (1096), Cambridge (1209), Florence (1321) and Prague (1348).

Long before them, the Magnaura school in Constantinople (855), sometimes called the University of Constantinople, brought together prominent scholars specifically to create a “centre of Greek science and culture”. Some historians also regard Arab madrasas in Muslim Spain and Sicily as precursors of universities.

The University of Bologna is considered the “mother of European universities”. It specialized in law.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Each medieval university was unique in its own way. From the Middle Ages, Paris enjoyed a strong reputation for academic achievement in the humanities, especially theology and philosophy. In 1257, a Catholic college called the Sorbonne was founded in the Latin Quarter of Paris. In the XVII century, the Sorbonne effectively merged with the university, and their names became identified with one another.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

The University of Salerno is one of Europe’s oldest. It developed from the world-famous medieval medical school, the Schola Medica Salernitana, the first and most important institution of its kind. Situated in the southern Italian city of Salerno, it was founded in the IX century and became renowned in the X century as the most important source of medical knowledge in Western Europe. Salerno earned the title “City of Hippocrates”. People came from around the world: patients hoping to recover and students wishing to learn the art of medicine.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

Most European universities followed the models of Bologna and Paris.

Early Western European universities enjoyed the Catholic Church’s patronage and usually had the status of cathedral schools, as at Paris, or Studium Generale, general schools. Later, kings also founded universities, including Prague and Kraków.

In the Middle Ages, universitas was not used as the name of an educational institution. These were called Studium, a word denoting an institution with a university curriculum. Universitas meant a corporation of teachers and students: a strictly regulated association for receiving and transmitting education.

The medieval designation Studium generale applied to a widely known school that attracted pupils from afar and could teach the seven liberal arts as well as at least one specialist subject: civil or canon law, theology or medicine. Graduating from a university conferred the right to teach the sciences in educational institutions.

University education had two stages. The first, lasting 3–4 years, consisted of mastering the seven liberal arts. A studiosus, or student, first learned to write and speak by mastering the trivium, from Latin trivium, “a trio” or “three”: grammar, rhetoric and logic. Having completed it, the student could proceed to the quadrivium, from Latin quadrium, “a set of four”: arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy.

After mastering the seven liberal arts—or, in some cases, the trivium alone—a student could enter the second stage. This took place in a higher faculty, generally specializing in one of three disciplines: theology, medicine or law.

There was no unified curriculum comparable to that of modern universities. A student could study any subject for any length of time. Education often stretched over many years: students moved between universities seeking rare books or better professors, interrupted their studies to work, and saved for another course. The common teaching language, Latin, meant that borders effectively did not exist for them.

Structurally, the University of Paris, for example, was divided into faculties, from Latin facultas, “possibility” or “ability”: theology, law, medicine and arts. In its character and teaching, the arts faculty essentially provided general education. Its formal curriculum comprised the seven liberal arts.

Philosophical disciplines dominated there. By the XIII–XIV centuries, this faculty therefore fully deserved the name or status of a philosophy faculty. To study in theology, law or medicine, one first had to complete the general faculty’s course and earn a master of arts degree.

Faculties organized lectures and disputations—public examinations—and awarded degrees conferring the right to teach. Lectures and disputations were the main teaching methods. In lectures, teachers read prescribed authors and explained the text through appropriate commentary.

At the arts faculty, Aristotle’s works formed most of the material studied. The theology faculty read Scripture and presented Christian doctrine systematically, offering rational arguments for Catholic dogmas.

In the medical faculty, authorities such as Galen, Hippocrates and Celsus played a major role.

The first universities of medieval Europe also contributed significantly to mathematics. Initially, however, mathematics played a secondary role. Training mathematicians was not the universities’ purpose.

One of the most popular medieval authors on the quadrivium was Severinus Boethius (480–524), a statesman and Neoplatonic philosopher. He wrote *Foundations of Arithmetic*, a superficial translation of Nicomachus and Euclid using numerical and geometric examples without proofs.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

Medieval university scholars noted for their contributions to mathematics include Nicole Oresme (1323–1382), a French mathematician and mechanician, bishop of Lisieux, who taught at the Sorbonne; Johann Müller (Regiomontanus, 1436–1476), a leading mathematician of the XV century who worked at the University of Vienna; the Italian Luca Pacioli (1445–1517), who worked at Bologna; and Jan Widman (1460–1505), who taught in Leipzig and was the first to lecture on algebra at a university.

The university idea was first realized in Russia in 1725 with the establishment of the Academic University in Saint Petersburg (1725–1767). The first example of Russia’s classical university form, however, was the Imperial Moscow University, founded in 1755. Twelve imperial universities existed in the history of the Russian Empire. Among the earliest were Dorpat (1802), Vilna (1803), Kazan (1804), Kharkov (1805) and Saint Petersburg (1819). But that is the next chapter of the story.
