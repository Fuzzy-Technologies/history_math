*Marking the 125th anniversary of her birth*

Nina Karlovna Bari (6 [19] November 1901–15 July 1961) was a Soviet mathematician and a leading figure in the Moscow school of the theory of functions of a real variable. Her name is associated above all with trigonometric series, from subtle questions of convergence to the uniqueness problem: when can the same function be represented by different trigonometric expansions?

## Moscow and International Research

She was born and grew up in Moscow. After attending a private girls’ gymnasium, she entered Moscow University following the reforms of 1917, among the first women able to do so. There she joined an exceptional mathematical community that included Nikolai Luzin, Dmitri Egorov, Sergei Chaplygin and other outstanding mathematicians. In the circle the students called “Luzitania”, her style took shape: rigorous and disciplined, yet lively in its choice of problems.

Bari’s early work attracted international attention. In the early 1920s she published and presented results on the uniqueness of trigonometric expansions. Her 1923 address to the Moscow Mathematical Society is often recalled as a landmark: women then had very little public voice in Moscow’s scientific life.

In the late 1920s Bari worked at the Sorbonne and the Collège de France in Paris and gave talks at Jacques Hadamard’s seminar. She spoke at the 1928 International Congress of Mathematicians in Bologna. Back in Moscow, Bari worked at the university. In the 1930s she became a professor and soon received the degree of Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences. Decades of teaching and research followed at Moscow State University’s Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics, where she helped uphold the demanding standards of the Moscow school of function theory.

## Research and Books

Her research extended beyond any single topic. Alongside trigonometric series, Bari studied superpositions of functions, orthogonal systems and bases. The term “Bari basis” entered mathematical usage for minimal systems quadratically close to a complete orthonormal system. This concerns structure rather than calculation, and concepts of this kind move a theory forward.

Perhaps her most recognizable legacy, however, is her writing. She made rigorous theory readable. Her textbooks *Higher Algebra* and *Theory of Series* were written for teacher-training institutes, while her monograph *Trigonometric Series*, published in English as *A Treatise on Trigonometric Series*, became a fundamental reference for specialists: a book to revisit for a map of the subject, rather than just an elegant formula.

Nina Bari’s life ended tragically in the summer of 1961. Yet her story has a rare coherence: a researcher’s talent, a taste for difficult problems and a powerful gift for teaching. People like her shape mathematical culture as well as mathematical results.
