The history of mathematics can be divided into periods distinguished by characteristic features. Such a division usually rests on an assessment of the content, level of achievement and nature of mathematical research: its principal methods, results and ideas.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The periodization most often used in practice is A. N. Kolmogorov's, set out in his article “Mathematics” (1954). He distinguished four periods in mathematical development.

## I. The origins of mathematics {#section-1}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

This period begins with the emergence of humanity and lasts until the sixth–fifth centuries BCE. Mathematical facts accumulate within a general, undivided body of knowledge. Initial ideas of natural numbers, fractions, geometrical figures and solids take shape. Methods develop for solving the simplest practical problems.

It includes the mathematics of ancient Egypt, Babylon, India and China, and ends in ancient Greece.

People mastered two mathematical operations: counting and measurement. They first measured time and distance, then area and volume, in response to the needs of agriculture and crafts.

## II. The period of elementary mathematics {#section-2}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

This period runs from the sixth–fifth centuries BCE to the end of the sixteenth century CE. It is characterized by achievements in studying constant quantities, and is therefore sometimes called the “period of the mathematics of constant quantities.” This mathematics is largely taught in secondary school. Mathematics becomes a rigorous deductive science. The period includes ancient Greece, the Hellenistic countries, medieval China and India, the Islamic countries, medieval Europe and the Renaissance.

In ancient Greece, geometry is first organized systematically on a deductive basis. The decimal place-value number system first appears in India. Elementary algebra develops as a science of equations in Arab countries and later Western Europe. The beginnings of trigonometry emerge in astronomy through the efforts of Greek, Indian and Arab scholars.

> Leading Greek mathematicians: Pythagoras, Euclid, Archimedes and Apollonius.

> Well-known mathematicians of the second period: Heron, Claudius Ptolemy, Diophantus, Aryabhata, Brahmagupta, Bhaskara II, Muhammad al-Khwarizmi, Omar Khayyam, Leonardo Fibonacci, Johann Müller (Regiomontanus), Luca Pacioli, François Viète and John Napier.

## III. The creation of the mathematics of variable quantities {#section-3}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

This period runs from the early seventeenth century to the mid-nineteenth century. It is distinguished by the introduction and study of functions. Bringing variable quantities into geometry produces analytic geometry. Differential and integral calculus are created to study functional relationships. Almost all the disciplines forming the classical foundation of modern mathematics take shape in this period, which is therefore also called the “period of higher mathematics.” It is conventionally subdivided into seventeenth- and eighteenth-century mathematics.

The foundations of analytic geometry and mathematical analysis are laid. The foundations of number theory also develop, and probability theory, descriptive geometry and projective geometry appear.

> Leading mathematicians of the third period:

> Johannes Kepler, Galileo Galilei, René Descartes, Pierre de Fermat, Christiaan Huygens, Blaise Pascal, Isaac Newton, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, Jacob Bernoulli, Johann Bernoulli, Leonhard Euler, Jean le Rond d'Alembert, Joseph-Louis Lagrange and Pierre-Simon Laplace.

## IV. The period of modern mathematics {#section-4}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

This period begins in the mid-nineteenth century and continues to the present. It arose from a critical re-examination of the foundations of mathematics. Many new mathematical theories appear, and applications expand. Group-theoretic methods develop in algebra, along with non-Euclidean geometries. Mathematical analysis is reconstructed on rigorous definitions of real numbers and limits.

New fields emerge: in the nineteenth century, non-Euclidean geometries, group theory, set theory, mathematical logic and complex analysis; in the twentieth, real analysis, foundations of mathematics, topology, functional analysis, algebraic geometry, mathematical statistics, mathematical physics, discrete mathematics and history of mathematics.

> Leading mathematicians of the fourth period:

> In the nineteenth century: Carl Friedrich Gauss, Adrien-Marie Legendre, Gaspard Monge, Siméon Denis Poisson, Jean-Joseph Fourier, Augustin-Louis Cauchy, Karl Weierstrass, Niels Henrik Abel, Évariste Galois, Mikhail Vasilyevich Ostrogradsky, Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky, Pafnuty Lvovich Chebyshev, Sofya Vasilyevna Kovalevskaya, Bernhard Riemann, Georg Cantor, Felix Klein, Arthur Cayley, Henri Poincaré and David Hilbert.

> In the twentieth century: Henri Lebesgue, Hermann Weyl, Jacques Hadamard, Felix Hausdorff, Kurt Gödel, John von Neumann, Norbert Wiener, Stefan Banach, Erik Ivar Fredholm, Nikolai Nikolayevich Luzin, Vladimir Andreyevich Steklov, Emmy Noether, Hermann Minkowski, Dmitry Alexandrovich Grave, Dmitry Fyodorovich Egorov, Andrey Nikolayevich Kolmogorov, Otto Yulyevich Schmidt, Mikhail Alekseyevich Lavrentyev, Pavel Sergeyevich Alexandrov and Nikolai Grigoryevich Chebotaryov.

In the second half of the twentieth century, as computing developed rapidly and information technology entered every area of practical and theoretical activity, mathematicians too began using computers. Their use certainly leaves its mark on mathematics, but there is as yet no reason to regard the twentieth century as the beginning of a new period: the period of computer mathematics.
