“No one shall be able to expel us from the paradise that Cantor has created.”

— David Hilbert, *On the Infinite* (1926)

He was born in St Petersburg on 3 March 1845.

Georg Cantor was a German mathematician and the founder of set theory. He graduated from the University of Berlin, where he studied with Weierstrass, and taught at the University of Halle.

He was one of the founders and the first president of the German Mathematical Society (1890–1893). He also advocated an international mathematical congress; the first such congress met in Zurich in 1897.

Cantor was the first to define how arbitrary sets, including infinite sets, can be compared by their cardinality, a generalization of the idea of quantity. His set theory, presented in *Foundations of a General Theory of Manifolds* (1883), now underlies mathematical analysis, topology, functional analysis and measure theory.

Cantor developed the theory of infinite sets and transfinite numbers. He proved that the set of all real numbers is uncountable (1874), thus establishing the existence of infinite sets of different cardinalities. He defined the concepts of cardinality, the continuum, countable and uncountable sets.

He created one of the rigorous constructions of the real numbers; one formulation of the continuity axiom is associated with his name. In his construction, a real number is defined as a class of Cauchy sequences of rational numbers.

Cantor’s theory prompted a general reconsideration of the logical foundations of mathematics and influenced the whole structure of modern mathematics. He systematically used actual infinity. Many of his contemporaries, however, rejected actual infinity and accepted infinity only as a process. Kronecker was Cantor’s principal opponent.

Around the turn of the twentieth century, set theory found broad applications, including in measure theory. Logical difficulties nevertheless emerged, among them the Russell and Burali-Forti paradoxes. These problems intensified debates over the foundations of mathematics, in which formalism and intuitionism were among the positions that took shape.

Here is a mind-bender: “Cantor’s theorem: the cardinality of the set of all subsets of a given set is strictly greater than the cardinality of the set itself.”
