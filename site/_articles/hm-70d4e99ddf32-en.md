*Marking the 195th anniversary of his birth*

Richard Dedekind (6 October 1831–12 February 1916) was a German mathematician who brought a new precision to the study of numbers through definitions, axioms and rigorous logic. His name is firmly linked to three major nineteenth-century questions: the foundations of the real numbers, algebraic number theory and the philosophy of arithmetic.

## Brunswick, Göttingen and Zurich

He was born in Brunswick and spent most of his life there. He first studied at the local technical institution, the Collegium Carolinum, before continuing in Göttingen. As a young man Dedekind was deeply interested in physics, but gradually mathematics drew him more strongly. He sought an impeccable logical construction as well as formulas.

In 1852 Dedekind earned his doctorate in Göttingen under Carl Friedrich Gauss, becoming one of Gauss’s last pupils. Years of intensive study and work followed: he wanted to make up for what a conventional university education had not supplied and reach the forefront of European mathematics.

In 1858 he took a post at the Zurich Polytechnic, now ETH Zurich. In 1862 he returned to Brunswick, where he taught higher mathematics and for a time headed the institution. He did not pursue a conspicuous academic career; instead, he wrote texts that changed how generations of mathematicians thought.

## Cuts, Ideals and Numbers

Dedekind’s best-known idea is the cut. In his short 1872 work *Continuity and Irrational Numbers*, he proposed defining irrational numbers by dividing the rationals into two classes. This was a radical move: the continuity of the number line became an arithmetical fact rather than a geometrical picture. It produced one of the classical rigorous constructions of the real numbers.

His second great achievement was in algebraic number theory. Working on Dirichlet’s legacy, Dedekind prepared and published *Lectures on Number Theory*, adding his own supplements to later editions and developing the concept of an ideal. This restored unique factorization where ordinary integers no longer sufficed: in rings of algebraic integers, factorization of elements into irreducibles can fail, whereas every nonzero ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals.

Finally, there is Dedekind’s celebrated declaration in *The Nature and Meaning of Numbers* (*Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen?*, 1888): “Die Zahlen sind freie Schöpfungen des menschlichen Geistes”—“Numbers are free creations of the human mind.” Behind it lies a whole programme: construct the natural numbers as a logical system and explain induction, recursion and the foundations of arithmetic independently of geometry or physical intuition.

Dedekind’s ideas live on in mathematical terms and theorems, but something else matters more. He showed how a new mathematical world could arise from a careful definition. Here, unusually, rigorous exposition became a source of freedom.
