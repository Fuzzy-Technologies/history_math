The gyroscope was invented as an instrument for orientation in space. Before it, people used instruments such as the plumb line, level, compass and astrolabe.

A gyroscope is a rapidly rotating rigid body whose axis of rotation can change direction in space.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The simplest examples are rotating toys such as spinning tops, yo-yos and spinners. Bicycle and motorcycle wheels act as gyroscopes, preventing the rider from falling.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

The gyroscope was invented in 1817 by the German mathematician and astronomer Johann Bohnenberger. The main component of Bohnenberger's instrument was a rotating, massive sphere in a gimbal mounting.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

In 1852, the French scientist Jean Foucault improved the gyroscope and first used it as an instrument demonstrating the changing direction of the Earth's rotation. He also introduced the term “gyroscope”, from the Greek words γῦρος, “circle”, and σκοπέω, “I look”.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

A gyroscope demonstrates precession: when an attempt is made to turn a spinning body, a force arises perpendicular to the applied force. When an external torque acts about an axis perpendicular to the rotor's spin axis, the gyroscope begins to turn about a precession axis perpendicular to the external torque.

Gyroscopes are classified by their number of degrees of freedom, or possible axes of rotation, as two-degree or three-degree instruments, and by their operating principle as mechanical, optical or laser instruments.

Among mechanical gyroscopes, the rotor gyroscope is the main type: a rapidly rotating rigid body, or rotor. Its rate of spin greatly exceeds the rate at which its spin axis turns. Its principal property is the ability to preserve a fixed direction of the spin axis in space when no external torques act on it.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

The behaviour of a gyroscope in an inertial reference frame is described by a differential equation.

Angle sensors and torque sensors are used to control a gyroscope and obtain information from it. A gyroscope is sometimes called an angular-rate sensor: an inertial sensor measuring the angular velocity of a rotating object in °/s.

People make extensive use of gyroscopic properties. Gyroscopes are employed in shipping, aviation, drones, rockets and spacecraft, including gyrocompasses and attitude indicators, to solve many problems of navigation and control of moving objects. At least three gyroscopes are needed for quadcopter flight.

They are also used instead of compasses in mine surveying and topographic work. One example is the gyrotheodolite, a gyroscopic sighting instrument used to orient tunnels and mine workings and establish surveyed positions.

In firearms, the principle is applied as follows: a bullet fired from a rifled barrel is set spinning, which gives it much greater stability and substantially improves accuracy. Precision gyroscopes are used in the guidance systems of long-range strategic missiles.

More than a hundred different phenomena and physical principles are now known that can be used to solve gyroscopic problems.

According to advocates of navigation methods such as GPS and GLONASS, advances in high-precision satellite navigation have made autonomous gyroscopic systems unnecessary.
