Old Russian mathematics textbooks contain entertaining problems in verse. One is known as Yefim Voytyakhovsky’s problem.

A French madam newly arrived in Russia<br>
Wanted to value the wealth in her trunk:<br>
A fashionable, elegant fourreau,<br>
And a festive bonnet à la Figaro.<br>
The appraiser was a Russian man.<br>
To Madam thus his judgement ran:<br>
“Your first possession, the fourreau,<br>
Costs three and a half times the bonnet à la Figaro.<br>
Together they are worth not four and a half altyns;<br>
Their true price is only half of that.”<br>
Find the price of each of these things<br>
The French madam brought to the Russians.

Even Dmitry Mamin-Sibiryak wrote about the problem: “Among the ‘examples’ were some solved by a certain ‘blind’ or maiden’s rule; number 375 was this problem in verse.”

## Glossary

- Furo, from French *fourreau*: a narrow, straight dress.
- Vpolchetverta: three and a half times.
- An altyn: 3 kopecks.
- “Four and a half altyns”: $4.5$ altyns, or $13.5$ kopecks.

## Solution

Let the bonnet cost $x$ kopecks. The fourreau then costs $3.5x$. Their “true price is only half” of $4.5$ altyns, or $13.5$ kopecks: a total of $6.75$ kopecks. An equation gives:

$x+3.5x=13.5\div2$.<br>
$4.5x=6.75$.<br>
$x=1.5$.<br>
$3.5x=5.25$.

Answer: the bonnet costs $1.5$ kopecks ($0.5$ altyn), and the fourreau $5.25$ kopecks ($1.75$ altyns).

## Historical background

1. Yefim Dmitriyevich Voytyakhovsky (?–c. 1812) was a Russian mathematics teacher and career military man, holding the artillery rank of shtyk-yunker. He taught at the Artillery and Engineering Cadet Corps. In 1781 he opened a private mathematics school in Moscow. His five-volume *Theoretical and Applied Course in Pure Mathematics* (1786–1790) was reprinted many times.

The full title shows the course’s breadth: “A Theoretical and Practical Course in Pure Mathematics, Containing Arithmetic, Geometry, Trigonometry, with Practice and a Description of the Proportional Compass or Sector; Algebra with Higher Powers; Curvilinear Geometry, with the Theory and Practice of the Art of Bomb Projection; for the Benefit and Use of Young People and Those Studying Mathematics.”

2. The “blind” or “maiden’s” rule is a method of solving problems by starting to act even without a complete plan or understanding of how to reach the goal. In school arithmetic the name was often used for naive but workable computational schemes preceding a formal equation.

The idea is to take the first, simplest step towards a solution. It often reveals a new perspective and the next step, setting the solution in motion almost by itself.

It is a popular, somewhat old-fashioned name, based on the saying, “Do something, anything; perhaps you will work it out later.”

## Notes

D. N. Mamin-Sibiryak wrote about this problem in his works:

- D. N. Mamin-Sibiryak, *Sobraniye sochineniy v 10 tomakh* (Collected Works in Ten Volumes), vol. 10: Legends; Stories and Tales for Children; Autobiographical Note; Memoirs; Selected Letters, Moscow: Pravda, 1958, Ogonyok Library, pp. 295–304 (in Russian).

The problem also appears in popular mathematics books, for example:

- Y. I. Ignatyev, *V tsarstve smekalki, ili Arifmetika dlya vsekh* (In the Realm of Ingenuity, or Arithmetic for Everyone), a book for family and school, Book Three, Petrograd: A. S. Suvorin–Novoye Vremya, 1915, 322 pp. (in Russian).
