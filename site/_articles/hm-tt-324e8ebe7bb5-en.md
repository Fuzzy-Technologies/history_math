(For the 125th anniversary of the Yelabuga Institute, the 80th anniversary of the lifting of the siege of Leningrad, and the 80th anniversary of the liberation of Voronezh)

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-1" %}

The cooperation of three higher-education institutions in Yelabuga during the war exemplifies selfless service to a common cause: preserving scientific and teaching personnel and continuing their training and development. They were the Yelabuga State Teacher Training Institute, the Leningrad University branch and Voronezh University.

Our research shows that in those harsh years they lived as a single organism, surviving and preserving themselves through mutual support. They had, of course, come to Yelabuga by government order. Yet without the attention and human concern shown to the evacuees by the local authorities and the teacher training institute's leadership and staff, they would have struggled to resolve all their problems.

Academician V. A. Ambartsumyan, director of the Leningrad State University branch, recalled the Yelabuga residents' kindness towards the Leningraders and thanked them for their assistance and concern for Soviet science. Those from Voronezh believe that although only a tenth of the university reached Yelabuga, this small group preserved continuity and prevented their institution from fragmenting and closing. Despite every deprivation, it continued both teaching and research.

The Leningrad State University branch, which reached Yelabuga in August 1941, was a research branch rather than a teaching one. Its researchers in various fields, chiefly physics, mathematics, chemistry and biology, came from Leningrad University and other research institutions in the city. They were highly qualified and had already distinguished themselves in the 1930s by solving theoretical and applied scientific problems. They were therefore sent to the rear to continue their research and undertake new work of importance to defence. They brought their laboratory equipment with them. Classrooms in the teacher training institute were allocated as laboratories. The institute's leadership also resolved many questions of laboratory accommodation and the researchers' everyday living arrangements.

Voronezh University was in a more difficult position. In the summer of 1942, the Germans' unexpected and rapid advance prevented Voronezh from organizing the evacuation of many institutions, including the university. People had to leave in great haste, abandoning possessions and homes. The People's Commissariat of Education ordered the university's leadership to proceed to Yelabuga.

In early September, scattered groups of staff and students reached Yelabuga by different routes: 43 lecturers and employees and 56 students. On 10 September, the first Party meeting considered “Beginning the University's Work in Yelabuga” and made the fundamental decision to start the academic year on 1 October. All 6 faculties were accommodated in 26 rooms of the teacher training institute.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The first need was to obtain the minimum necessary equipment and teaching materials. Once again the teacher training institute came to their aid. Under director M. G. Strekalov's order of 17 September 1942, rooms, supplies and furniture were allocated, along with all the teaching equipment of the biology and chemistry rooms, all teaching literature in biological and chemical subjects, and some equipment and furniture from the Leningrad University branch and the pedagogical college. The institute's stables also had to accommodate Voronezh University's horses, the principal transport of the time. Kazan University gave its Voronezh colleagues invaluable help by sending two railway wagons of scientific literature, teaching materials and laboratory equipment to Yelabuga.

Expanding student enrolment was another problem for Voronezh University. Local young people provided new recruits, and by November about 200 students had been enrolled. Some graduates of the teacher training institute continued their studies at Voronezh University. It is known that the Yelabuga resident Lyubov Iosifovna Chagina, later an associate professor at the Yelabuga State Pedagogical Institute, was then in year 2 in Voronezh University's Faculty of History and Philology. Evacuated Leningraders also studied there, including N. A. Nikiforovskaya, who later wrote her memories of Yelabuga.

All three institutions also had to address the need for qualified teaching staff together. Some lecturers from the teacher training institute had left for the front. A large group of outstanding scholars from Leningrad and then Voronezh universities accepted invitations to teach part-time at the Yelabuga institute.

Throughout the evacuation, 1941–1944, academician V. I. Smirnov headed the Yelabuga Institute's Department of Physics and Mathematics, teaching mathematical analysis and elementary mathematics. The Leningraders N. A. Tolstoy and A. I. Petrova taught physics teaching methods, while associate professor G. I. Petrashen taught mathematics courses.

Professor V. V. Mavrodin (1942), followed by Professor S. I. Kovalyov (1942–1944), headed the Department of History. They taught historical and social subjects, as did Professors A. V. Venediktov, M. I. Artamonov and N. P. Poletika.

The Faculty of Natural Sciences and Geography employed Leningrad University Professors M. N. Tverskoy (physical geography), V. V. Sharonov (geology and mineralogy, cartography and topography) and B. N. Dolgov (general chemistry); Voronezh University Professors A. M. Sigriansky (head of the Department of Biology, teaching biology and zoology) and D. I. Damperov (geology and mineralogy); and Voronezh associate professors V. I. Bukhalova (ecology) and I. N. Yezhov (general and physical geography).

Voronezh associate professors A. S. Kuznetsova and O. A. Bavykina also taught social subjects. Voronezh Professor I. N. Zhuravlyov did extensive work developing and teaching an optional course to train physical-education teachers, introduced at the Yelabuga institute in 1942. V. K. Vasilyeva, a Candidate of Biological Sciences working at the Leningrad University branch, taught military medical training. Leningraders A. A. Ambartsumian (linguistics and speech culture), V. G. Zhuikova (general physics) and P. Ya. Molodenko (chemistry) also worked in various faculties.

For several months, Voronezh University's teaching staff was reinforced by Leningrad University chemistry professors B. P. Nikolsky and B. N. Dolgov, historians S. I. Kovalyov, V. I. Ravdonikas and A. V. Venediktov, mathematician V. I. Smirnov, N. A. Domnin, A. V. Smirnov and a number of other scholars.

The Voronezh scholars also resumed research. Almost all topics concerned defence or military patriotism. Geography faculty associate professor G. T. Grishin wrote “How the Economic Power of the Soviet Union Was Created”, arguing for the superiority of the socialist economy over Nazi Germany's economy. S. N. Benkliyev, dean of the Faculty of History and Philology and an associate professor, wrote the monograph The Role of the Slavic Peoples in the Fall of the Eastern Roman Empire.

Applied research was also undertaken. Chemists A. P. Palkin and associate professor S. A. Kretinin advised the local Promkombinat cooperative on organizing match production. The Department of Organic Chemistry resumed production of streptocide. Scientists from the Faculty of Biology conducted research at the local slaughterhouse and gave valuable advice on collecting blood and using it as food. Professor I. N. Zhuravlyov helped the meat-processing plant establish production of several pharmaceutical preparations, including hematogen. The young biology professor D. F. Petrov surprised local residents with a scientific approach to economizing on planting material. He recommended planting potato eyes rather than whole tubers. To many people's surprise the potatoes grew, although the harvest was poor.

Lecturers and students actively engaged in military training, helped collective farmers bring in the harvest and regularly contributed part of their pay to the defence fund. The scientists lived and worked under the same conditions and shared the same concerns. On 30 October 1941, Yelabuga's newspaper Stalin's Path published “The Contribution of University Employees”, reporting that the Leningrad University branch had given the district committee 9475 roubles in cash and various goods worth 1560 roubles to support the front.

The Voronezh group kept their spirits up and found the strength to organize aid for the front. Much work went into raising funds for the Soviet Scientist tank column. In April 1943 the rector received a government telegram: *“Please convey my fraternal greetings and the Red Army's gratitude to the scientific workers and students of Voronezh State University, who have collected 12000 roubles in cash and government-loan bonds worth 7500 roubles to build the Soviet Scientist tank column. I. Stalin.”*

The Voronezh historian Professor M. D. Karpachyov writes that the institutions' cooperation was exceptionally fruitful for both students and teachers. Voronezh University long remembered the brilliant lectures by its Leningrad colleagues, and the connections established proved very useful to its young scholars. Joint meetings of scholarly societies and scientific conferences were also held in Yelabuga.

Voronezh University completed its second semester of 1943 in Yelabuga. The summer practical training of history students is described as particularly successful. They were sent to archaeological excavations at well-known historical sites, including the Ananyino, Lugovskoy and other burial grounds. The eminent Leningrad archaeologists P. P. Yefimenko and V. I. Ravdonikas directed the training.

In January 1943 Soviet troops liberated Voronezh. The university spent the 1943–1944 academic year in Voronezh and Lipetsk.

The Leningraders in Yelabuga began thinking about returning home after the siege was lifted. The summons arrived in June 1944. Director M. G. Strekalov's order settling their accounts, granting leave and releasing them from work was issued on 19 June 1944.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

In May 2013, Yelabuga hosted the scientific conference “Three Institutions Working Together During the Great Patriotic War”. A delegation from St Petersburg State University attended, led by Vice-Rector for Research S. P. Tunik. A memorial plaque was unveiled commemorating the wartime work of the Leningrad University branch and Voronezh University in Yelabuga.

Thus the fortunes of three Russian higher-education institutions became intertwined. *“It truly was a time of arduous feats and profoundly moral relationships.”* (V. A. Ambartsumyan.)
