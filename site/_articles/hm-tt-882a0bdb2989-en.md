In the late 8th and early 9th centuries, Bulgars appeared in the Middle Volga region: some of the tribes of Great Bulgaria from the Sea of Azov region. Pre-Bulgar and Bulgar Turkic-speaking peoples entered into extensive ethnic and cultural contact with the local Finnic tribes. To complete the unification of all the tribes into a single state, the Bulgar princes decided to adopt Islam officially and publicly.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Ahmad ibn Fadlan's well-known travel account describes how an embassy arrived from Baghdad in 922 to establish Islamic law in Volga Bulgaria. The Bulgar emir publicly affirmed the official adoption of Islam. This marked the beginning of a new centralized Bulgar state in the north, Volga Bulgaria, a powerful neighbour of ancient Rus'. By the 12th century a single people had formed under the common name Bulgars.

Supreme authority belonged to the emir. The first towns appeared in the early 10th century. The largest were the capital Bulgar (Tatar Bolgar), Suvar and Bilyar. Remains from the Bulgar period are found in present-day Tatarstan and the Ulyanovsk and Samara regions.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

In the 8th–9th centuries, the Bulgars had the beginnings of writing in the eastern runic script of the Turkic Khaganate. With the adoption of Islam, it was replaced by Arabic script. Adopting this script promoted close cultural connections with the East and the development of literature, education and various sciences: history, medicine, philosophy and law. Intensive cultural contacts with Muslim centres encouraged education, culture and science to develop in Bulgaria on an Islamic foundation.

According to the Arab scholar and geographer Ibn Rustah, the towns and villages of Volga Bulgaria had mosques and schools. Literacy was taught in maktabs and madrasas. Education required mastery of Arabic and Persian as well as the native Turkic language.

The subjects studied in maktabs and madrasas can be grouped into five areas:

• an introduction to Arabic grammar and literary theory;

• the tenets of Islamic doctrine and religion;

• elements of Muslim legislation and law;

• information about Muslim rites and morality;

• the fundamentals of the exact sciences.

The maktab is a Muslim elementary school teaching literacy. The Russian/Tatar form mektebe comes from Arabic maktab, “school”, and kataba, “to write”; maktab literally means “a place where people write”. A maktab had one teacher who taught every subject. It was funded mainly by the local population and attended by boys. Its principal subject was Din dereslere, “Lessons in Religion”. Instruction was narrowly practical. The teacher was usually the imam, the head of the mosque, or the parish mullah. Most children who completed the maktab course ended their schooling there and began working in the household economy. Some children of wealthier parents who wished to continue their education entered a madrasa.

The madrasa is a Muslim school of secondary and higher level. The word medrese apparently derives from Arabic madarasat, literally “a place of teaching or study”, meaning a place where the Quran is read. Daras literally means “lesson, lecture”. Madrasa pupils were called shakirds, and a teacher was a mugallim, from Arabic muallim, “teacher”. Pupils were divided into four grades according to their progress through the course: iptidaia, rushdia, igdadia and galia.

Iptidaia was the lowest grade and corresponded to the elementary school, or maktab. The second grade, rushdia, involved three years of study and corresponded to a two-class urban school. The third, igdadia, was preparation for the highest grade and also lasted three years, corresponding to a gymnasium or a Realschule. The fourth, galia, was the highest class, with three years of study. Some madrasas organized a one-year preparatory class, tahziria, for pupils entering igdadia from other educational institutions. This division was not strictly observed in every madrasa, however. Shakirds advanced from one grade to another after mastering the prescribed body of material.

Madrasas prepared people for different professions depending on how much of the course they completed. Those completing the full course could become religious ministers, muftis (state officials), qadis (judges), tarikachi (people who handled the division of inheritances under a judge), or scribes. Those who completed igdadia mostly became heads of mosques and teachers in rural maktabs and madrasas. Thus a specialized, multistage educational system was established.

At the turn of the 18th–19th centuries, Tatar schools developed a fairly clear system of mathematics education. In maktabs this meant introductory arithmetic taught empirically, without a theoretical treatment of the subject. It was needed for trade calculations and the division of property. Arithmetic was rhetorical: operation signs and unknown quantities were expressed in words. Basic concepts of algebra and geometry — such as laws of operations, equations, the shapes and dimensions of figures, and areas — were also introduced in the arithmetic course. Geometry consisted of rules for solving practical problems. They were studied deductively, but without proofs.

In later madrasas, arithmetic, algebra and geometry with trigonometry were studied as logically connected branches of elementary mathematics, alongside physics and astronomy.

The best-known madrasas were Izh-Bubi, Apanayev, Akhunov, Muhammadiya, Kasimiya and Khusainiya.
