*Marking the 200th anniversary of his birth*

> “Pedagogy is not a science but an art—the most extensive, complex, highest and most necessary of all arts.” K. D. Ushinsky

A great Russian educator and writer, and one of the founders of scientific pedagogy in Russia.

## The Anniversary and Birth Year

At a meeting with members of the public in 2021, Russian President Vladimir Putin said: “To mark the highest public significance of the teaching profession, 2023—the 200th anniversary of the birth of Konstantin Dmitrievich Ushinsky, one of the founders of Russian pedagogy—will be dedicated in our country to teachers and mentors.”

That anniversary year, and the Year of the Teacher and Mentor, has passed. Yet we return to Ushinsky’s memory in connection with 2024. As the historian L. N. Modzalevsky, a coauthor of Ushinsky’s classic reader *The Native Word*, put it: “Ushinsky is truly our national educator, just as Lomonosov is our national scientist, Suvorov our national military commander, Pushkin our national poet and Glinka our national composer.”

The difficulty is that all encyclopaedic publications printed in the USSR and in the post-Soviet period currently give 1824 as the great educator’s birth year. That year is also inscribed on the K. D. Ushinsky Medal for Merit in the Field of Pedagogical Science.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Modern Russian researchers have established that Ushinsky was born in Tula on 19 February 1823, or 3 March in the Gregorian calendar.

We do not enter into this historical question. Here we consider only one aspect of his work: how his educational ideas influenced the improvement of mathematics teaching in his time.

His pedagogical system rested on the democratization of public education and education rooted in the people’s own culture, the principle of *narodnost*. His ideas appear in *Children’s World* (1861), *The Native Word* (1864), the fundamental two-volume work *Man as the Subject of Education: An Essay in Pedagogical Anthropology* (1868–1869) and other educational writings.

Ushinsky’s educational work had many dimensions, but mathematics teaching was clearly not among his principal research interests. Nevertheless, his ideas influenced the development of Russian thought on mathematics teaching methods.

## Arithmetic and Practical Activities

In 1864 Ushinsky published his celebrated *The Native Word*, outlining new approaches to teaching arithmetic in the appendix “On the Initial Teaching of Counting”. Here he set out fundamental principles for arithmetic teaching.

He sharply criticized the widespread school practice of memorizing mathematical rules: “It goes without saying that children should not learn any arithmetic rules by heart, but discover them for themselves.”

He argued that pupils should be given arshin and sazhen measures, scales, weights and coins as soon as possible, and allowed to “measure, weigh and count”. The arshin and sazhen are traditional Russian length measures. Problems should be practical and concrete, drawn from the world around children.

It is important to “teach a child to handle a group of ten with complete freedom—to divide, multiply and break it into parts”. In other words, he believed that pupils should learn operations as soon as they began counting within the first ten numbers, rather than simply studying number composition. This was a fundamental principle: introduce the foundations of arithmetic from the outset.

## First Lessons in Logic

Ushinsky attached enormous importance to teaching pupils to think logically. His *First Lessons in Logic* offers a brilliant example of explaining logical techniques and categories to children. It presents the basic concepts and rules of logic accessibly: comparison, difference and similarity, judgement, genera and species, properties, concepts, definition, cause and effect.
