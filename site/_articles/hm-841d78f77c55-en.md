Norbert Wiener (1894–1964) was an MIT mathematician often called the “father of cybernetics”. He made the word *cybernetics* famous, bringing together control, communication and feedback in machines and living systems.

In everyday life, however, Wiener became famous for something quite different: absent-mindedness. Several stories about it have become almost canonical.

## The new address

The best-known story, retold by mathematics populariser Howard Eves, concerns a move. Wiener’s wife put a slip of paper with their new address in his wallet. During the day, an idea struck him: he covered the paper with formulas, realised the idea was wrong and threw it away. That evening, he went to the old address and found that the family no longer lived there. Completely bewildered, he went back outside. Then an idea came to him. He approached a little girl standing nearby and said, “Excuse me, perhaps you remember me. I am Professor Wiener, and my family recently moved away from here. Could you tell me where they went?” The girl listened very carefully and replied, “Yes, Daddy. Mummy thought you would forget, so she sent me to show you the way home.”

## Have I had lunch?

Another little exchange from the corridors of MIT, recalled by Hanna Gray.

After they met in a corridor and talked for a while, Mr Wiener asked Charles, “Which way was I going when we met?” Charles pointed. “Ah,” said Mr Wiener, “then I have had lunch.”

## Allow me to introduce myself

Phyllis L. Block, an administrator in MIT’s mathematics department, recalled that Wiener’s office was a few doors down the corridor and he often stopped by to talk. When her office moved a few years later, he came in… to introduce himself all over again, failing to recognise her in the new room.

## The integral

Recalled by Gene B. Chase, in a story from Wiener’s course on Fourier series and integrals.

He wrote a very complicated integral on the board, then, without any intermediate steps, put $=6$. A student asked, “Could you do it another way?” Wiener left the room for about twenty minutes. On returning, he erased $=6$ and wrote $=6$ again, saying, “Yes, of course you can!”

## Wiener—that is the word!

And an especially anecdotal ending, also attributed to Eves’s retellings: a student greeted him, “Good afternoon, Professor Wiener!” Suddenly he slapped his forehead, cried, “Wiener—that is the word!” and ran off. He had been trying to remember a word for a crossword—a play on English *winner*, meaning “victor”, and his name, Norbert Wiener.
