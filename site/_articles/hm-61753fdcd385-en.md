Just when you think a medieval monk’s life was all manuscripts and prayers, along comes Bede (c. 672–735). In the eighth century, he was the man who could tackle a question that still starts arguments every year: how exactly do you calculate the date of Easter?

In 725, Bede wrote *De temporum ratione* (*The Reckoning of Time*). In the Church of his day, chronology effectively had one main purpose: *computus*, the calculation of Easter from ecclesiastical tables — the Sunday after the first tabular full moon falling on or after the ecclesiastical equinox of 21 March. It sounds simple… until you remember that solar years, lunar months, cycle tables and calendar rules all have to agree.

Bede used the 19-year Metonic cycle — an astronomical period based on the near equivalence of 19 solar years and 235 lunar months. His tables and explanations were copied and used for centuries: people wanted to celebrate Easter on the same day, rather than “whenever the neighbouring monastery worked it out”.

There was another interesting effect. Through his chronological writings, and especially his famous *An Ecclesiastical History of the English People* (731), Bede helped spread Anno Domini dating — “in the year of the Lord”, the very scale of years we now take for granted.

And his epithet is perfect for a mathematician who studied chronology: “the Venerable”. On his tomb in Durham, you can read:

“Hic sunt in fossa Bedae venerabilis ossa.”<br>
(“Here in this grave lie the bones of the Venerable Bede.”)
