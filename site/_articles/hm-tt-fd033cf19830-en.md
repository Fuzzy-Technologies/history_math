Here is an example straight away: draw four circles of different circumferences without changing the compass opening. You have 1 minute to solve it or think of an approach.

The revolutionary step is to move into space and generalize the concept of a circle in three dimensions. The circles specified can be drawn on surfaces of revolution, such as a sphere or cone, and on a plane if objects of different heights are placed under the compass point.

## Where to use qualitative problems and how to solve them {#section-1}

Every pupil studying physics encounters qualitative problems; see, for example, M. E. Tulchinsky’s *A Collection of Qualitative Problems in Physics* (Moscow: Prosveshchenie, 1965). Problems under this heading could also be called “practical questions”, “ingenuity questions”, “logic problems”, “trick questions”, “teasing problems”, “oral problems”, “qualitative questions”, “review questions” or “question-problems”. Teaching literature contains still other names for problems we consider qualitative: “original problems”, “nonstandard problems”, “problems without numerical data” and “beautiful problems”.

We believe the concept of a “qualitative problem in mathematics” is legitimate. Its chief characteristic is that solving it requires attention first to the qualitative aspect of the mathematical fact involved. Mathematical operations are used minimally. The solution proceeds logically from mathematical laws, rules, formulas and theorems.

Qualitative problems are as valuable in mathematics lessons as mental-arithmetic exercises. In classes specializing in the humanities, they deserve even greater attention.

Their principal purpose should be to awaken pupils’ intellectual curiosity. To do this, their emotional impact should be enhanced: they should be “beautiful” and “spectacular”. Where possible, the statement and solution can be staged as a miniature performance with visual aids and scenery.

A qualitative problem usually takes 2–3 minutes to solve. Sometimes, however, it involves an unpredictable move or an unexpected result. Pupils should see that the answer is surprisingly simple and elegant. They should gain confidence that such problems can be solved and offer intellectual pleasure. Qualitative problems are also used in extracurricular work and are an important part of mathematical KVN contests, quizzes and Brain Ring competitions.

We can therefore give this definition: qualitative problems are original logical problems applying mathematical concepts and their properties, solved through ingenuity and imagination in a short time (30–60 seconds), possibly as a group. Their setting should be presented clearly, vividly, spectacularly and attractively.

## Examples {#section-2}

Here are examples of different types of qualitative problems for you to enjoy solving.

1\. Find the sum of the angles at the points of an arbitrary five-pointed star. Hint: use the sum of a triangle’s angles.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

2\. What is the value of a fraction with КАРЛСОН (“Karlson”) in the numerator and ВАРЕНЬЕ (“jam”) in the denominator? (К∙А∙Р∙Л∙С∙О∙Н / В∙А∙Р∙Е∙Н∙Ь∙Е). Different letters stand for different digits, with multiplication signs between them. Hint: the numeral system is decimal.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

3\. Divide a cake fairly into 5 pieces, so that everyone is satisfied with the piece they receive.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

4\. They travelled around a track. They never turned off. They disappeared. How many of them were there?

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

5\. Continue the number sequence: 111, 213, 141, 516, 171, ...

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

Solving these problems will delight adults as well as children. Good luck! Write to us if you enjoy them, and we will prepare more.
