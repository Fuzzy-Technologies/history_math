*Marking the 150th anniversary of his birth*

Dmitry Dmitrievich Mordukhai-Boltovskoi (27 July [8 August] 1876–7 February 1952) was a Russian and Soviet mathematician, historian of mathematics, educational methodologist and teacher. He helped establish the Rostov mathematical school, combining rigorous theory with attention to how mathematics actually lives in the classroom and textbook.

## St Petersburg, Warsaw and Novocherkassk

He was born in Pavlovsk, near St Petersburg. He finished a classical secondary school in 1894 and graduated from St Petersburg University in 1898, having studied with K. A. Posse and A. A. Markov. On his teachers’ recommendation, the young mathematician went to Warsaw and began teaching at the Polytechnic Institute, alongside the strong school that included G. F. Voronoi.

His early mathematical development centred on analysis and differential equations. In 1906 he defended his master’s dissertation on Abelian integrals. In 1907 he helped establish the Don Polytechnic Institute in Novocherkassk, then returned to Warsaw. In 1909 he became a professor at Warsaw University.

## Research and the Rostov School

His interests increasingly turned towards a question at the forefront of mathematics in the early twentieth century: when can a differential equation be solved in finite form, using combinations of elementary operations and integrals? In 1910 he developed Liouville’s ideas on solvability by quadratures; the subject lives on, for example, in the Liouville–Mordukhai-Boltovskoi criterion. He also worked in number theory and geometry, including projective and non-Euclidean geometry.

The turning point came in 1915, when Mordukhai-Boltovskoi moved to Rostov-on-Don with the evacuated Warsaw University. He spent almost all the rest of his life there. He taught at the university for about thirty-five years, lecturing across mathematics and building a research community in southern Russia: seminars, problems, pupils and traditions.

The scale of his work emerges from the biographical article in *Uspekhi Matematicheskikh Nauk*, which records more than two hundred publications and a substantial teaching load, including dozens of courses and the supervision of postgraduate students. Official recognition came in the 1930s, when he was awarded the degree of Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences without defending a dissertation.

## Classical Texts and Teaching

Working with original sources and the history of mathematics forms a separate chapter of his legacy. He opened two fundamental texts to twentieth-century Russian readers: a translation from ancient Greek of Euclid’s *Elements* (1948–1950), with extensive commentary, and a translation from Latin of Isaac Newton’s *Mathematical Works* (1937), with an introductory essay and notes. These were more than translations: they offered a scholarly way of reading the classics, without mythology and with respect for precise meaning.

He also played an active part in mathematics education. He spoke at all-Russian congresses of mathematics teachers and wrote about teaching methods and the design of geometry and analysis courses that would teach reasoning rather than computation alone. Rostov University sources and Mathedu also discuss his work on Hilbert’s problems, crediting him with a solution to the twenty-second and with coming close to the seventh, later solved by A. O. Gelfond.

During and immediately after the war he moved frequently: to Pyatigorsk, then Ivanovo, back to Rostov and again to Pyatigorsk. He died in Rostov-on-Don while visiting on a short holiday. Mordukhai-Boltovskoi is remembered as a scholar of encyclopedic breadth, thinking across subjects from rigorous criteria for equations to the history of geometrical ideas. His name remains part of both mathematics and its educational culture, through his pupils, articles and books to which readers still return.
