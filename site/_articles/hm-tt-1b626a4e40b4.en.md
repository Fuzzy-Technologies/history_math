An arithmometer is a desktop or portable mechanical calculating machine designed to perform arithmetic operations on numbers.

The name comes from Greek αρiθμος, “number”, and μετρεω, “measure”, and literally means “measuring numbers”.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Leonardo da Vinci is believed to have drawn the design of the first arithmometer. Dated to 1500, the device is a 13-digit adding machine using ten-toothed wheels. Da Vinci's ideas did not become widespread in his own time.

In the modern era, the first calculating machines were constructed by W. Schickard (1623), B. Pascal (the Pascaline, 1642) and G. W. Leibniz (1674).

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

The Parisian engineer Thomas de Colmar proposed the word “arithmometer” in 1820. He created the first commercial arithmometer, based on the principle of G. W. Leibniz's calculator. Real progress in arithmometer design came with P. L. Chebyshev's invention of a continuous-action arithmometer (1876) and the creation of the basic mechanism of all arithmometer designs, the “Odhner wheel”, named after its inventor, the Swedish–Russian engineer W. T. Odhner (1874).

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

Of the many arithmometer systems used in Russia, the Felix, manufactured in 1929–1978, became the most widespread. It was a modification of Odhner's arithmometer. Schoolchildren were taught to operate it.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

Arithmometers mechanized all four arithmetic operations. They performed calculations 10–12 times faster than ordinary calculation on paper.

Their operating principle is digit-by-digit addition and shifting of the sum of partial products. The work is mechanical, using rotating toothed wheels and cylinders. The sequence of operations is always set manually: immediately before each operation, the appropriate key must be pressed or the required lever turned. Numbers are entered into the Felix by moving special small levers up and down.

An arithmometer consists of:

— a setting mechanism: pins moved along slots numbered from 0 to 9;

— a crank handle;

— a carriage with two rows of windows: result windows and revolution-counter windows;

— reset handles, known as “swallows”, whose rotation replaces the digits in the result and crank-revolution-counter windows with zeros;

— metal decimal markers, sliding along a rail;

— a carriage-shifting mechanism.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-8" %}

As computing technology developed, electronic pocket calculators displaced arithmometers in the 1970s.
