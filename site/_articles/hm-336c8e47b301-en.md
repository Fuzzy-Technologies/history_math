*Marking the 105th anniversary of his birth*

In school mathematics, geometry teaching has suffered more than any other subject. Its content and teaching methods have been the subject of constant argument, and the course has repeatedly undergone fundamental changes. Authors of school geometry textbooks have faced criticism from every direction, regardless of their scientific standing. Academician Alexey Vasilyevich Pogorelov was one such author.

Unlike many of his fellow geometers, Alexey Vasilyevich became known not only to mathematicians but to millions of ordinary people—who has not heard of Pogorelov’s school geometry textbook? Though perhaps today some readers have not.

## Education and Research

Alexey Vasilyevich Pogorelov was a Soviet mathematician and educator. He received his Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences degree in 1948, became a full member of the USSR Academy of Sciences, later the Russian Academy of Sciences, in 1976, and a full member of the Ukrainian SSR Academy of Sciences in 1960.

He wrote a school geometry textbook and university textbooks on analytic geometry, differential geometry and the foundations of geometry.

He was born into a working-class family on 3 March 1919 in Korocha, Kursk Governorate, now Belgorod Region. In 1931 his family moved to Kharkiv.

After completing secondary school, he entered the mathematics division of the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics at Kharkiv State University in 1938.

In 1941 he was sent on an eleven-month course at the Zhukovsky Air Force Engineering Academy. After completing the academy course, he was assigned to work as a design engineer at the Zhukovsky Central Aerohydrodynamic Institute, TsAGI.

His wish to complete his university education and devote himself seriously to geometry brought Pogorelov to Moscow State University. He enrolled in part-time postgraduate study in its Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics under Nikolai Efimov, working on topics proposed by Alexandr Alexandrov. After defending his Candidate of Sciences dissertation in 1947, he was demobilized and moved to Kharkiv.

He worked at the Research Institute of Mathematics and Mechanics at Kharkiv State University and in its geometry department. He defended his doctoral dissertation in 1948. He headed the University’s Department of Geometry in 1951–1960 and the Department of Geometry at the Institute for Low Temperature Physics and Engineering of the Ukrainian SSR Academy of Sciences in 1960–2000.

Pogorelov retained his passion for engineering and technology throughout his life. He combined pure mathematics with its practical use in a remarkable way.

From 2000 he lived in Moscow and worked at the Steklov Mathematical Institute.

## Geometry Research and Prizes

His principal research areas were convex and differential geometry, differential equations and shell theory.

In his work on Hilbert’s fourth problem, Pogorelov described a broad class of metrics in which straight lines are geodesics.

He received the following prizes:

- Stalin Prize, second class (1950), for work on the theory of convex surfaces;
- Lenin Prize (1962), for research in geometry in the large;
- Lobachevsky Prize (1959), for “Some Questions of Geometry in the Large in Riemannian Space”;
- Krylov Prize of the Ukrainian SSR Academy of Sciences (1988);
- State Prize of the Ukrainian SSR (1973);
- Bogolyubov Prize of the National Academy of Sciences of Ukraine (1998).

## The School Textbook

In the late 1960s, Pogorelov increasingly came to believe that school geometry education needed reform. In the mid-1970s he prepared a set of school geometry textbooks. From their widespread introduction in the 1980s until the 2000s, editions appeared in many languages with print runs in the millions.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The appeal of Pogorelov’s textbook lay in its development of the carefully thought-out classical textbooks and problem books of earlier years. It built the geometry course on an axiomatic approach. One of its chief difficulties was the demanding problems, which many schoolchildren found hard to understand. The course rested on a rigorous axiom system, but its exposition and problem set required substantial methodological adaptation by the teacher.

In the history of mathematics, Pogorelov remains one of the leading geometers of the twentieth century.
