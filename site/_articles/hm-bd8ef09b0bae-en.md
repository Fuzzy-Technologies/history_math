He was born around 1170 in Pisa, in the Republic of Pisa.

One of the foremost mathematicians of medieval Western Europe was Leonardo of Pisa, also known as Fibonacci. He absorbed and developed many achievements of ancient and Arabic-language mathematical traditions. His father ran a Pisan trading post at Béjaïa, in present-day Algeria, where Leonardo studied mathematics with Arab teachers. As a merchant he visited Egypt, Syria, Byzantium and Sicily, adding to his mathematical knowledge.

The name Fibonacci came into use in the nineteenth century and is traditionally explained as a contraction of the Latin *filius Bonacci*, “son of Bonacci.”

Leonardo Fibonacci’s principal mathematical work was *Liber Abaci* (*The Book of the Abacus*, 1202). By abacus he meant arithmetic in general. He systematized knowledge drawn from Arabic works and ancient mathematics and added his own problems and methods. The book helped spread the new arithmetic in Europe. Its exposition used Indian numeration and compared it with arithmetic using Roman numerals. At that time, very few Europeans knew the positional number system and Indian digits.

The book also considered prime factorization, divisibility tests, methods for solving problems in commercial arithmetic, mixture problems and the summation of progressions.

In *Liber Abaci*, Leonardo described the recurrence sequence now known as the Fibonacci sequence. Apart from its first two terms, both equal to one, each term is the sum of its two predecessors: $1,1,2,3,5,8,13,\ldots$. The sequence appears in the famous rabbit problem: “How many pairs of rabbits will there be after one year, starting from a single pair, if each mature pair produces a new pair every month, each new pair begins reproducing after one month, and no pair dies?” Fibonacci numbers now have applications in many areas of science; for example, they are connected with the golden ratio.

Fibonacci presented the algebra of linear and quadratic equations. He considered the same six classes of equations as al-Khwarizmi, solved by *al-jabr* and *al-muqabala*. The exposition is verbal, although it includes abbreviations. He called the unknown *res* (thing) or *radix* (root). He investigated systems of linear equations with several unknowns and interpreted negative quantities as debts.

His other works include *Practica Geometriae* (*The Practice of Geometry*, 1220), *Flos* (*The Flower*, 1225) and *Liber Quadratorum* (*The Book of Squares*, 1225).

Leonardo Fibonacci was a convinced advocate of the new decimal numeration, but it entered everyday use very slowly. Many commercial ledgers used Roman numerals, while calculations were performed on the abacus. A 1299 statute of the Florentine money changers’ guild prohibited Hindu-Arabic numerals in official records. Their use in Italian merchants’ account books expanded during the fourteenth century.

The Fibonacci Association and its research journal, *The Fibonacci Quarterly*, devoted to Fibonacci numbers, bear his name.
