Teachers sometimes need simple hands-on experiments to help pupils see what a method means. I. Ya. Depman describes one such technique in his book *Rasskazy o reshenii zadach* (Stories about Solving Problems).

Old geometry textbooks and teaching manuals often used compass-and-straightedge construction problems. One classical approach is to assume the problem is already solved, work backwards from the desired figure to the given information, then reverse the process. Pupils often find this stage of the analytic-synthetic method difficult. It is known as ascending analysis.

Consider the familiar knot-tying problem.

“A piece of rope lies straight on a table. Take one end in one hand and the other end in the other hand, then tie a knot without letting go of either end.”

Not everyone finds the solution straight away. Try it yourself.

## Solution

Let us use the method recommended in geometry.

Analysis. Suppose the knot is already tied and you are holding both ends. Straighten the rope without letting go. The left hand, passing beneath the stretched rope and above the right arm, will hold the right-hand end; the right hand, passing above the rope and beneath the left arm, will hold the left-hand end. This is the required arrangement of the arms.

Construction (synthesis). Follow these steps.

1. The rope lies on the table.
2. Place your right arm over the rope and take the left-hand end.
3. Pass your left arm beneath the rope and above your right arm, then take the right-hand end.
4. Without letting go, move your arms apart.

The rope will form a knot, with every condition of the problem satisfied. This last step corresponds to the proof stage in a geometry problem.

The experiment demonstrates analysis and synthesis: first analyse — “the solution already exists” — then synthesise by carrying out the steps you have found. Classical geometry often uses this approach for compass-and-straightedge constructions, but it works just as well beyond geometry.
