(Leningrad — Yelabuga — Kazan)

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-1" %}

From autumn 1942 to autumn 1943, Lev Borisovich Modzalevsky (1902–1948) lived with his family at 109 Azin Street in Yelabuga. A historian of Russian literature and Pushkin scholar, he was the best-known archivist of 1930–1950.

In his 46 years of life, he did much to study the work of some of Russia’s greatest figures: Lomonosov and Pushkin, and even Lobachevsky. Although Lobachevsky does not belong among the great Russian writers, it was Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky (1792–1856) who led us to L. B. Modzalevsky.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Lobachevsky’s name is sacred to Kazan, Kazan University and Russian science as a whole. He was a great Russian mathematician, a brilliant geometer and the creator of non-Euclidean geometry. He was also more than a scholar: he was Kazan University’s best-known rector and its “great builder”.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Many researchers of N. I. Lobachevsky’s life and work cite the fundamental *Materials for a Biography of N. I. Lobachevsky* (USSR Academy of Sciences Press, 1948), compiled and edited by L. B. Modzalevsky. The Leningrad literary scholar and archivist began collecting material for Lobachevsky’s biography in 1942 in Kazan, at the initiative of the Academy’s Commission on its History. A substantial part of the USSR Academy of Sciences had been evacuated there during the war. Its 827 large-format pages contain 622 documents, various recollections of Lobachevsky and a wealth of reference material.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

Who, then, was this versatile historian, Lev Borisovich Modzalevsky?

Much can be learned about the lives and work of several generations of the renowned Modzalevsky family from the biographical book written by Lev Borisovich’s daughter, Tatyana Lvovna Modzalevskaya.

The book is dedicated primarily to the memory of L. B. Modzalevsky: a historian of Russian literature of the thirteenth to nineteenth centuries, a gifted scholar, Doctor of Philology, professor, and member of staff at Pushkin House and the Academy of Sciences Archive, the institutions with which his whole life was associated.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

Lev Borisovich Modzalevsky was born in St Petersburg on 27 July 1902. His father was Boris Lvovich Modzalevsky (1874–1928), the well-known literary historian and Pushkin scholar, and one of the founders of Pushkin House, which became the Institute of Russian Literature of the USSR Academy of Sciences, IRLI, in 1930.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

On 15 (28) December 1905, at the initiative of several figures in Russian culture, Nicholas II signed a decree founding Pushkin House as a repository for the manuscripts of Alexander Sergeevich Pushkin and the great figures of his era. B. L. Modzalevsky assembled its core collection. He also drafted the “Statutes of Pushkin House”, approved by Emperor Nicholas II in 1907. He was the institution’s director in 1922–1924.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

Boris Lvovich’s own father was Lev Nikolaevich Modzalevsky (1837–1896), the outstanding educator, director of the Tiflis Girls’ Gymnasium and former tutor to the sons of Grand Duke Mikhail Nikolaevich.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-8" %}

Lev Nikolaevich wrote works on history and education. He is also known as a children’s writer and poet, and a contributor to K. D. Ushinsky’s classic reader *The Native Word* (1864). This collection of poetry for children first published verses such as “Children, Get Ready for School …”, “Finish Your Work, Then Play without Worry”, and “Descend to Us, Quiet Evening …”. We once learned his poem “An Invitation to School” by heart from our schoolbooks:

*Children! Get ready for school,*

*The little rooster crowed long ago.*

*Hurry now and get dressed!*

*The sun is looking through the window.*

*People, beasts and little birds —*

*Everyone sets about their work;*

*A little insect trudges with its load,*

*A bee flies off in search of honey.*

*The field is bright, the meadow cheerful,*

*The forest has awakened and rustles;*

*The woodpecker taps with its beak!*

*The oriole calls out clearly.*

*The fishermen are already hauling their nets,*

*A scythe rings out in the meadow …*

*After your prayers, children, take up your books!*

*God does not permit idleness.*

(Of course, we learned the poem without its last stanza.)

The names of L. B. Modzalevsky, his father B. L. Modzalevsky and his grandfather L. N. Modzalevsky are recorded for posterity in the biographical index *Famous University Alumni: Graduates of St Petersburg–Petrograd–Leningrad University* (2002).

Lev Borisovich received a good education at home. From childhood he learned French and piano. In 1910, he entered the preparatory class of K. I. May’s gymnasium. His first historical studies appeared in the school magazine; for example, issue No. 4 opened with his article “Who Are the Tatars, and Where Did They Come From?”. He completed his secondary education under Soviet rule in 1919. In 1922, he entered the Faculty of Social Sciences at Petrograd University, graduating in 1925.

In 1926, Lev Modzalevsky began scholarly work at the Academy of Sciences Archive. After his father’s death in 1928, he obtained a research post at Pushkin House. Between 1924 and 1932, his first studies of nineteenth-century Russian literature appeared, devoted to A. K. Tolstoy, including poems, parodies, improvisations and unpublished works by Kozma Prutkov, and to L. N. Tolstoy’s unpublished correspondence. His most significant works were *In Pushkin’s Hand* and *Pushkin’s Conversations* (1929).

In 1933, the Presidium of the USSR Academy of Sciences appointed Modzalevsky to the Pushkin Commission. In effect, he became the first full-time custodian of the Pushkin manuscript collection. He worked there until his final days, combining this with work for the Commission on the History of the USSR Academy of Sciences and its Archive. His scholarly interests included archival science, source studies, Pushkin studies, textual scholarship, bibliography and genealogy.

In 1935, Lev Borisovich received the degree of Candidate of Philological Sciences. From 1933 to 1948, he published numerous articles, chiefly on the work of Trediakovsky, Bryullov, Lomonosov, Zhukovsky, Lermontov and Gogol.

He did especially much, of course, for the comprehensive study of A. S. Pushkin’s life and work. B. L. Modzalevsky had planned an edition of the great poet’s correspondence and published two volumes, in 1926 and 1927. His son, L. B. Modzalevsky, continued the work with the third volume in 1935.

Lev Borisovich lived through the beginning of the Great Patriotic War and the terrible first year of the siege in the city on the Neva, remaining there until July 1942. His wife, Evgenia Alekseevna Modzalevskaya, stayed with him. In July 1942, the family was evacuated from Leningrad to Kazan on an Academy of Sciences train. His wife’s serious illness then forced them to move to Yelabuga so she could recover.

He continued working as far as his strength allowed. The Archive of the Russian Academy of Sciences holds his letter to Academician Sergei Ivanovich Vavilov, sent from Yelabuga on 4 February 1943. He lamented that work on Lobachevsky’s biography, which he had begun in Kazan, had stalled. He also told Vavilov that he had accepted an offer from Voronezh State University, evacuated to Yelabuga, to lecture on eighteenth-century Russian literature and head the Department of Russian and World Literature.

*“The university obligations I have undertaken until the end of the academic year prevent me from even considering leaving Yelabuga immediately.”* Significantly, Modzalevsky also reported that neither he nor his family had *“felt boots or the necessary warm clothing”. “Frosts here reach 45°C. We sometimes suffer greatly from the cold, although we laid in some firewood for the winter.”*

In September 1943, they moved permanently to Kazan. In 1943–1944, he served as scholarly secretary to the Museum and Archive commissions under the Academy’s Presidium and made inspection trips from Kazan to Moscow, Tomsk, Novosibirsk and Sverdlovsk. Pushkin House’s library, a large collection of manuscripts, museum exhibits and priceless books had been moved to these cities.

The family returned to Leningrad in May 1944. In June 1945, Modzalevsky received the Order of the Badge of Honour and the medals “For the Defence of Leningrad” and “For Valiant Labour in the Great Patriotic War 1941–1945”.

In 1947, at Pushkin House, he defended his doctoral dissertation in philology, “Lomonosov and His Literary Relationships at the Academy of Sciences”. The St Petersburg branch of the Russian Academy of Sciences Archive published his monograph in 2011 for the 300th anniversary of M. V. Lomonosov’s birth.

His last work concerned materials for N. I. Lobachevsky’s biography. This monumental book appeared in 1948 under Modzalevsky’s editorship, with Academician S. I. Vavilov as editor-in-chief. In his preface, Vavilov writes that the materials collected, selected and annotated by Modzalevsky cast new light on many aspects of Lobachevsky’s scientific, teaching, organisational and personal life.

On 26 June 1948, Modzalevsky died tragically in unexplained circumstances while travelling to Moscow on an assignment to study A. S. Pushkin’s autograph manuscripts. The official account was an accident: Lev Borisovich had “fallen” from the Moscow–Leningrad train.

Thus the name of another remarkable Soviet scholar, Lev Borisovich Modzalevsky, became part of the wartime history of Yelabuga and Kazan.
