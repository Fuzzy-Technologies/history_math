Let us remember the women who contributed to mathematics despite every barrier and obstacle imposed by men and the state.

Women inspired the “stronger half” of humanity to scientific achievements, and themselves boldly entered the dark rooms and burning houses where mathematical secrets lay hidden.

As soon as the first ancient Greek philosophical schools appeared, remarkable Greek women graced them. Women were among the pupils of the first legendary mathematician, Pythagoras. One was his wife Theano, who lived in the sixth century BCE. After Pythagoras's death, Theano ran his school for another six years!

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Yet the beautiful Hypatia (370–415 CE), from Alexandria, the capital of Hellenistic culture, is considered the first woman mathematician. A philosopher, mathematician and astronomer, she was the acknowledged head of a philosophical school. Commentaries on Ptolemy's *Almagest*, Diophantus's *Arithmetica* and Apollonius's *Conics* are attributed to her. Reports that she invented the hydrometer, astrolabe and planisphere also indicate the direction of her scientific interests. Hypatia became the last known representative of the Alexandrian school. I recommend the feature film *Agora* on this subject.

Hypatia also participated in the city's public affairs and was highly popular. A mob of fanatics killed her for refusing to embrace Christianity. Some historical studies of Hypatia, however, argue that she died because of her involvement in politics, rather than because she was a pagan, a scholar or a woman.

For a long time afterwards, women did not pursue science because no religion or prevailing social view permitted it. Indeed, practising science that did not conform to orthodox beliefs was dangerous even for men. Nevertheless, gifted and intelligent women embarked on scientific inquiry.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

The world's first woman programmer was Lady Ada Lovelace (1815–1852), an English mathematician, countess and daughter of the poet Byron. She is best known for her description of the calculating machine designed by Charles Babbage. Ada wrote the world's first program for this machine. In her scientific notes, Lady Ada Lovelace predicted that the Analytical Engine could create algebraic formulas and eventually compose music and paint pictures, opening *“paths for science of which we have never dreamed”*.

The lot of women mathematicians has always been difficult. Russian women seeking to create mathematics as well as study it were no exception.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

The first among them is said to be Sofya Vasilyevna Kovalevskaya (1850–1891), a Russian mathematician, mechanician, writer and essayist, and a corresponding member of the Saint Petersburg Academy of Sciences. Born in Moscow, she showed extraordinary mathematical ability early in life. At that time, however, Russian universities did not admit women.

To pursue science, she went to Germany, studying at the universities of Heidelberg and Berlin. She worked with the celebrated mathematician Weierstrass, one of the creators of modern analysis. The University of Göttingen awarded her a doctorate in philosophy.

She then returned to Russia but had no opportunity to apply her knowledge. She was invited to work at Stockholm University, where she remained for 8 years. In 1888, she wrote “On the Rotation of a Rigid Body about a Fixed Point”, for which she received a prize from the Paris Academy of Sciences and later another from the Swedish Academy of Sciences.

Kovalevskaya did eventually gain worldwide recognition as a scholar. She was also the first woman to win high recognition for scientific achievement in Russia. Her election as a corresponding member of the Saint Petersburg Academy of Sciences was an exceptional event for Russia. She also wrote literary works, including the well-known *Recollections of Childhood* and *The Nihilist Girl*.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

Women's position improved to some extent in enlightened twentieth-century Europe. For example, mathematics as a whole became increasingly algebraic during that century. Among those who contributed greatly to this process was the German mathematician Emmy Noether (1882–1935), who even led the creation of abstract algebra. Noether was the best-known woman mathematician of the twentieth century and a professor at the University of Göttingen.

Yet despite her exceptional achievements, she never obtained a professorial position, although the mathematics faculty repeatedly petitioned the university administration. Such an appointment required the consent of all university professors, many of whom believed that a woman could not be a professor. The famous mathematician David Hilbert is known to have objected to these reactionaries:

*“Gentlemen, this is a university, after all, not the men's section of a bathhouse!”*

When the celebrated German mathematician Edmund Landau was asked whether Emmy Noether was indeed a great woman mathematician, he replied:

*“I can swear that she is a great mathematician, but I cannot swear that she is a woman!”*

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

Women's mathematical talents also emerged in Russia, especially later in the USSR. One such woman was Elena Sergeevna Wentzel (1907–2002), a Soviet mathematician and well-known author of textbooks on probability theory, operations research, game theory and dynamic programming, a doctor of technical sciences and professor. She was also a celebrated writer under the pseudonym I. Grekova. Her literary works were adapted for the stage and screen, including *The Department* and *Bless the Woman*. Her own path to the heights of mathematics therefore reads like the plot of an engaging story.

Her father taught mathematics and believed higher mathematics was simpler than elementary mathematics. He studied it with his daughter when she was 7–8 years old. As a result, mathematics “fell in love” with Elena Sergeevna. She graduated from the physics and mathematics faculty of Leningrad University and worked for many years at the N. E. Zhukovsky Air Force Academy.

Her husband, Dmitry Alexandrovich Wentzel, was head of the ballistics department, a professor, doctor of technical sciences and major-general of aviation. He viewed his wife's scientific achievements sceptically. When Elena Sergeevna was preparing to defend her doctoral dissertation, she had good reason to suspect that this full member of the Academy of Artillery Sciences would do everything he could to prevent her success. She scheduled the defence for the start of their holiday. They bought tickets and travelled south. In Serpukhov, it suddenly emerged that the iron had been left on at home. She assured him that this was no problem: she would return to Moscow, turn it off and catch up with him the next day. Of course, the car took her from Serpukhov to the academy rather than home. The defence succeeded. Dmitry Alexandrovich learned what had happened only several days later, in the south.

As we can see, women mathematicians faced a difficult path into science, yet they achieved remarkable creative heights.
