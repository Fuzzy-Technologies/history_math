> “Patronage of the sciences has always been my chief aim; what was lacking was a great monarch sufficiently interested in the cause.”  
> G. W. Leibniz

Gottfried Leibniz was born on 21 June (1 July) 1646 in Leipzig, Saxony, in the Holy Roman Empire.

The brilliant German scholar Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz is remembered above all as a founder of mathematical analysis, but he was also a physicist, philosopher, inventor and linguist. He contributed to logic, history, law, diplomacy, biology, geology and psychology. Leibniz is regarded as one of the most wide-ranging geniuses in human history.

He graduated from Leipzig University's law faculty and studied mathematics at the University of Jena. He worked in legal and diplomatic service and travelled to Paris, where his creative mathematical work began. There he met Huygens and, under his guidance, studied Galileo, Descartes, Fermat, Pascal and Huygens himself. He spent most of his life in Hanover, serving the duke as librarian, bibliographer and privy councillor for justice.

In 1700 Leibniz organized the Academy of Sciences in Berlin and became its first president. He was a member of the Royal Society of London from 1673 and a foreign member of the French Academy of Sciences. He helped promote academies of sciences in Vienna and St Petersburg. He met Peter I and worked on a plan for organizing education in Russia.

His many scientific achievements could be listed at length. He discovered kinetic energy and developed theories of geological evolution and the psychology of the subconscious. In diplomacy, he sought to promote the unification of the German states. He anticipated the European Union.

Leibniz developed his new calculus in 1673–1676, under Huygens's influence and while studying Descartes and Pascal. He knew that Newton possessed a similar method. Newton's approach was mechanical, however, while Leibniz's was geometrical. Rather than beginning with quadrature of curves, as Newton did, he began with the tangent problem. He considered the characteristic triangle (dx, dy, dz), which had already appeared in Pascal. He brought earlier isolated techniques into a unified system of interrelated analytical concepts, allowing operations on infinitesimals to follow a defined algorithm.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Leibniz first presented his calculus in print in 1684, in “A New Method for Maxima and Minima, and Also for Tangents, Which Is Obstructed Neither by Fractional nor by Irrational Quantities, and a Remarkable Type of Calculus for Them.” It introduced the symbols dx and dy, differentiation rules for products and quotients, and conditions for extrema and inflection points.

His explanations shared Newton's lack of rigour and ambiguity. Sometimes dx and dy were finite quantities; sometimes they were smaller than any specified quantity, yet still not zero.

A further article, “On Hidden Geometry…,” appeared in 1686, giving the rules of integral calculus. It contained the symbol ∫, which Leibniz called a “sum”; Jacob Bernoulli later introduced the term “integral.”

Leibniz was one of the most prolific inventors of modern mathematical terminology and notation. Few mathematicians understood so well the unity of form and content in symbols. The name “differential and integral calculus” is also his. He introduced the terms function, variable quantity, coordinates, abscissa, ordinate, differential and algorithm. Through his influence, scholars came to use the equality sign =, multiplication sign · and logical notation.

His mathematical work was not confined to analysis. He sought a universal method for mastering the sciences and a “universal language” in which all errors of thought would reveal themselves as errors of calculation. This led him to symbolic logic. He is thus regarded as a founder of mathematical logic.

Leibniz can also be seen as an intellectual pioneer of modern machine mathematics. He was among the first to construct a calculating machine capable not only of addition and subtraction but also multiplication, division, exponentiation, and extraction of square and cube roots. He spent more than 40 years improving his invention. He also invented the first integrating mechanism and stood at the very beginnings of modern computer science.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

He fully described the modern binary system in *Explication de l’Arithmétique Binaire*. Interested in Chinese culture, Leibniz knew the *Book of Changes* and noticed that its hexagrams corresponded to binary numbers from 0 to 111111. He may have been the first programmer and information theorist. He designed a binary calculating machine using a precursor of the punched card.

Leibniz introduced the concept of a determinant and proposed ideas about determinant theory, later developed by Vandermonde, Cauchy and Gauss and brought to completion by C. G. J. Jacobi.

His influence on contemporaries was enormous. He established his own mathematical school, including the Bernoulli brothers, l'Hôpital and Euler. He was also the first to break the centuries-old tradition of writing scholarly works only in Latin.

Much has been written about priority in the discovery of differential and integral calculus. It has been established that Newton and Leibniz developed their methods independently. Newton discovered his methods first, in 1665–1666; Leibniz later, in 1673–1676. Leibniz, however, published first: in 1684–1686, compared with Newton in 1704–1736.

Toward the end of his life, Newton entered a long priority dispute with Leibniz. They did not start it themselves in 1699, but soon took an active part. The bitter, partisan controversy, involving many scholars, caused both men considerable distress. Both moved far from their initially high regard for each other's achievements.

The dispute grew into an international feud, with unhappy consequences for science. English mathematicians refused to use Leibniz's algorithms, while continental mathematicians neglected the achievements of Newton's school. This continued for more than a hundred years. Their priority dispute became known as “the most disgraceful quarrel in the entire history of mathematics.”

The Newton–Leibniz formula, or fundamental theorem of calculus, relates integration to the calculation of an antiderivative. It connects differential and integral calculus. Just as those two forms of calculus are inseparable, so are these two geniuses' achievements and their relationship in history.

Denis Diderot observed in the *Encyclopédie* that Leibniz was to Germany what Plato, Aristotle and Archimedes together were to ancient Greece. Norbert Wiener said that, if asked to choose a patron saint of cybernetics, he would choose Leibniz.
