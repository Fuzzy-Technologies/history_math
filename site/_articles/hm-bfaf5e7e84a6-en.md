If anyone has ever told you, “Sundays are for rest!”, remember George Boole (1815–1864), the man whose work is why modern programmers write and, or and if rather than “well, whatever feels right”.

At sixteen he was already an assistant teacher, supporting his family after his father’s business declined.

What comes next would fit in a vintage comic. In an 1884 letter, a former pupil recalled that when Boole taught in Doncaster in the early 1830s, he read mathematics on Sundays and even solved problems in the chapel. Some families objected to his studies and his religious views. Apparently that was rather too vigorous a form of worship for the school.

Spoiler: heaven took no offence. In 1844, the Royal Society awarded Boole its Royal Medal for “On a General Method in Analysis”, published in the *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society*. He was later elected a Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS) in 1857.

His story also has a decidedly “wet” chapter: he caught a cold after arriving to lecture in rain-soaked clothes and died soon afterwards. Contemporaries recounted that his wife’s home treatment, based on “like cures like” in the spirit of the contemporary fashion for homeopathy, may only have worsened his condition.

And a final twist worthy of a novel: his youngest daughter was Ethel Lilian Voynich, author of *The Gadfly*. Her father died when she was only about six months old; the book later became immensely popular, particularly in the Soviet Union. Yes, that Voynich — and the surname was her husband Wilfrid Voynich’s, the same man whose name is familiar from the Voynich manuscript. How did cryptography get into this?

### The laws of thought

From the [MacTutor biography of George Boole](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Boole/).

Boole’s most important work is generally considered to be the book published in 1854: *An Investigation into the Laws of Thought, on Which Are Founded the Mathematical Theories of Logic and Probabilities*. The Russian title is «Исследование законов мышления, на которых основаны математические теории логики и вероятностей».

Boole approached logic in a new way, reducing it to a simple algebra and thereby bringing logic within mathematics. He pointed out the analogy between algebraic symbols and symbols representing logical forms. This was the beginning of the algebra of logic, now called Boolean algebra, which is used in computer design, switching circuits and other applications.

Boole himself understood the importance of this work. In a letter to Thomson dated 2 January 1851, he described his theory of logic and probabilities as the most valuable — perhaps the only valuable — contribution he had made or was likely to make to science, and as the work by which he hoped to be remembered.

Boole also worked on:

- Differential equations: his influential *A Treatise on Differential Equations* appeared in 1859.
- The calculus of finite differences: *A Treatise on the Calculus of Finite Differences* appeared in 1860.
- General methods in probability theory.

He published about fifty papers. In Chapter II of [*The Laws of Thought*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15114), Boole examined how algebraic laws such as distributivity apply to the symbols of logic.

### Recognition and recollections

The brilliance of his work was recognised in his lifetime. Boole received numerous honours, including honorary degrees from the universities of Dublin and Oxford, and was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1857. Yet his career, which had begun rather late, ended prematurely: he died at forty-nine. Macfarlane wrote that in 1864 Boole walked two miles from his home to the college in drenching rain and lectured in wet clothes; a feverish cold then affected his lungs and led to his death.

Macfarlane does not mention that Boole’s wife believed a remedy should resemble the cause. She put Boole to bed and poured buckets of water over the bed, because his illness had been caused by getting wet.

De Morgan praised Boole’s system of logic as evidence of genius and patience combined. He argued that the symbolic processes of algebra could express acts of thought and supply the grammar and vocabulary of an all-embracing system of logic; in Hobbes he saw only a remote glimpse of the ideas that Boole brought into the light.

Boolean algebra is widely used in the design of modern computers. Boole’s work should be seen as a fundamental step in the modern computer revolution.
