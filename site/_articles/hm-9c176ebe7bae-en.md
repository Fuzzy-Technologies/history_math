The properties of paper give rise to a distinctive geometry: the geometry of folding.

The sheet’s edges and the creases made by folding act as lines; its corners and the intersections of creases act as points.

In practice, these constructions are naturally approximate. But their steps can be described theoretically. We first need a list of the simplest basic constructions. For simplicity, we can ignore the paper’s thickness.

## Example

A triangle has been cut out of paper. Explain how to find the following lines and points by folding:

- the bisector of a given angle;
- the altitude from a given vertex, provided the angles at the other two vertices are acute;
- the median to a given side;
- the perpendicular bisector of a given side;
- the incentre;
- the circumcentre, for an acute triangle.

## Constructing the angle bisector

To bisect angle A of triangle ABC, fold the paper so that side AB lies along side AC. The crease is then the axis of symmetry of angle BAC: its bisector.

Try the other exercises yourself, then see the additional answers in Notes.

## Notes

### Additional solutions

To draw a perpendicular from A to BC—an altitude—fold the triangle along a line through A so that edge BC coincides with itself. The resulting crease is perpendicular to BC.

To construct the median to BC, first find the midpoint of that segment. Then join it to vertex A with a crease.
