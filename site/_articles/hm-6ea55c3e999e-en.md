On 8 March, International Women’s Day, one wants to recall a story not about “overcoming the odds”, but about the way knowledge can sometimes arrive almost like light through a window.

Sofya Kovalevskaya described one cause of her interest in mathematics as a “curious circumstance”. When her family moved to Palibino, there was not enough wallpaper for one of the children’s rooms. A wall was temporarily covered with sheets of M. V. Ostrogradsky’s lithographed lectures on differential and integral calculus. [An earlier post about Ostrogradsky](https://t.me/history_math/145).

And so her childhood acquired a “mysterious wall” in front of which she could spend hours:

“I remember spending whole hours in my childhood before that mysterious wall…”

The formulas made no sense to her, but their appearance lodged in her memory like a pattern. Many years later, when the fifteen-year-old Sofya took her first lesson in differential calculus, her teacher was astonished at how quickly she grasped limits and derivatives: “as if I had known them in advance”.

Sometimes a future in science begins neither with a textbook nor with a lecture, but with the irresistible wish to decipher strange signs on a wall.

Sources:

- The original passage about the “mysterious wall” and Ostrogradsky’s lectures is in [*Воспоминания детства* (Childhood Recollections, in Russian)](https://www.troykaonline.com/files/pdf/9785171776527.pdf).

- A [MacTutor biography](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Kovalevskaya) also mentions the wallpaper made from lecture sheets.

The wallpaper story appears on p. 33 of the memoirs:

> In speaking of these first encounters of mine with mathematics, I cannot fail to mention a very curious circumstance which also awakened my interest in this science.

> When we moved to the country, the whole house had to be refurbished and all the rooms newly papered. But there were so many rooms that the wallpaper ran out before one of our nurseries was finished. It had to be ordered from St Petersburg, which was quite an undertaking, and it was simply not worth doing for a single room. Everyone waited for a suitable opportunity; meanwhile, this neglected room remained for many years with one wall covered in plain paper. By a fortunate chance, the paper used for this temporary covering consisted of sheets from lithographed lectures by Ostrogradsky on differential and integral calculus, which my father had acquired in his youth.

> These sheets, covered with strange, incomprehensible formulas, soon caught my attention. I remember spending whole hours as a child before that mysterious wall, trying to make out even individual phrases and discover the order in which the sheets ought to follow one another. Through long daily contemplation, the appearance of many formulas became engraved in my memory; the text itself also left a deep impression on my mind, although I understood none of it at the time.

> Many years later, when I was already fifteen and took my first lesson in differential calculus from the well-known St Petersburg mathematics teacher Aleksandr Nikolayevich Strannolyubsky, he was surprised at how quickly I grasped and absorbed the ideas of limits and derivatives, “as if I had known them in advance”. I remember that these were precisely his words. What had happened was that, as he explained the ideas, I suddenly recalled vividly that all of this had been on those memorable sheets of Ostrogradsky’s lectures, and the very idea of a limit seemed long familiar.
