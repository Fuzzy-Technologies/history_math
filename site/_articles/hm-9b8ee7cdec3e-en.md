*Written to mark the 135th anniversary of his birth*

Antonin Ivanovich Fetisov (12 November 1891 – 21 October 1979) was a Soviet mathematics educator and specialist in teaching methods, and the author of school textbooks, methodological works and popular books on geometry. His name is familiar not through “Fetisov’s theorems”, but through a rare ability to explain what we are actually doing when we prove something.

He was born in Odoyev, in Tula Province.

His route into mathematics did not begin in a university department. He devoted much of his early life to independent study and eventually mastered the university curriculum well enough to pass examinations at Moscow State University in 1926–1928 and obtain a university diploma. From 1929 he taught at educational institutions in Moscow. At Moscow State University he lectured on methods of teaching mathematics. He taught future mathematics teachers how to explain the reasoning behind a solution.

From 1937 to 1941 he was a researcher in the mathematics unit of the State Research Institute of Schools under the People’s Commissariat for Education. During the war he taught at an artillery school in Moscow. Afterwards he was invited to the mathematics teaching methods section of the Research Institute of Teaching Methods of the RSFSR Academy of Pedagogical Sciences, where he worked until 1970.

Fetisov’s central educational idea was to rebuild school geometry around the “idea of motion”: rather than simply listing the properties of figures, pupils should see the transformations, symmetries, translations and rotations behind them — geometry as a language of actions. He developed this approach in methodological works, articles and textbooks. One textbook, intended to replace A. P. Kiselev’s classic, was judged unsuitable by specialists of the time. Yet the approach of “geometry through transformations” remained influential in teaching methods for decades.

For a wider readership, Fetisov is especially remembered for a short and precise work, *Proof in Geometry* (1954), in the *Popular Lectures in Mathematics* series. It presents geometry as a way to learn how to reason: proof is needed not to tick a box, but to distinguish justified confidence from guessing. The booklet appeared in large print runs and was translated into other languages — an unusual fate for a book on teaching methods.

His later books continued the same approach. *Essays on Euclidean and Non-Euclidean Geometry* (1965) and *Geometry in Problems* (1977) sought to keep both rigour and a clear understanding of geometric ideas in school geometry. Fetisov belonged to those teachers of science who clarify difficult ideas rather than merely simplifying them.
