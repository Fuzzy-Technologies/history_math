Paul Erdős (Hungarian: Erdős Pál, 1913–1996) was one of the most prolific mathematicians of the twentieth century. He published more than 1,400 research papers, most of them with co-authors, and became a legend in his own lifetime.

He was called an “itinerant mathematician”: he lived in hotels and colleagues’ homes, travelled from conference to conference and looked for problems wherever he went. Mathematics was his true “home”; he said that he felt at home only in the world of numbers.

Once, when a journalist asked whether he was too pessimistic, Erdős replied:

“A person lives for a short time and is dead for a long time.”

He was also known for his dependence on coffee, which he treated with mathematical humour. According to a humorous legend, someone at a conference remarked that coffee was poison. Without looking up from his formulas, Erdős replied:

“Of course it’s poison. But mathematics is the antidote!”

He later joked that he had proved the “coffee theorem”:

“If there is enough caffeine in the coffee, any theorem becomes obvious.”

(This remark has also been attributed to other mathematicians—but it caught on as an Erdős story.)
