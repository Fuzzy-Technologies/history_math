Triangles and tetrahedra are known to be rigid figures. You may know that geometry also has figures whose faces are regular polygons and which can bend. Such figures are called flexagons.

Flexagons — from English “to flex”, Latin flectere, “to fold, bend”, and Greek ωνος, “polygon” — are flat models made from paper strips that can fold and bend in particular ways. Flexing reveals surfaces previously hidden within the construction, while previously visible surfaces move inside. They conceal an interesting mathematical theory with practical applications as well.

The most commonly used flexagons are hexagonal hexaflexagons and square tetraflexagons.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The first flexagon was discovered in 1939 by the Englishman Arthur Stone, then studying mathematics at Princeton University in the United States. A “Flexagon Committee” soon formed, consisting of Stone, mathematicians Bryant Tuckerman and John Tukey, and physicist Richard Feynman. By 1940, they had developed flexagon theory.

Flexagons became popular after Martin Gardner’s Mathematical Games column on hexaflexagons appeared in Scientific American (1956).

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Their names follow this pattern: a prefix for the number of surfaces, a prefix for the shape, and “flexagon”. The first prefix thus indicates how many surfaces can eventually be revealed; the second indicates how many parts each surface contains. A trihexaflexagon, for example, has three surfaces, each comprising six equilateral triangles: Stone’s flexagon.

Flexagons have repeatedly been patented as toys but have not achieved widespread commercial adoption.

## How to fold a flexagon {#section-1}

A classical flexagon can be folded from a straight strip of paper. Templates and assembly instructions are available online through a search such as “three-face flexagon”. The results include templates with different pattern combinations. You can also print a blank template and colour the flexagon as you wish.

**Folding a trihexaflexagon**

Use a strip of fairly stiff paper about 3–4 cm wide.

First mark out 10 equilateral triangles (а).

Fold the strip along ab and turn it over (б).

Fold again along cd and arrange the ends so that the penultimate triangle overlaps the first (в).

Fold the last triangle down and attach it to the reverse of the first triangle (г).
