90 years ago, in December 1934, Nicolas Bourbaki was born. He died in November 1968, just one month short of his 34th birthday, leaving some 70 monographs on fundamental mathematical questions. “Bourbaki” is the collective pseudonym of a group of French mathematicians established in 1935.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The founders present at its first meeting were Henri Cartan, Claude Chevalley, Jean Delsarte, Jean Dieudonné, René de Possel, Szolem Mandelbrojt and André Weil. They undertook to reconsider all mathematics from its foundations.

The German mathematical school greatly influenced the group: D. Hilbert, H. Weyl, J. von Neumann, and especially the algebraists E. Noether, E. Artin and B. L. van der Waerden.

Many outstanding mathematicians participated at various times: Laurent Schwartz, Jean-Pierre Serre, Alexander Grothendieck, Samuel Eilenberg, Serge Lang, Armand Borel and Pierre Cartier. The group’s exact membership and size were always kept secret.

Its official name is Association des collaborateurs de Nicolas Bourbaki, the “Association of Collaborators of Nicolas Bourbaki”. Graduates of Paris’s École Normale Supérieure formed it at that institution. The pseudonym came from General Charles Denis Bourbaki, well known in Nancy, who commanded armies and corps during the Franco-Prussian War. One condition of membership was an age no greater than 50.

Bourbaki aims to create a fully self-contained interpretation of the mathematics of its time, an idea of D. Hilbert’s. Since 1939, it has published a collective treatise under the single title Éléments de mathématique, “Elements of Mathematics”, or more precisely “Foundations of Mathematics”. The Bourbaki Seminar operates alongside it, hearing talks by scientists from different countries as well as preparing the treatise.

Bourbaki’s books use a rigorous axiomatic style and present mathematics as a closed system based on Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory as developed by Bernays and Gödel. Their exposition rests on axiomatically defined structures, such as order structures, groups and topological structures. Volumes published over the years include *Set Theory*, *Algebra*, *Topology*, *Functions of a Real Variable*, *Integration*, *Commutative Algebra*, *Lie Groups and Lie Algebras*, and others. All have been translated into Russian.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Bourbaki’s books first introduced the empty-set symbol Ø; the symbols N, Z, Q, R, C for the natural, integer, rational, real and complex numbers respectively; and the terms injection, surjection and bijection.

Bourbaki’s influence on world mathematics was enormous in France, strong in Belgium, Switzerland and Italy, fairly substantial in the United States, and less substantial in England and Germany. Attitudes in the USSR were sceptical.

Contemporary mathematicians often criticize the approach of Bourbaki’s books, now called “Bourbakism”, accusing it of excessive formalization and “destroying the spirit of mathematics”. Nevertheless, the books have significantly influenced modern mathematics, and the mathematical community unquestionably recognizes the authority of the group’s members.

The Bourbaki group entered a crisis in the late 60s. Discord arose among members; some left, while others gave less attention to collective work. Scholars of lower standing began speaking at the Bourbaki Seminar. The group has since become active again. In 2022, for example, it published chapters of *Spectral Theory*.

The term “Bourbakization of education” describes developments in scholarship in the 50–60s of the last century. Part of the world mathematical community came under the treatise’s influence, affecting mathematical writing styles generally. Bourbakists prioritized complete formalization based on algebraic structures even in school mathematics, from which virtually all real geometry was expelled.

We also recommend N. Bourbaki’s *Essays on the History of Mathematics*, which presents the group’s understanding of mathematics’ development.
