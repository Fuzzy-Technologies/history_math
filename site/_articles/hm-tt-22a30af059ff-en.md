Bibikov's agrometer is a ruler that measures the area of a triangle on a drawing in a single operation.

Usually, to determine a triangle's area from a drawing or model, we first measure some of its elements: sides, angles or altitudes.

This raises a question: can we find a segment in a triangle whose length is numerically equal to the triangle's area?

In the 1840s the Russian mathematician Bibikov showed that this was easily done. He devised a simple instrument and called it an agrometer. He believed that it would become a reliable aid to land surveyors. Vitkovsky described the instrument in 1904.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Its construction is very simple: an ordinary ruler with centimetre and millimetre divisions, but exactly 2 cm wide. The agrometer uses the idea of replacing the triangle whose area is required with an equal-area triangle having an altitude of 2 linear units.

Suppose we need to measure the area of ∆ABC, as in the diagram above. Through vertex C, draw a line CK parallel to the triangle's base, using whatever is available, such as squared paper or transparent graph paper. Then place the agrometer on the triangle so that the ruler's zero coincides with point A and point B lies on its opposite edge. See which division is intersected by line CK; call this point K. The length AK is numerically equal to the required area of ∆ABC in square units, because ∆ABC and ∆ABK have equal areas. They share the base AB, and their altitudes equal the distance between the parallel lines.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

A similar description appears in S. M. Solovyov's *A Short Course in Elementary Surveying* (1909).

There is also an electronic instrument sold under the same name, Agrometer, with a high-precision system for measuring field areas and perimeters.
