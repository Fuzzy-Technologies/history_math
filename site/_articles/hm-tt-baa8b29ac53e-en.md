Kayum Nasyri was an outstanding educator of the Tatar people, an ethnographer, writer and founder of the Tatar literary language. His life and educational work allow us to trace many processes and trends accompanying the integration of different peoples into the Russian state education system.

Nasyri was born on 2 (14) February 1825 in the village of Verkhniye Shirdany, Sviyazhsk District, Kazan Governorate, now in Zelenodolsk District. He learned basic literacy in the village maktab. In 1841, he went to Kazan and studied at the madrasa of the fifth congregational mosque under the progressive imam Ahmed ash-Shirdani. He mastered Turkish, Arabic and Persian, as well as the foundations of Islamic philosophy and law.

He always showed a strong interest in learning Russian. He took a position teaching Tatar at the Kazan Theological School, then at the Kazan Theological Seminary.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

At that time, public opinion among Kazan Tatars disapproved of devout Muslims cooperating with Orthodox Christianity in education. Muslims often called him “Urys Kayum”, or “Russian Kayum”. Centuries-old prejudices rooted in fear of Christianization made the young teacher an outcast among his fellow believers. He lived in a tiny attic room in the seminary building, staying up late over Eastern manuscripts, Russian and European literature, and notes and drafts of his first works.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

In the nineteenth century, the formation of the Tatar nation and its literary language, together with growing entrepreneurship, brought a new stage in education, including mathematics. Concerned by the autonomy of Muslim religious education, the tsarist government adopted measures to integrate it into the Russian state system. These included compulsory Russian-language teaching for shakirds, or students, in maktabs and madrasas, discussed in our [article Maktabs and Madrasas](https://teletype.in/Mektebe-i-medrese-02-24). This was the period of the emerging Tatar national school and the struggle to democratize it through secular education and teaching in the native language. These changes met resistance from the clergy and Muslim population, although the emerging Tatar bourgeoisie and merchant class favoured school reform.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

The only Muslim in Kazan capable of productive teaching work in the new Tatar schools was Kayum Nasyri. In 1871, he organized the first Russian-Tatar school in Kazan, near the Marjani Mosque, and began teaching Tatar children Russian and mathematics. He wrote the first Tatar-language mathematics textbooks: *Arithmetic*, or *Hisaplyk* (1873), and *The Science of Geometry*, or *Istyylahat gyylme həndəsə* (1895). They drew on Russian school experience and progressive educational ideas. He was the first to introduce the class-and-lesson system in the Tatar school.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

For Ministry of Public Education officials, however, he was too independent a teacher and unwilling to undertake missionary work. His conflict with the inspector of Tatar schools, V. V. Radlov, went so far that in 1876 Nasyri was forced to give up teaching.

In preparing his arithmetic textbook, Nasyri studied and used traditional mathematical knowledge: measures of weight, length and time, comparing them with common standard measures. He included entertaining problems designed to engage children's imaginative thinking. The book defines the subject of arithmetic, indicates its practical uses, and gives theoretical principles and teaching advice. He was the first to introduce Indian numerals, familiarized readers with the Russian number system, and skilfully compared Arabic and Russian terminology. The terminology he created became the basis of the Tatar people's scientific, technical, popular and literary writing. He devoted more than 35 years to education, beginning as an ordinary teacher and becoming a leading educational scholar.

Solitude and a peaceful life in lodgings with the muezzin of the Galeyev Mosque on Sennaya Street, now 35 Paris Commune Street, helped him undertake serious research. He produced important works on philology, history, ethnography, education, teaching methods, astronomy, geography, physiology and agriculture. Before him, the Tatars had no scientific or popular scientific literature. He published his own calendar from 1871. It included not just dates but educational information, and was consequently called encyclopedic. Other works include the popular *Bush vakyt*, or *At Leisure* (1860); the Russian-Tatar *Lögat kitaby*, or *Dictionary* (1892); the three-part textbook *Ysul jəghrafiyai kabir*, or *Great Geography* (1894–1899); and the first explanatory dictionary of Tatar, *Lahjəi tatari*, or *Tatar Dialects*, in two volumes (1895-1896). In 1885, he was elected a full member of Kazan University's Society of Archaeology, History and Ethnography. His volume of sermons and counsels, *Fruits of Conversation*, issued by the university printing house in 1884, expresses the entire Tatar educational ideology and has considerable historical value.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

Nasyri produced more than 40 published works. He translated many entertaining and popular scientific books from Russian and Eastern languages into Tatar. Particularly popular, including among children, was the fantastical tale of the famous scholar, philosopher and poet Avicenna, *Abugalisina* (1881).

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-8" %}

Kayum Nasyri became a true idol for the young Tatar intelligentsia of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries: Gabdulla Tukay, Fatikh Amirkhan, Galimjan Ibragimov, Galiaskar Kamal and others who stood at the origins of professional national literature, theatre, art and science.

He was buried in the cemetery of Kazan's New Tatar Quarter. A street in the Old Tatar Quarter bears his name. On 2 February 2002, a museum-estate opened in the house where he lived on Sennaya Street, now 35 Paris Commune Street. The USSR issued a commemorative postage stamp in 1952.
