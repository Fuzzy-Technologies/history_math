*Marking the 555th anniversary of his birth*

Albrecht Dürer (1471–1528) was a German painter and printmaker of the Northern Renaissance, best remembered for his portraits and engravings. Yet he had another passion: geometry, including perspective, proportion, constructions, shapes and measurement.

## Precise Lines and Geometry

He was born in Nuremberg on 21 May 1471. The precision of workshop practice—his father was a goldsmith—taught him early to work carefully with form and line. Travel and contact with scholars and humanists deepened his interest in the mathematics of art: how to make an image convincing and a construction verifiable.

He wanted to understand why a picture works, as well as make it beautiful. He studied perspective and proportion as a language that could lend the same rigour to a painting and a technical drawing. His sheets bring workshop practice together with pure geometry: grids, measuring instruments, construction diagrams and polyhedra.

He also thought beyond the individual work of art, working for print as well as display. Engraving and woodcut made it possible to reproduce images and spread ideas. An artist accustomed to the precise line of a graver could readily turn a drawing into a diagram, and a diagram into a teaching sheet.

## Treatises on Measurement, Space and Proportion

In 1525 he published *Instruction in Measurement with Compass and Straightedge* (*Underweysung der Messung mit dem Zirckel und Richtscheyt*). This was practical geometry for artists and craftspeople, written in German rather than Latin so that a working craftsperson could read it. It covers lines and planes, polygon constructions, curves and solids, and ways to translate the three-dimensional world onto a flat surface.

The book contains famous images presenting perspective as a technique: an artist looks at an object through a frame or grid, fixes the directions of rays and transfers the form to paper. Here a description of the method opens up what had seemed a secret of artistic skill.

Two years later came *Instruction on the Fortification of Towns, Castles and Strongholds* (1527). Geometry directly serves engineering and defence: laying out plans, reasoning about lines and angles, and thinking about space at scale without sacrificing precision.

*Four Books on Human Proportion* appeared after his death in 1528. It sought to discuss beauty through relationships, measurement and variations in form as well as taste. Dürer assembled observations, compared proportions and showed that the ideal need not be unique, but depends on the task and its setting.

## Melencolia I and the Culture of Measurement

His celebrated engraving *Melencolia I* (1514) became a symbol of the union of art and mathematics. Beside a pensive figure are dividers, measuring tools, a mysterious polyhedron often called Dürer’s solid, and a magic square whose rows and columns have equal sums. He seems to suggest that inspiration needs measure as well as flight.

Today we might say that he built an interface between the eye and number. Where a modern designer or engineer opens a 3D model, he sets up a grid and dividers. Where we trust calculation, he teaches us to test a construction by hand. The habit of measuring, constructing and checking keeps his treatises alive five centuries later.

He died in Nuremberg on 6 April 1528. His books and prints still remind us of something simple: mathematics is especially beautiful when it becomes a practice that helps people see the world more precisely. An artist armed with dividers can be every bit as compelling as one holding a brush.

Dürer’s *Melencolia I* is one of the three major prints from 1513–1514 collectively known as the *Meisterstiche*, or master engravings. The others are *Knight, Death, and the Devil* and *Saint Jerome in His Study*. They do not constitute a series, though they have been related to the moral, theological and intellectual virtues of medieval scholasticism, reflecting the complexity of his thought and his period.
