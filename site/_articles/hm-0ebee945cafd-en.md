*Marking the 170th anniversary of his birth*

Thomas Joannes Stieltjes (29 December 1856–31 December 1894) was a Dutch mathematician who taught analysis to speak the language of limits while keeping structure behind every calculation. Today his name is most often associated with the Riemann–Stieltjes integral and moment problems. Behind those terms lies a remarkably concentrated life: a path into research without a university degree, leading nevertheless to work of the highest quality.

## An Independent Path into Mathematics

He was born in Zwolle into a family with an established reputation. His father, also Thomas Stieltjes, was a well-known hydraulic engineer and politician. In 1873 the younger Stieltjes entered the Polytechnic School in Delft, but soon adopted an unusual strategy for a student: instead of following courses step by step, he chose the library, Gauss, Jacobi and independent reading. The result was predictable: he failed examination after examination, and the conventional academic route closed to him.

A way forward came through Leiden Observatory, where in 1877 he became an “assistant for astronomical calculations”. Within its disciplined routine of calculation, his mathematical imagination paradoxically flourished. In 1882 he began corresponding with Charles Hermite. Their letters became a workshop for research as well as an exchange of ideas. The observatory’s director supported him: from 1883 Stieltjes was excused from observations and could devote himself almost entirely to mathematics.

In 1884 came an episode worthy of a novel about academic bureaucracy. He was offered a professorship in Groningen, but was then turned down because he lacked a degree. The mathematical community responded promptly: that same year Leiden University awarded him a doctorate honoris causa. Paris followed, then a doctoral defence in 1886, and eventually a chair in Toulouse, where he became professor of differential and integral calculus.

## Continued Fractions and a New Integral

Stieltjes’s greatest achievement was his study of continued fractions and the analytical constructions connected with them. In the major paper *Recherches sur les fractions continues* (*Investigations on Continued Fractions*, 1894–1895), he introduced the Stieltjes integral: integration with respect to an increasing function, naturally bringing sums, jumps and continuous parts into one formula. This was an important step towards measure theory and the Lebesgue integral, made within the classical nineteenth-century tradition of analysis. Alongside it stand his results on moment problems, orthogonal polynomials and a wide range of questions in complex analysis, where continued fractions provide a subtle tool for approximation and analytic continuation.

Stieltjes lived for only thirty-eight years. One detail is particularly poignant: recognition arrived in a final letter, when a few days before his death he learned that the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences had elected him a corresponding member. In mathematics, however, his life has lasted well over a century. His name returns whenever we learn to replace formulas with concepts without losing computational power.
