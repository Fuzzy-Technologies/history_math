*Marking the 475th anniversary of his birth*

> “Having become convinced that nothing else … caused greater difficulties in mathematical practice, or hindered and troubled calculators more than multiplication, division and the extraction of square and cube roots—operations which, besides consuming time in tedious labour, are the main source of numerous errors—I began to consider what reliable and easy means might remove these obstacles. Considering various ways to achieve this, I finally found remarkable short rules that could be used thereafter. Among all these rules, none are more useful than those which … remove from calculations the numbers to be multiplied, divided or reduced to roots and replace them with other numbers by which all calculations can be performed through addition, subtraction or division by two or three alone.”
> — John Napier

During the Renaissance, calculation reached new levels of sophistication. Growing demand for engineers required improvements in the mathematical tools of computation. After the decimal fractions of the engineer and mathematician Simon Stevin, the invention of logarithms was a major advance. The term proposed by Napier became established in mathematics, from the Greek λόγος, “ratio”, and ἀριθμός, “number”.

## Life and Interests

Lord John Napier was a distinguished Scottish mathematician, astronomer, inventor and theologian. He was one of the inventors of logarithms and the first to publish logarithmic tables.

He was born on 1 February 1550 at Merchiston Castle, then outside Edinburgh in the Kingdom of Scotland.

In December 1563 his father decided to send the thirteen-year-old John to the University of St Andrews. During this period Napier travelled through Germany, France and Italy. Historians suggest that he continued his education during these travels.

He returned home in 1571, settled in his ancestral castle and never again left Scotland.

A devout Puritan, Napier devoted all his time to theology, astrology and the mathematical calculations associated with them.

## The Idea of Logarithms and the First Tables

The need for complex calculations grew rapidly in the sixteenth century. Much of the difficulty lay in multiplying and dividing numbers with many digits. While carrying out trigonometric calculations, Napier conceived the idea of replacing laborious multiplication with simple addition by using special tables to relate a geometric progression to an arithmetic progression.

His central idea was to construct two sequences linked so that, as one increased arithmetically, the other decreased geometrically. The product of two numbers in the second sequence was simply related to the sum of the corresponding numbers in the first, allowing multiplication to be reduced to addition. This system greatly simplified calculations involving sines.

In 1614 Napier published *A Description of the Wonderful Canon of Logarithms*, Latin *Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio*, in Edinburgh. It briefly described logarithms and their properties and provided seven-place logarithmic tables of sines, cosines and tangents for angles from $0^\circ$ to $90^\circ$ at intervals of $1'$. His tables contained errors beyond the sixth digit.

## Briggs and Later Developments

In 1615 Henry Briggs, then professor of mathematics at Gresham College in London, visited Napier. Napier was already ill and could not improve his tables, but advised Briggs to modify the definition of a logarithm to bring it closer to the modern one. Briggs published his tables in 1617, the year of Napier’s death. They listed the common logarithms of the integers from 1 to 1000 to fourteen places. Errors were found in Briggs’s tables too. The first error-free edition based on Georg Vega’s tables appeared only in 1857 in Berlin.

The modern definition of logarithms as the inverse operation of exponentiation first appeared in John Wallis and Johann Bernoulli, and was finally established by Leonhard Euler in the eighteenth century.

Napier’s ingenious device for rapid multiplication, known as Napier’s bones, also became popular.

## Notes

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Napier’s work is divided into two books. The first concerns logarithms; the second treats plane and spherical trigonometry and also serves as a practical guide to the first. A fuller explanation appeared in another work published after his death by his son, in which Napier explained how he constructed the tables. The concept of a function did not yet exist, so he defined logarithms kinematically by comparing uniform and decelerating motion. In modern notation his model can be represented by the differential equation $\mathrm{d}x/x = -\mathrm{d}y/M$, where $M$ is a scale factor introduced to give an integer value with the required number of digits. Decimal fractions were not yet widely used. Napier chose $M = 10\,000\,000$.

All the values in Napier’s table turned out to contain calculation errors beyond the sixth digit. This did not prevent the new method from becoming enormously popular. Many European mathematicians, including Kepler, began constructing and refining logarithmic tables. Napier’s book was reprinted five times and translated into many languages.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Besides mathematics, Napier studied astronomy, astrology and theology. His interpretation of the Apocalypse, *A Plaine Discovery of the Whole Revelation of S. John*, appeared in Edinburgh in 1593.

It is written in mathematical form, with the contents divided into theorems and proofs. Napier’s proof placed the end of the world between 1688 and 1700. The book was far more successful than all his scientific works.
