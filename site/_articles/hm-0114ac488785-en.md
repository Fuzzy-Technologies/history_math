The best-known sum of two squares is the sum of the squared legs of a right triangle.

Which integers can be represented as a sum of two squares? This is a classical question in number theory. In the seventeenth century, Pierre de Fermat formulated an important special result: every prime of the form $4k+1$ is a sum of two squares.

A remarkable algebraic identity helps us investigate such sums and has many other mathematical uses. A product of two sums of squares can itself be written as a sum of squares. Two formulas give such a representation, and their results can coincide:

$(a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2)$<br>
$=(ac-bd)^2+(ad+bc)^2$<br>
$=(ac+bd)^2+(ad-bc)^2$.

## Example

$(1^2+2^2)(3^2+4^2)=125=5^2+10^2=11^2+2^2$.

This is called the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity, or Diophantus’s identity. Diophantus uses a numerical case in [*Arithmetica*](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~cbruni/CO480Resources/references/CO480MayAug2017/arithmetica.pdf), III.19 (T. Heath’s translation, pp. 166–167). Brahmagupta gives a more general composition rule in the [*Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/web_material/Circulars/2012/68_KTPI/Module_7.pdf) (628, verses 18.64–65). In modern notation, the sum-of-squares identity is the case with coefficient −1. Fibonacci uses both formulas in Proposition 6 of his [*Book of Squares*](https://archive.org/stream/bub_gb_JYb-VYM12ocC/bub_gb_JYb-VYM12ocC_djvu.txt) (*Liber quadratorum*, 1225, B. Boncompagni’s edition, vol. II, pp. 257–260).

Try representing the following product of sums of squares:

$(5^2+6^2)(7^2+8^2)$.

## Notes

How does the identity arise in complex-number theory? Let $a+bi$ and $c+di$ be complex numbers. Then the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity is equivalent to the familiar multiplicative property of the complex modulus:

$\lvert a+bi\rvert\lvert c+di\rvert=\lvert(a+bi)(c+di)\rvert$.

Indeed, squaring both sides gives:

$(a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2)=(ac-bd)^2+(ad+bc)^2$.

Here is another application of the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity. Fermat’s remarkable “Christmas theorem” states that every prime of the form $4k+1$ can be represented as a sum of two squares.

Fermat stated the theorem in a [letter to Marin Mersenne](https://archive.org/stream/oeuvresdefermat02ferm/oeuvresdefermat02ferm_djvu.txt) dated 25 December 1640 (*Œuvres de Fermat*, vol. II, letter XLV, p. 213). Many proofs have appeared since.
