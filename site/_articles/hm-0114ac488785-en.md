The best-known sum of two squares is the sum of the squared legs of a right triangle.

Which integers can be represented as a sum of two squares? This is a classical question in number theory. In the seventeenth century, Pierre de Fermat formulated an important special result: every prime of the form $4k+1$ is a sum of two squares.

A remarkable algebraic identity helps us investigate such sums and has many other mathematical uses. A product of two sums of squares can itself be written as a sum of squares—in two ways:

$(a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2)$<br>
$=(ac-bd)^2+(ad+bc)^2$<br>
$=(ac+bd)^2+(ad-bc)^2$.

## Example

$(1^2+2^2)(3^2+4^2)=125=5^2+10^2=11^2+2^2$.

This is called the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity, or Diophantus’s identity. Versions of it occur in Diophantus of Alexandria’s *Arithmetica*. In the seventh century the Indian mathematician and astronomer Brahmagupta described it in his *Brahmasphutasiddhanta* (“Improved Teaching of Brahma”, 628). In Europe it appears, among other works, in Fibonacci’s *Book of Squares*, *Liber quadratorum* (1225).

Try representing the following product of sums of squares:

$(5^2+6^2)(7^2+8^2)$.

## Notes

How does the identity arise in complex-number theory? Let $a+bi$ and $c+di$ be complex numbers. Then the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity is equivalent to the familiar multiplicative property of the complex modulus:

$\lvert a+bi\rvert\lvert c+di\rvert=\lvert(a+bi)(c+di)\rvert$.

Indeed, squaring both sides gives:

$(a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2)=(ac-bd)^2+(ad+bc)^2$.

Here is another application of the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity. Fermat’s remarkable “Christmas theorem” states that every prime of the form $4k+1$ can be represented as a sum of two squares.

Since Christmas 1640, when Fermat announced that he had proved the theorem—hence its name—dozens of different proofs have been found.
