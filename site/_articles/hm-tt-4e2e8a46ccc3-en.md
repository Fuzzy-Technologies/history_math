The quadrant is an astronomical angle-measuring instrument used to measure the altitudes (zenith distances) of celestial bodies and the angular distances between them. It was an early predecessor of the sextant. Its name derives from the word “quadrant”, from the Latin *quadrantis*, meaning “a fourth part” or “a quarter”.

A quadrant consists of a quarter-circle whose arc is divided into degrees and fractions of a degree. It was usually mounted in a vertical plane. A rule fitted with sights, or a telescope, rotated about an axis passing through the centre of the circle and perpendicular to its plane. The zero point, from which readings were taken, was established with a plumb line whose weight rested in a vessel of water or oil. The position of the alidade or telescope, when aimed at the object under observation, was read using a vernier scale.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Portable quadrants mounted on tripods were made for travelling astronomers. For permanent observatories, mural quadrants were fixed to the building's stone walls in the plane of the meridian.

The mural quadrant was one of the most important observing instruments in pre-optical astronomy. In the Islamic world, the largest were the mural quadrants of al-Biruni (R = 7.5 m), Nasir al-Din al-Tusi at the Maragha observatory (R = 6.5 m), and the giant instrument at Ulugh Beg's observatory in Samarkand (R = 40 m). These instruments provided the greatest measuring precision of their time. The instrument fell out of use in the late seventeenth century.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

A quadrant was also used in artillery as an instrument for laying a gun. It served to check the sights and measure the angle through which the barrel needed to be elevated or depressed. The Italian mathematician Niccolò Tartaglia's *La nova scientia* (1537) first describes the use of the gunner's quadrant that he invented.
