*Marking the 245th anniversary of his birth*

Bernard Bolzano (5 October 1781–18 December 1848) was a Bohemian mathematician, logician and philosopher from Prague, a Catholic priest and a persistent advocate of complete mathematical rigour. Many of his ideas were ahead of their time and became widely recognized only in the second half of the nineteenth century.

## The University of Prague

He was born in Prague and attended Charles University, studying philosophy, physics and mathematics before completing a course in theology. In 1805 Bolzano was ordained a priest and soon became a professor at the University of Prague. He quickly gained a reputation as an accomplished lecturer and independent thinker.

His university career was short-lived. His public criticism of militarism, war and social injustice antagonized the authorities and conservative circles. He was dismissed in 1819 and thereafter lived without a university chair, concentrating on philosophy, logic and mathematics.

## Rigour, Numbers and Infinity

Much of Bolzano’s mathematical reputation rests on his 1817 work *Purely Analytic Proof…*. It insists on a fundamental principle: statements in analysis should be proved through rigorous reasoning, independently of geometrical obviousness. This logically disciplined attention to definitions became one of the strands from which the culture of modern analysis later developed.

Another major concern was the foundations of real numbers. In manuscripts from the 1830s Bolzano introduced and investigated measurable numbers, seeking an arithmetical basis for convergence. Today this is often regarded as an early approach to a rigorous construction of the real line, long before the classical formulations of the later nineteenth century.

Bolzano is also important in the history of infinity. His posthumously published *Paradoxes of the Infinite* examines infinite collections and carefully shows how paradoxes can arise from unclear definitions rather than from nature itself.

The central meaning of his legacy lies in disciplined thought. Bolzano demanded that a mathematical statement rest on concepts and proof rather than habit and intuition. That is why his writings can still be read as lessons in intellectual honesty.
