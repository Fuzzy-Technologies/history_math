In practice, people often have to divide objects into several parts according to some principle. Homogeneous objects can be divided equally when their quantity and quality allow it: a batch of pies for everyone, a pile of coal or sand, or coins of the same denomination. Sometimes, however, the objects are heterogeneous, or their number does not permit equal division: a single cake or watermelon for everyone, household goods or clothing. In these cases the theory of fair division can help.

By fair division we mean a division in which everyone concerned is satisfied with the share they receive.

There is no general theory of fair division suitable for every case. We will describe only a few typical practical problems and use them to try to understand the general mathematical theory, as well as outline a common algorithm that can generalize these cases.

**Suppose the participants are dividing identical objects among themselves.** Fair division then coincides with division into equal parts. For example, 20 identical sweets must be divided among 5 children. The division is fair if each child receives 4 sweets. The same principle applies to any objects that can be cut and measured precisely.

**If the divisible objects cannot be measured**, at least at the time of division, other algorithms are needed. For example, a cake must be divided fairly among several people. There is a clear procedure for doing this.

When two people divide a cake, one cuts and the other chooses. Neither partner can regard themselves as disadvantaged. The first secures their share by cutting the cake into parts that seem equal in value to them. The second at least avoids short-changing themselves by choosing the better share.

When a cake must be divided among three or more partners, proceed as follows. We will explain it for five people, labelled A, B, C, D, E; the same approach works for other numbers of partners.

А cuts a portion that they consider to be 1/5 of the cake. В may reduce this portion if they wish. The reduced or original portion then passes to С, who may also reduce it or leave it unchanged, and so on. In this way, every participant exercises their right to reduce the first portion or leave it as it is. Finally, the portion goes to the participant who last cut some of it off, and that person leaves the division. The remaining cake is divided among the remaining participants by the same principle. One participant leaves at each stage. Eventually two remain, and they divide the remaining cake by the rule “one cuts, the other chooses”.

With this procedure, each participant receives a share that satisfies them, because they chose it themselves.

> The general rule for division among n participants is also clear: at each stage, the person starting must cut a portion that they regard as 1/n of the cake. If a participant is not the starter and considers the portion cut by the previous partner larger than 1/n, they must reduce it to what they regard as 1/n. If they judge the portion to be 1/n or less, they should leave it unchanged. This rule allows each participant to receive at least the share due to them by their own assessment.

The same procedure can divide, for example, a pile of coal or sand, fruit and vegetables, or even liquids.

**It can also be used to play a game.** The players contribute equal sums in identical coins. The pile of money is placed within a sheet of paper on a table and then divided. Small rakes or forks replace the knife. Coins may be moved only with a single stroke of the rake. The person with greater manual dexterity and a good eye often wins.
