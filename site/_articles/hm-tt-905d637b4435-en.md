> “In general, the subject matter of mathematics may include any forms and relations in reality that are objectively independent of their content to such a degree that they can be abstracted from it completely.”  
> Academician A. D. Alexandrov

In the history of mathematics, it is customary to refer to the definition of its subject matter and the division of its history into periods given by A. N. Kolmogorov (1903–1987). He believed that an adequate formal definition of the subject of mathematics was impossible, and defined it through its history.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

His famous article “Mathematics” in the *Great Soviet Encyclopaedia* (1954) begins with the definition given by F. Engels (1820–1895) in *Anti-Dühring* (1877): *“Pure mathematics has as its object the spatial forms and quantitative relations of the real world, and therefore very real material.”*

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

At the same time, Kolmogorov emphasises that the range of quantitative relations and spatial forms studied by mathematics continually expands in response to the needs of the natural sciences, giving this definition an ever richer content. Additions and refinements intended to characterise modern mathematics have repeatedly been proposed.

For example, it has been contrasted with N. Bourbaki's definition: *“Mathematics appears as a collection of abstract forms: mathematical structures.”*

In the view of many scholars, including V. I. Arnold and L. D. Kudryavtsev, the subject matter of mathematics consists of models. Mathematics is understood as a field of human knowledge that studies mathematical models: logical structures in which certain relations between the elements have been specified.

In contemporary philosophy of science, a distinction is commonly drawn between the object of mathematics and its subject matter. F. Engels defined its object: spatial forms and quantitative relations. N. Bourbaki, V. I. Arnold and others characterised its subject matter: models.

We believe that it is impossible to give a complete, exhaustive and final definition of mathematics for all time. Any definition would place mathematics within certain boundaries, but the science may expand those boundaries, and mathematics would then need a new definition.
