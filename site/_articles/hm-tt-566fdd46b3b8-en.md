Goats offer a simple way to explain a **geometric locus**. A mathematical goat is ideal: it has no size. Its appetite is infinite, and it eats everything it can reach.

Suppose a goat is tethered to a stake. It will eat the grass inside a disk whose centre is the stake and whose radius equals the rope's length. This disk is the locus of points whose distance from the centre is less than or equal to the radius.

Now attach the goat to two stakes with a rope and a ring that slides along it. The region in which the goat eats the grass will be elliptical. Its boundary has the property that the sum of the distances from any point on it to the stakes equals the rope's length. Such a curve is called an ellipse.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The concept of a locus was once part of school geometry. The specific Russian term *geometricheskoye mesto tochek*, literally “geometric place of points,” appeared in Russian mathematical literature in the nineteenth century. The method of loci for solving construction problems is discussed in detail in geometry books from different periods, such as I. I. Alexandrov's *A Collection of Geometrical Construction Problems* (1950). It appeared in earlier school textbooks by L. S. Atanasyan and A. V. Pogorelov. For various reasons, however, the term gradually fell out of active use.

A geometric locus is the set of points in a plane or in space possessing a specified defining property. In other words, every point of the locus, and only those points, must have that property. English-language literature uses the analogous Latin term *locus*, meaning “place.”

A locus is sometimes described as a turn of phrase for defining a geometrical figure as the set of points with a particular property. We can say that a locus is the “maximal” set of points in the plane satisfying a problem's condition. In practice, problems asking for a locus are sometimes connected with compass-and-straightedge constructions.

Here are the main plane loci from school geometry.

• The locus of points at a fixed distance from a given point is a circle.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

• The locus of points equidistant from the endpoints of a segment, or from two points, is the segment's perpendicular bisector.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

• The locus of points inside an angle and equidistant from its sides is its angle bisector.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

• The locus of points equidistant from two intersecting lines consists of two perpendicular lines: the bisectors of the angles formed by the given lines.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

• The locus of points at distance d from a given line is a pair of parallel lines.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

• The locus of points from which a given segment subtends a specified angle consists of two circular arcs of equal radius sharing that segment as a chord, with the segment's endpoints excluded. For a right angle, this becomes one circle with the diameter's endpoints removed.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-8" %}

More complicated loci also exist. A locus with two properties is the intersection of the figure having the first property and the figure having the second. Loci in space are defined similarly. For example, the locus of points at a fixed distance from a given point in space is a sphere.

Here are further examples**\***, from analytic geometry rather than the school curriculum.

• A **parabola** is the locus of points whose distance from a given point, the focus, equals their distance from a given line, the directrix.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-9" %}

• A **hyperbola** is the locus of points for which the absolute difference between their distances from two specified points, the foci, is constant.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-10" %}

• An **ellipse** is the locus of points in the plane for which the sum of the distances from two fixed points, the foci, is a constant greater than the distance between the foci.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-11" %}

That is why the mathematical goat eats the grass in an elliptical region!
