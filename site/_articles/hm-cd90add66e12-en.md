The English mathematics populariser Henry Dudeney (1857–1930) kept his sense of humour even in difficult situations. Once, while out for a walk, he tripped over the lead of his dog, whose unusual name, Chance, had a distinctly mathematical flavour. Having broken his arm, Dudeney quoted Alexander Pope: “Chance is but direction which thou canst not see.”

## Notes

Dudeney’s daughter recalled the incident and the quotation in Martin Gardner’s introduction to [*Puzzles and Curious Problems*](https://jnsilva.ludicum.org/HMR13_14/536.pdf) (1967). The line is from Alexander Pope’s *An Essay on Man*, Epistle I.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

[Henry Ernest Dudeney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Dudeney) (1857–1930) was an English mathematician and science populariser, a world-famous creator of puzzles, some of which prompted serious mathematical research. He first published puzzles in articles he sent to newspapers and magazines, often under the pseudonym “Sphinx”. In 1893 Dudeney began corresponding and exchanging puzzles with the American puzzle-maker Sam Loyd. Dudeney later became angry when Loyd published puzzles sent to him under his own name.

We discussed one of Dudeney’s problems here: [the Round Table problem](https://t.me/history_math/452) (in Russian).
