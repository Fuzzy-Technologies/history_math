**We are beginning a series entitled “Historical and Methodological Learning Situations and Problems”, abbreviated УИМЗ in Russian. These problems turn a learning situation into a concrete task. They contain a historical mathematical context and a system of exercises.**

If someone successfully completes these exercises, we can regard them as having understood the learning situation. The educational ideas built into these problems will expand a mathematics teacher’s repertoire of methods. Unexpected connections between different situations encountered in practice may also interest and benefit anyone interested in mathematics.

## A learning situation: “Large Numbers” {#section-1}

In everyday life, we sometimes encounter the names of large numbers. Imagine a child asking what a “trillion” is and why it has that name. How would you explain it? Is there a pattern in the names of large numbers?

We will suggest various practical situations in which the names of large numbers occur. Exploring these problems will give you a coherent picture of how people use large numbers in life.

1. The Italian traveller and merchant Marco Polo (1254–1323) is credited with first using the term “milione”, “a great thousand”, to describe the enormous wealth of the East. In his *Science of Numbers in Three Parts* (1484), the French mathematician and physician Nicolas Chuquet (1445–1500) similarly introduced the terms billion, trillion and so on, up to nonillion.

Describe the mathematical objects to which these terms refer and the pattern by which the terms were formed. Determine what the last term means. If possible, extend the sequence of terms and the corresponding objects. Analyse their present-day usage: has it changed since Chuquet’s time? Do “milliard” and “octillion” belong among them, and what do they mean?

2. What names did different peoples use for large numbers at different times? Compare historical information about the Slavs, Arabs and other peoples. For example, the Slavs once called ten thousand a *tma*. In Old Slavonic numeration, names existed up to 10⁵⁰. Beyond that came the note: “And more than this the human mind cannot comprehend.” Why do you think that was?

3. In this context, how would you interpret these lines from A. Blok’s poem *The Scythians*: “You are millions. We are tmas, and tmas, and tmas …”?

4. It turns out that there is a certain limit beyond which large numbers no longer have practical uses. The well-known “googol”, for example, can be regarded as such a limit. Do you know what a googol is?

## Answers and historical background {#section-2}

We will give the answers straight away so you can see how to approach the questions.

1. We have already encountered “million” and “milliard” in everyday situations as names for fairly large numbers. They also appeared in mathematics when decimal numeration was introduced. These terms therefore name classes of large numbers. In practice, the prefix “milli”, as in millimetre and milligram, is used to denote thousands and is also related to the word “mile”. Thus, in Russian, “million” means “a thousand thousands” or “a great thousand”, and its value is 10⁶. We also encounter “billion”, used in English-speaking countries in place of the Russian “milliard”: 10⁹, for example in computing.

The next class of numbers is also familiar: trillions, 10¹². Notice the roots *bi-* and *tri-* and the suffix *-llion*. They suggest the pattern for forming these words: add “llion” to the Latin numerals for two, three and so on. The next numbers in the sequence are therefore quadrillion, 10¹⁵ (we know the word “quadrant”); quintillion, 10¹⁸ (few people use the name); sextillion, 10²¹ (think of the English numeral “six”); septillion, 10²⁴ (unfamiliar to most); octillion, 10²⁷ (octave and octant are connected with the number 8); and nonillion, 10³⁰ (we have encountered the name of a 9-fold multiple in physics). It is easy to guess the next number: decillion, 10³³.

These names belong to the “short scale”. Short-scale number naming is used in Russia and other former USSR countries, and in the English-speaking and Arabic-speaking worlds. Another system, the “long scale”, is used in most French-speaking, Scandinavian and Spanish-speaking countries.

2. Ways of writing and naming large numbers arise in the history of mathematics when studying Archimedes’ *Psammites*, or *The Sand Reckoner*. He tries to determine how many grains of sand would fill the universe. To do this, he attempts to calculate the universe’s size from the astronomical ideas of his time and proposes a way of naming very large numbers. In Archimedes’ time, the numerical system could describe numbers up to a myriad, 10000. Archimedes reached numbers in the myriad-myriad period. He ultimately showed that the universe could contain no more than 10⁶³ grains of sand.

Slavonic numeration used these names for large numbers from the ninth to the seventeenth centuries: *tma* (10⁴ or 10⁶), *legion* (10⁵ or 10¹²), *leodr* (10⁶ or 10²⁴), *voron* (10⁷ or 10⁴⁸), *koloda* (10⁸ or 10⁴⁹), and *tma tem* (10⁹ or 10⁵⁰). In ordinary Russian speech, *tma* means an enormous, uncountable quantity. It gave rise to the military title *temnik*, a senior commander. The number 10⁵⁰, *tma velikaya* or *tma tmushchaya*, was the greatest number: an infinite quantity, an innumerable multitude. For example, Revelation says: “The number of the mounted army was two tmas of tmas; and I heard their number.” In *The Tale of Igor’s Campaign*, we find the phrase “the horde covered [the land] with a raven’s wing”.

3. There are many of you, millions, while we are innumerable: a *tma*.

4. A googol is a number written in decimal notation as a one followed by 100 zeroes. The term has no substantial theoretical or practical importance; it is used to mean an unimaginably large number.

The company name Google is a misspelling of “googol”.
