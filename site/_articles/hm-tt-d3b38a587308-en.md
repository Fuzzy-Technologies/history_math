In many countries, parliamentary elections use proportional representation of political parties. Let us consider how seats can be divided fairly in an election.

Suppose a constituency is to elect 5 members of parliament, and 3 parties, A, B and C, are contesting the election. They divide the seats in proportion to the votes cast for their candidate lists. For example, suppose 150000 citizens vote: 43500 for A, 69000 for B and 37500 for C.

Under proportional representation, the number of seats would be:

- For party A: 43500 × 5 / 150000 = 1.45.
- For party B: 69000 × 5 / 150000 = 2.3.
- For party C: 37500 × 5 / 150000 = 1.25.

Fractions create a difficulty, and there are different ways to handle them. First, allocate seats according to the integer parts: A and C receive one each, and B receives two. Then compare the fractional remainders, 0.45, 0.3 and 0.25. The largest is 0.45, so the remaining seat goes to A.

The final allocation is 2 seats for A, 2 for B and 1 for C.

This allocation procedure is associated with the “largest-remainder method”. It has an interesting, visual geometric interpretation.

Consider a regular polygon with as many sides as there are parties. The figure below shows a “seat-allocation triangle”, since our problem has three parties. Choose a scale, for example 1 mm for 1500 votes, so that 150 000 votes correspond to 10 cm. We use an equilateral triangle of height 10 cm. Its sides are then 20√3/3 ≈ 11.55 cm. For convenience, draw segments inside the triangle parallel to its altitudes, dividing the sides into 10 equal parts. Some intersections of these segments will give us the vertices of the regions we need.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Label the triangle's sides A, B and C, for our three parties. Every point inside the triangle then represents a possible election outcome. This follows because the sum of the distances from any interior point of an equilateral triangle to its sides is constant and equals its height.

The points corresponding to each seat allocation form a region of the triangle. For example, the points corresponding to (2, 2, 1) form a regular hexagon. This is because the boundary at which the allocation changes is a line segment parallel to one of the triangle's altitudes. Three pairs of such segments form a regular hexagon.

Draw lines parallel to the bases at distances 43500 / 1500 = 29 mm from A, 69000 / 1500 = 46 mm from B, and 37500 / 1500 = 25 mm from C. They intersect at M, the point representing the election outcome. In this case, M lies in region 221. This means that party A has 2 seats, B has 2 and C has 1.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

The largest-remainder method has a paradox. In a repeat election with the same number of voters, party A may lose a seat even though it receives more votes. At point N, for example, A has gained votes, B has lost votes and C has also gained votes, yet the allocation is 1 seat for A, 2 for B and 2 for C. This depends on the fractional parts, as described at the start of the article.

Other systems of proportional allocation exist, but none is free of paradoxes.

In Russia, proportional representation is used in elections to the State Duma, since 2007, and to the legislative or representative bodies of the constituent entities of the Russian Federation. Its practical implementation has become a task for mathematicians.
