The culture of the ancient land between the Tigris and Euphrates is called Babylonian after one of its largest cities. The region is also known as Mesopotamia, the land between the rivers. This culture originally arose farther south, on the Persian Gulf. In the IV millennium BCE, Sumerian cities emerged in the rivers’ deltas.

Mesopotamia’s rich history comprises several periods: the Sumerian–Akkadian period; flourishing in the XVIII century under Hammurabi, with its capital in Babylon near modern Baghdad; conquest by the Assyrians in the VIII century; and restored power in the VII century BCE under Nebuchadnezzar. It was then conquered by Persia in the VI century BCE and by Alexander the Great in 336. After his death, Mesopotamia became a region of the Hellenistic Seleucid state.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

The Sumerians invented cuneiform writing. Clay tablets were the principal writing material. Signs were impressed on a slab of soft clay, which was then fired or simply dried. With careful handling, these tablets could survive for centuries. Archaeological excavations have uncovered many, dating from the XXX to the I century BCE. They are now held in museums worldwide. About 150 fragments with mathematical problems and 200 with numerical tables are known. O. Neugebauer, F. Thureau-Dangin and A. Sachs made major contributions to deciphering cuneiform texts. They deciphered and analysed these mathematical texts in the 30s of the XX century.

The ancient historian Herodotus reported the Babylonians’ fairly extensive mathematical knowledge.

Babylon provides the first example of consistent positional numeration. It uses only two cuneiform signs: a vertical wedge for 1 and a horizontal wedge for 10. Numbers from 1 to 59 are written by repeating the required number of each wedge. The number 60 is represented by the same vertical wedge.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

There was initially no zero sign. Later, a substitute sign was introduced to separate place values, but it was never placed at the end of a number. The vertical wedge subsequently came to denote any integer power of 60, including negative powers.

Positional numeration has enormous significance in the history of science. It replaced a complex notation system with a simpler one. Traces of sexagesimal numeration also survive in modern measurements of angles and time. Astronomers of all nations used sexagesimal fractions until the XVII century, calling them astronomical fractions.

Babylonian mathematics is considered more advanced than Egyptian mathematics. It significantly influenced Greek mathematics and that of other peoples with political and economic ties to Babylon.

The Babylonians possessed techniques for solving problems reducible to quadratic equations. They also solved problems reducible to cubic and biquadratic equations. Many surviving problems take the form of equations and systems of equations of the first and second degree. As in Egypt, their solutions were limited to listing the stages of calculation required.

The principal feature of Babylonian geometry was its algebraic character. Formulas for areas and volumes are given, including rules for calculating the areas of some regular polygons.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

The Babylonians are believed to have known the Pythagorean theorem. Van der Waerden held that they discovered it around 2000 BCE. One clay tablet lists right triangles with integer sides: Pythagorean triples.

The substantial achievements of Babylonian mathematicians and astronomers provided a foundation for the science of later civilizations, especially ancient Greece.
