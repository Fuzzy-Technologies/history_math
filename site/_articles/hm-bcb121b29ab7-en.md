*Marking the 220th anniversary of his birth*

A German mathematician and researcher in mechanics whose research covered number theory, algebra, mathematical analysis and dynamics.

He became a member of the Berlin Academy of Sciences in 1836, a Foreign Member of the Royal Society in 1833, a member of the Paris Academy of Sciences in 1846, a foreign corresponding member of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences in 1830 and its honorary member from 1833, a member of the Vienna Academy in 1848 and a corresponding member of the Madrid Academy in 1848.

He was the younger brother of the Russian academician and physicist Boris Semyonovich Jacobi, also known as Moritz Hermann von Jacobi.

## Education and Early Work

He was born on 10 December 1804 in Potsdam, in the Kingdom of Prussia.

He entered the University of Berlin at sixteen. In 1825 he converted to Christianity and changed his name from Jacob Simon to Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi.

In 1825 he defended his doctoral dissertation on decomposing rational functions into partial fractions. Soon afterwards he began lecturing on differential geometry at the University of Berlin as a privatdozent.

In 1827 Jacobi was invited to the University of Königsberg as an extraordinary professor; he became a full professor there in 1829.

## Functions and Notation

He published his first masterpiece, *New Foundations of the Theory of Elliptic Functions*, in 1829, and studied theta functions and other transcendental functions. Jacobi was the first to apply elliptic functions in number theory. The proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem, published in 1995, relied on the connection between elliptic curves and modular forms.

Jacobi brought into general use the now standard rounded $\partial$ notation for partial derivatives, which Legendre had occasionally used. A class of orthogonal polynomials generalizing Legendre polynomials bears Jacobi’s name.

## Teaching and Scholarly Connections

He moved to Berlin in 1843 and taught at the University of Berlin.

Jacobi’s lectures and scholarly correspondence had a marked influence on nineteenth-century mathematics. Hermite, Liouville, Cayley and other distinguished mathematicians studied and developed his work.

Jacobi’s 1841 work “On the Formation and Properties of Determinants” acquainted mathematicians with determinant theory. The idea of the determinant goes back to Leibniz, Lagrange, Cauchy and Cramer. Jacobi also brought functional determinants into use; some are known as Jacobians.

He maintained close ties with Russian mathematicians, carrying on an active and friendly correspondence with Mikhail Ostrogradsky and helping train the students Ostrogradsky sent from Russia. At the same time as Ostrogradsky, he proposed the method of changing variables in multiple integrals.
