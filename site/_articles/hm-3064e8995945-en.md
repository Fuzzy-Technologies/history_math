*Marking the 100th anniversary of his birth*

> “One must tell the truth. But people do not care much about the truth.” (A. S. Yesenin-Volpin)

A Soviet and American mathematician, philosopher and poet, with a Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences degree. He was the son of the poet Sergei Yesenin and Nadezhda Volpin; his parents were not married.

He wrote a number of fundamental works in general topology, mathematical logic and the foundations of mathematics.

## Family and Education

He was born on 12 May 1924 in Leningrad. His father, the poet Sergei Yesenin, died when Alexander was a year and a half old. His mother was the poet and translator Nadezhda Davydovna Volpin. His parents were fellow writers and friends but were not married, although they lived together for a time.

In 1933 he moved with his mother from Leningrad to Moscow. In 1946 he graduated with honours from Moscow State University’s Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics. In 1949 he completed postgraduate study at the University’s Mathematics Research Institute and defended his Candidate of Sciences dissertation in topology, “On the Weight of Bicompacta”, supervised by P. S. Alexandrov and P. S. Novikov. He then went to work in Chernivtsi in 1949.

## Mathematics and Philosophy

He proved a theorem on dyadic spaces that bears his name.

From 1961, Yesenin-Volpin developed ultrafinitism, a conception that rejects the infinity of the set of natural numbers. He attempted to prove the consistency of the Zermelo–Fraenkel axiom system. In 1969 he translated Paul J. Cohen’s *Set Theory and the Continuum Hypothesis* into Russian and wrote its preface.

## Human-Rights Work and Persecution

Yesenin-Volpin was one of the leaders of the Soviet dissident and human-rights movement. He entered history less as a mathematician and poet than as an active dissident.

He was first arrested on 21 July 1949 after being denounced and was charged with “anti-Soviet agitation and propaganda”. He was sent for a forensic psychiatric examination, declared legally insane and subjected to compulsory treatment in a psychiatric hospital. In 1950, as a “socially dangerous element”, he was exiled to Karaganda Region for five years. He was released under an amnesty in December 1953.

He returned to Moscow. In 1959 he was again confined to a psychiatric hospital for sending a collection of his poems and his *Free Philosophical Treatise* abroad. He spent about two years there.

In total, he spent six years in prisons, exile and psychiatric institutions as a political prisoner. It is often said that Volpin’s only illness was “pathological truthfulness”.

He signed his poems, circulated in samizdat and published in the West, with the surname Volpin. His book *A Leaf of Spring*, published in New York in 1961, included the *Free Philosophical Treatise* alongside his poems.

In 1965 Yesenin-Volpin organized the Glasnost Rally, held on 5 December in Moscow’s Pushkin Square—the first public protest demonstration in the post-war USSR.

In February 1968 he was again confined to a psychiatric hospital. Several prominent mathematicians signed the “Letter of the Ninety-Nine” protesting against his forced hospitalization. After his release, he joined the Committee on Human Rights in the USSR in 1970.

## Emigration and Poetry

In 1972 he emigrated to the United States at the insistent suggestion of the Soviet authorities. He worked at the University at Buffalo and later became an honorary professor at Boston University.

From 1989 he repeatedly returned to his homeland to visit his mother. He loved and respected his famous father, Sergei Yesenin, and never ceased to say that he had inherited his father’s character.

Born of my own father, perhaps,\
Or directly of his fame,\
Gifted with a subtle curiosity,\
I scorned the games of childhood…

For the unbridled ones like me,\
Cantor has laid out the road. (1946)
