*Marking the 150th anniversary of his birth*

> “The capacity to learn and improve is a fundamental principle of a reasonable and just world.” Edward L. Thorndike

Mathematics teaching should rest on sound psychological and pedagogical foundations. Let us turn to the classic figures in these fields.

Edward L. Thorndike was an outstanding American psychologist, educator and researcher in animal psychology, and a classic figure in educational psychology. He became a member of the US National Academy of Sciences in 1917. He was president of the American Psychological Association in 1912 and the American Association for the Advancement of Science in 1934. He received his Master of Arts degree in 1897 and his PhD in 1898.

## Education and Research into Learning

He was born on 31 August 1874 in Williamsburg, Massachusetts.

He graduated from Wesleyan University in 1895, then studied at Harvard University, receiving his master’s degree in 1897, and at Columbia University, receiving his doctorate in 1898.

His first psychological investigations studied animal behaviour. These experiments led Thorndike to conclude that animals act through “trial, error and accidental success”.

After his doctoral dissertation, *Animal Intelligence: An Experimental Study of the Associative Processes in Animals* (1898), he left animal experiments behind and turned to people. In 1899 he became a psychology instructor at Teachers College, Columbia University, where he investigated human learning. On the basis of this research, he formulated several laws of learning.

He explained a successful outcome at the level of neurons. During a successful action, a neural system that has been made ready actually functions rather than remaining inactive. His “law of readiness” states that changes in the organism’s readiness to conduct nerve impulses are connected with practice.

## Teaching and Recognition

He became a professor at Columbia University in 1904 and worked until 1940 as head of the psychology department at the Institute of Educational Research.

His contribution was highly regarded by many scientists and researchers, including the great Russian scientist I. P. Pavlov and the founder of behaviourism, John Watson. The objectivity and precision of Thorndike’s research made his works classics.

Many of his works continue to be published and used today.

## Notes

The following works by Thorndike appeared in Russian. The titles given here are translations of the Russian editions:

- *Principles of Teaching Based on Psychology* (1926).
- *New Methods of Teaching Arithmetic* (1930).
- *The Psychology of Adult Learning* (1931).
- *The Psychology of Arithmetic* (1932).
- *New Methods of Teaching Algebra* (1932).
- *Questions of Teaching Algebra* (1934).
- *Human Learning* (1935).
- E. Thorndike and J. B. Watson, *Behaviourism. Principles of Teaching Based on Psychology. Psychology as the Science of Behaviour* (1998).

### A Sweet for the Orphans

For his first scientific experiment, the young psychology graduate Edward Thorndike, who held a master’s degree, worked with children from a nearby orphanage. He showed them a sweet and said it would go to the first child to guess, from his facial expression, the word or number he had in mind.

The experiment soon ended. The university authorities forbade Thorndike from visiting the orphanage.
