At the beginning of the war, fate brought thousands of people to the small, quiet town of Yelabuga on the Kama. Many enterprises and institutions were evacuated here from the combat zone.

To save Leningrad State University’s most important laboratories and scientific staff engaged in defence research, a research branch was to be established far behind the front. Professor V. A. Ambartsumian, the university’s vice-rector for research, was entrusted with its leadership.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The rector’s office told him: *“Viktor Amazaspovich! You are appointed head of the Leningrad State University branch in Yelabuga, in the Tatar ASSR. All staff, their families, instruments and property must be evacuated there immediately. An entire train — twenty-four freight wagons — is at your disposal. You are in command of the train.”*

They set out on 18 July 1941. Approximately 150 people arrived in Yelabuga, including about 70 research staff and leading scientists: one academician (V. A. Fock), two corresponding members of the USSR Academy of Sciences (V. I. Smirnov and V. A. Ambartsumian) and more than a dozen Leningrad University professors.

We can imagine the difficulties and hardships the evacuees endured on their journey. At the time, Yelabuga could be reached only by water. A small landing on the Kama was everyone’s first point of arrival: refugees and employees of evacuated enterprises and institutions alike. From there, the town was two kilometres away, on foot or by cart.

The town stands on the right bank of the Kama, with a beautiful view. A solitary stone tower — the “Devil’s Settlement” — crowns the steep hill by the landing. Stone houses and three churches of beautiful architecture almost entirely line the embankment. Most of the town consists of wooden houses.

The arrivals were hardly likely to be concerned with this beauty. They all remarked, however, on the considerate reception they received at such a difficult time. Part of the teacher-training institute building was placed at their disposal. University staff were housed in private apartments, institute apartments or the institute building itself. The university’s research laboratories occupied the third floor of the left wing.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Among the arrivals was the mathematician Vladimir Ivanovich Smirnov, a corresponding member of the Academy of Sciences. His family was accommodated in a nearby house (formerly 77 Lenin Street, now 175 Neftyanikov Avenue). Other evacuated Leningrad families lived there too: those of M. L. Lozinsky, the well-known poet and translator, N. A. Tolstoy, and the physicist Professor E. F. Gross. On 5 May 2016, a memorial plaque honouring V. I. Smirnov was unveiled on this house.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

In 1981, V. A. Ambartsumian wrote to Yelabuga: *“I remember with great warmth the kindness of Yelabuga’s residents towards the Leningrad group I had to lead. It truly was a time of arduous feats and relationships of high moral integrity.”*

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

Throughout its existence, the beautiful diocesan girls’ school building, constructed in 1903, has housed teacher-training institutions. In 1939, a teacher-training institute opened on the basis of the pedagogical college, with four faculties — physics and mathematics, Russian language and literature, natural sciences and geography, and history — and a correspondence department. It trained teachers for incomplete secondary schools. During the war, the building housed the teacher-training institute, the pedagogical college, the evacuated branch of Leningrad University and the evacuated Voronezh University.

Despite all the difficulties, the Leningrad scientists began work as early as September. Their principal activity was naturally research, chiefly for defence and practical purposes. Under V. A. Ambartsumian’s leadership, in particular, a theory of light scattering in turbid media was developed. It addressed the problem of determining the visibility range of objects and lights in the atmosphere and ocean; Soviet submariners used it in practice. The branch’s physicists calculated projectile trajectories for firing from mine and torpedo tubes. The invariance principle, now widely used, was also developed in Yelabuga in 1941–43. Academician V. A. Fock carried out fundamental studies of radio-wave propagation. V. A. Ambartsumian later wrote: *“I am proud of the remarkable results achieved by the Yelabuga branch of Leningrad University. Thank you, people of Yelabuga, for your assistance and care for Soviet science.”*

V. I. Smirnov also worked productively in Yelabuga. The first edition of the fifth volume of his famous *Course of Higher Mathematics*, on which he worked there, appeared in 1947. In 1943, he was elected a full member of the USSR Academy of Sciences, and in 1948 he received the Stalin Prize, 2nd class, for *Course of Higher Mathematics*.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

The scientists who arrived in Yelabuga also actively joined the teacher-training institute’s teaching work. On 5 November 1941, V. I. Smirnov was appointed head of the physics and mathematics department and a part-time mathematics lecturer.

Wartime conditions changed the organization of teaching and educational work. With schools urgently needing staff, teacher training was accelerated. Curricula and course lengths were reduced, students’ weekly workload increased, and examination periods and holidays shortened. V. I. Smirnov undertook all kinds of work at the institute. Orders survive appointing him chair of the State Examination Commission in June 1942 and a member of the admissions commission in June 1943.

Teachers and students alike faced hardship, with shortages of food and fuel. A. Kh. Valeeva, who lived in the same house as the Leningrad evacuees, wrote: “*Summer brought relief to Yelabuga’s people. In spring, after the water receded, wild onions appeared in the meadows. People made soup and porridge with them and sold them in the street. The institute garden was divided into plots, and everyone who wished could have a vegetable garden. People went to the plantation with carts and shoulder yokes to gather fallen wood. They stocked up on fuel for winter. They also burned peat. Peat extraction was in full swing.*” At the end of June 1942, the institute issued an order requiring all teachers, from 1 July, to report to work at 8 in the morning and spend at least 8 hours a day working on the auxiliary farm. For each workday they received 1 kg of grain, 2 kg of vegetables, 1 kg of potatoes and 8 rubles in cash.

Throughout the war, however, people retained a deep faith in Victory and a desire to contribute what they could to defeating the enemy. Teachers and students actively undertook military training, helped collective farmers bring in the harvest, and regularly donated part of their wages to the defence fund.

The Leningrad scientists lived and worked under the same conditions and shared the same concerns. Household tasks took considerable time. Branch employees cut firewood, gathered hay for horses and grew vegetables on allocated plots. F. D. Klement recalled: *“The years in Yelabuga behind the front were difficult for all of us in their own way, especially the first, when we were just beginning to adapt to a new way of life. <...> Our salaries covered only potatoes. <...> At the beginning of 1942, researchers were allocated rations, which made life noticeably easier. <...> In Yelabuga, we nevertheless managed to organize and adapt our daily lives so that we lived better than in Saratov and Kazan.”* Like all home-front workers, they worked under the slogan “Everything for the front, everything for Victory!” The Yelabuga newspaper *Stalin’s Path* of 30 October 1941 carried a note, “The University Staff’s Contribution”, reporting that the Leningrad University branch had handed the district commission 9475 rubles in cash and various goods worth 1560 rubles to support the front. This was the only wartime article in the local press covering the branch’s life.

An institute order of July 1943 stated: *“For the best academic results, discipline, and a high level of public and defence work, award the ‘Institute’s Challenge Red Banner Named for the 25th Anniversary of the Great October Socialist Revolution’ to the physics and mathematics faculty. <...> Commend Professor Vladimir Ivanovich Smirnov, <...> for fully completing the curricula, for the absence of outstanding academic requirements among students in the spring-semester subjects taught by these persons, and for exemplary educational work.”*

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-8" %}

In the summer of 1943, V. A. Ambartsumian left for Yerevan, and Fyodor Dmitrievich Klement was appointed in his place. Klement therefore had to carry out the difficult and responsible task of returning the branch from evacuation in the summer of 1944. All the Leningrad evacuees anxiously awaited permission to return home.

By order of the director, in connection with the return of the Leningrad University branch, Academician V. I. Smirnov was granted leave from 22 June 1944 and released from his work at the teacher-training institute when it ended.

Yelabuga’s residents remember the Leningrad scientists warmly. They were approachable, courteous and ready to help. Residents recall, for example, how in the spring of 1943 the Leningrad evacuees rescued children drowning in the flooded Kama. These are the traits of a true member of the Russian intelligentsia.

As a result of work carried out in Yelabuga during the war and in the first postwar years, several members of the group became academicians or corresponding members of the USSR Academy of Sciences and the academies of the Union republics, and received State Prizes: V. A. Fock, V. A. Ambartsumian, V. I. Smirnov, B. P. Nikolsky, V. N. Tsvetkov and N. P. Yerugin.
