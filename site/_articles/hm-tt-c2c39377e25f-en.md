(On the 125th anniversary of his birth)

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-1" %}

The most prominent physicist working at the Yelabuga branch of Leningrad University during the war was Academician Vladimir Alexandrovich Fock (1898–1974). He belonged to that brilliant group of theoretical physicists whose work created quantum theory, one of the greatest achievements of the human mind. Scholars of his work place this Russian physicist alongside Einstein, Bohr, Rutherford, Kapitsa and Landau. Fock’s distinctive quality was his versatility. He made enormous contributions to diffraction and radio-wave propagation, to Einstein’s theory of gravitation, and achieved outstanding results in theoretical photometry, elasticity and integral equations. Such breadth is more characteristic of eighteenth-century scholars; in the twentieth century, it gave Fock an exceptional place in science.

In writing this article, we used new material that had become available to us: a book dedicated to the 100th anniversary of his birth, *V. A. Fock: Selected Works* (St Petersburg, 2003), containing a biography written by his daughter N. V. Fock and recollections by pupils and colleagues, including Professor Yu. N. Demkov; and Academician V. V. Sobolev’s memoir “The Yelabuga Branch of the University”, in *Essays on the History of St Petersburg University*, vol. 7, 2000.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

V. A. Fock was born on 10(22) December 1898 in St Petersburg. He graduated from Petrograd University in 1922 and remained in the physics section of its Faculty of Physics and Mathematics to prepare for a professorship. Most of his life was associated with that university.

His earliest scientific work was already of a high standard; the Klein–Gordon–Fock equation is a well-known example. At Professor Max Born’s invitation, he spent 1927-28 in Göttingen, the leading international centre for the creation of quantum theory. Fock already had a reputation for solving the most difficult mathematical and physical problems effortlessly. Born described him as “Die Grosse Mathematishe Kanon”, “the great mathematical cannon”, an impressive description from the acknowledged head of a school of quantum theory.

In 1932, Fock was elected a corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences, but he received the title of professor and confirmation of his doctorate in physical sciences only in 1934. In 1936, his work on the quantum theory of complex atoms earned him the D. I. Mendeleev Prize. His 1935 work on the symmetry of the hydrogen atom was of fundamental importance. Specialists described “The Hydrogen Atom and Non-Euclidean Geometry” as a work of “extraordinary beauty and elegance”. In 1937 it earned Fock an Honourable Mention in the International N. I. Lobachevsky Competition and an honorary doctorate from the Kazan Physical and Mathematical Society.

In 1937, attacks on prominent physicists began appearing in the press, accusing them of idealism. V. A. Fock was arrested. He was soon released thanks to the immediate, vigorous intervention of Academician P. L. Kapitsa, who wrote to Stalin describing him as an outstanding physicist. One line read: *“Fock’s arrest is an act of rough treatment towards a scientist which, like rough treatment of a machine, impairs its quality. To impair Fock’s ability to work is to damage the whole of world science.”*

Fock was not in Leningrad at the beginning of the war. The evacuation train bound for Yelabuga had already left. At that time, he and his pupils began preparing firing tables for naval guns aimed at targets far from the shoreline. Eight notebooks of tables were delivered to the appropriate department and used in the defence of Leningrad. Fock later received the Medal “For the Defence of Leningrad” for this work, an award of which he was very proud.

V. A. Fock arrived in Yelabuga as an established scientist and an academician, a title he had held since 1939. In late September 1941, he was ordered to fly with his family from the besieged city to Moscow. There he familiarised himself with problems important to the country’s defence that he could work on in Yelabuga. The family travelled by train from Moscow to Kazan, then by steamer to Yelabuga.

At first, Fock and his family lived in V. I. Smirnov’s apartment; the city council later provided a separate house. It was cold, with many windows, and needed a great deal of firewood. Fock and his son had to gather the wood themselves. Rations in Yelabuga consisted of black bread and one cooked meal a day. In summer 1942, they acquired a vegetable plot and grew potatoes. Local people saw Academician Fock carrying the harvested potatoes home in buckets suspended from a shoulder yoke. Despite the difficulties, life became more settled. The children went to school, and the family did not go hungry.

In Yelabuga, V. A. Fock developed the theory of integral equations in connection with radio-wave diffraction. His results were published in *Doklady of the Academy of Sciences* in 1942–43 and in *Matematicheskii Sbornik* in 1944. Working under him in Yelabuga were A. I. Anselm and M. G. Veselov, both later professors, as well as V. G. Nevzglyadov, O. N. Trapeznikova, M. I. Petrashen and N. S. Krylov.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

In 1943, a government telegram summoned Fock to Moscow, where he began work at a research institute headed by Admiral A. I. Berg. His work on radio-wave diffraction earned him the State Prize in 1946. In Moscow, he worked at the Academy’s Physics Institute, the Institute for Physical Problems, and the Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna.

As soon as circumstances allowed, he resumed his work at Leningrad State University. He had to travel continually between Moscow and Leningrad. At the university’s physics faculty, Fock lectured on relativity and, in 1955, published the monograph *The Theory of Space, Time and Gravitation*.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

After the war, he continued defending science against ignorant and unscrupulous people. He did much to prevent physics from being devastated as genetics had been. His interventions were thoughtful and well founded, and he criticised without regard to rank, even criticising Bohr and Einstein. He firmly maintained that honest criticism in no way diminished Einstein’s greatness and was the correct stance to take. In his later years, Fock devoted much attention to philosophical questions in quantum mechanics and relativity.

In 1960, Fock received the Lenin Prize for his work in quantum field theory. In 1968, he was awarded the title Hero of Socialist Labour. He received four Orders of Lenin and the Order of the Red Banner of Labour. He was a foreign member of the Norwegian and Danish academies of sciences and the Academy of Sciences of the GDR, and held honorary doctorates from the universities of Delhi, Michigan and Leipzig.

The V. A. Fock Prize was established in his memory in 1995. It is awarded by the Russian Academy of Sciences for outstanding work in theoretical and mathematical physics.

In 1999, a three-rouble silver coin was issued for the 275th anniversary of St Petersburg University. It depicts four great scholars who are a source of pride for the university: the mathematician V. I. Smirnov, the physicist V. A. Fock, the chemist D. I. Mendeleev, and the physiologist A. A. Ukhtomsky.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

In 2018, shortly before Victory Day, a memorial plaque was unveiled in Yelabuga. The house at 81 Moskovskaya Street acquired a name of its own: Fock’s House. He had lived there during the Great Patriotic War.
