> “I do not know how the world sees me, but to myself I seem only a boy playing on the seashore, amusing himself by finding now and then a pebble more colourful than the others, or a beautiful shell, while the great ocean of truth lies unexplored before me.”  
> I. Newton

In the seventeenth century, natural science faced a new problem: finding the laws of motion. The mathematics of constant quantities was inadequate for this task. The work of Cavalieri, Descartes, Wallis, Huygens and Pascal prepared the way for differential and integral calculus. These emerged in the works of Newton and Leibniz and became powerful tools for solving new problems. Acknowledging their reliance on earlier generations of mathematicians, Newton said: *“I achieved so much because I stood on the shoulders of giants.”*

Isaac Newton was born on 25 December 1642 (4 January 1643) at Woolsthorpe, Lincolnshire, England. He was a brilliant English scientist, founder of modern mechanics, creator of the mathematics of continuous processes, and astronomer.

Newton studied first at Trinity College, Cambridge, then at Cambridge University under Isaac Barrow. He was a professor at Cambridge University from 1669 to 1701, Master of the Mint from 1699, and president of the Royal Society from 1703.

His university years were his most productive, when he wrote his most important works. In 1665–1666, plague broke out in Cambridge and Newton left for the countryside. His biographer Professor More observed: *“In the history of science, there is no comparable example of achievements such as Newton's during those two golden years.”*

During those years, he discovered his general method of analysis, which he called the “theory of fluxions”. Its first systematic presentation appears in the manuscript “The Following Propositions Are Sufficient to Solve Problems by Motion” (1666).

Newton's next work, *Analysis by Equations with an Infinite Number of Terms* (1669), contains fundamental discoveries concerning infinite series. In particular, he generalised the binomial expansion to any real exponent. His method of studying functions through series subsequently became enormously important throughout analysis.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

Publishing his work drew Newton into controversies with the scholarly world, so he stopped publishing. His writings appeared only after his name had gained worldwide fame. For example, *The Method of Fluxions and Infinite Series* was printed only in 1736, after his death. This late publication of his works on infinitesimal calculus limited the spread of his discoveries, especially outside England.

Newton's presentation of analysis has a mechanical foundation. The current variable quantities change with time; they are called fluents and denoted x, y, z. The rates at which each fluent changes in motion are called fluxions and denoted ẋ, ẏ, ż. Infinitesimal changes in the fluents, called moments of the fluxions, are denoted ẋo, ẏo, żo, where o is an infinitesimal quantity.

It is easy to see that Newton's fluxion is a derivative. His method, however, was not fully definite. The infinitesimal quantity was not rigorously defined: sometimes it was disregarded or discarded, while at other times one divided by it, treating it as non-zero.

Newton saw the enormous value of his abstract method, although perhaps he initially found it difficult to express that value more accessibly.

He stated the two main problems of analysis in terms of the method of fluxions.

The first problem of analysis: given a relation between fluents, determine the relation between their fluxions. This is the problem of differentiating functions that depend on “time”.

The second problem of analysis: given an equation containing fluxions, find the relation between the fluents. This is the problem of integrating a first-order differential equation.

Newton applied the method of fluxions to a broad range of geometric problems involving tangents, curvature, extrema, quadratures and rectification of curves. He devoted considerable attention to integrating ordinary differential equations and solved several problems in the calculus of variations.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

Newton's name is associated with the solution of many interconnected problems in mathematics and physics. He regarded mathematics solely as a tool for physical research. His principal work, *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy* (1687), is imbued with the spirit of the new calculi and demonstrates their full power in studying nature's laws. In it, he brought together all previously known and newly discovered knowledge of motion and force into a single deductive system of terrestrial and celestial mechanics. It contains an axiomatic construction of mechanics, the laws of mechanics and universal gravitation, a mathematically rigorous derivation of Kepler's empirical laws of planetary motion, a solution to the problem of tides, and a theory of lunar motion.

Newton defined the basic concepts of mechanics: mass, density, momentum, force, space and time. The same work contains a developed theory of conic sections, necessary for studying planetary and cometary motion. He also first developed a general theory of limiting processes, called the “method of first and last ratios”. The term “limit”, *limes*, is introduced here too. The concept itself is not defined; the method of limits is presented in 12 lemmas.

Newton's mathematical contribution extended beyond the creation of analysis. His *Universal Arithmetic* became one of the first early modern textbooks of arithmetic, algebra and the application of algebra to geometric problems. His algebraic contributions include a numerical method for solving algebraic equations, Newton's method; important theorems on symmetric functions of the roots of algebraic equations, Newton's identities; and results on isolating roots.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

In *Universal Arithmetic* (1707), he developed the theory of number and gave this definition: *“By number we understand not so much a multitude of units as the abstract ratio of one quantity to another of the same kind taken as a unit. Number is of three kinds: integer, fractional and irrational. An integer is measured by the unit; a fraction by a multiple subdivision of the unit; an irrational number is incommensurable with the unit.”*

Newton used “universal arithmetic” to mean algebra. Later books on algebra often bore the same title.

In *The Method of Differences* (1711), he solved the problem of passing a parabolic curve through given points and proposed the interpolation formula named after him. In *Enumeration of Curves of the Third Order* (1704), he classified these curves.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

Newton worked intensively on optics, discovered the dispersion of light and built the first reflecting telescope in 1668. He developed a theory of colour based on the observation that a prism separates white light into the colours of the visible spectrum, laying the foundations of modern optics.

The weaknesses of Newton's analytic methods provoked attacks on the theory of fluxions. In 1734, the English philosopher and bishop George Berkeley published the pamphlet known as *The Analyst*: *The Analyst; or, a Discourse Addressed to an Infidel Mathematician, Wherein It Is Examined Whether the Object, Principles and Inferences of the Modern Analysis Are More Distinctly Conceived, or More Evidently Deduced, than Religious Mysteries and Points of Faith*. It contained witty and largely justified criticism. These difficulties were resolved only when the modern concept of a limit was clearly established in the work of Bolzano and Cauchy.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-8" %}

Newton is buried in England's national pantheon, Westminster Abbey.

The statue erected to him in Trinity College in 1755 bears a line from Lucretius: *“Who surpassed humankind in intellect” (Qui genus humanum ingenio superavit).*
