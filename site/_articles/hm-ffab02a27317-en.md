*Marking the 325th anniversary of his birth*

Georg Wolfgang Krafft (15 July 1701–12 June 1754) was a German and Russian physicist and mathematician, an academician of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences and one of those who established systematic experimental physics in Russia. He matters just as much to the history of education: at the Academic Gymnasium and university, Krafft taught arithmetic, geometry, physics and geography, and wrote instructional manuals for his pupils.

## Tübingen and St Petersburg

He was born in Tuttlingen, Württemberg. He attended the monastic school at Bebenhausen and then the school at Blaubeuren Abbey, before entering the University of Tübingen in 1722 and receiving his master’s degree in 1724. On the advice of his mentor Georg Bernhard Bilfinger, he went to St Petersburg.

In St Petersburg, Krafft first taught at the Academic Gymnasium. From 1727 he took part in astronomical observations at the observatory under the academician Delisle. In 1731 he was appointed an adjunct in general mathematics; in 1733, when Euler took charge of mathematics, Krafft succeeded him as professor of theoretical and experimental physics. He became one of the most active contributors after Euler to the Academy’s *Commentarii*.

## Experiments, the Gymnasium and Textbooks

Krafft worked across physics: hydrodynamics, heat and thermometry, optics and acoustics, and magnetism. He improved instruments, measured the properties of ice and magnets, and in 1744 proposed an empirical formula for the temperature of a mixture of hot and cold water. In 1738 he delivered the first complete course of physics lectures and wrote the first physics textbook for students. Under him, the Academy’s physics cabinet was expanded and equipped for experimental demonstrations, and was considered one of the finest in Europe.

Administrative work also fell to him. In 1738 Krafft became inspector of the Academic Gymnasium, where a new statute brought order to the requirements for teachers and pupils. Documents from the period show his firmness on basic matters: he insisted that teachers know Russian, since otherwise teaching became an empty formality.

As a textbook author, Krafft favoured brief, practical manuals. His books for gymnasium pupils covered mathematical and physical geography using globes and maps (1739), and simple and compound machines (1738). Later, his *Brief Introduction to Theoretical Geometry*, intended for pupils of the Academy, circulated widely. These books set a standard for teaching: clear illustrations, careful experiments and a coherent sequence of questions and exercises.

## The Ice House and a Scientific Legacy

In St Petersburg’s history, Krafft’s name is also linked to the Ice House of 1740. He carried out its calculations and left a detailed description in 1741, turning a court spectacle into a historical document. Within the Academy he was also prominent as a teacher: Georg Richmann and Mikhail Lomonosov studied with him, while Lomonosov translated some of his writings and retained his respect for his older colleague.

In 1738 Krafft married Catherine Felten, joining a family well known in academic St Petersburg; after returning to Tübingen, he sent for his family in 1745. In 1744 he left his Russian post and became professor of mathematics at the University of Tübingen. He remained an honorary member of the St Petersburg Academy and corresponded with it until his death. He recommended Richmann as his successor in the Academy and handed the affairs of the gymnasium over to other colleagues.

Krafft’s name is heard less often today than those of his great contemporaries, but his contribution is clear in what he left behind: the Academy’s laboratory culture, its first physics courses, gymnasium textbooks and the habit of explaining an experiment so that a pupil could repeat it. He also did much to spread the heliocentric view of the world. These are foundations of science that may be less conspicuous than celebrated discoveries, yet support an entire age.
