> A lofty brow, the eyebrows drawn,  
> A ray reflected in cold bronze…  
> Yet motionless and stern, he stands  
> As though alive — serene and strong.  
> V. Firsov

Moralizing about ungrateful descendants is a doubtful enterprise these days. Still, it is upsetting when schoolchildren shown a portrait of Nikolay Ivanovich Lobachevsky say that it is Aleksandr Sergeyevich Pushkin.

1 December 2023 marks the 231st anniversary of the birth of the “Copernicus of geometry”. The life and work of the creator of non-Euclidean geometry are thought to have been thoroughly studied. Yet, like many other people of genius, Lobachevsky was surrounded by myths even during his lifetime. One concerns his likenesses. Studies of Lobachevsky's iconography are known only to specialists, so even today we are often offered false portraits of the scientist in place of authentic ones.

This happened with the portrait published in the second issue of Kvant in 2006 to illustrate academician A. D. Aleksandrov's article “Dullness and Genius”.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The original version of the article, in issues 11–12 of 1982, did not contain this portrait. Below it is Lobachevsky's signature, copied from another source, an engraving by V. Maté. Aleksandr Danilovich was hardly intending to present this particular portrait. Let us turn to the indisputable conclusions of B. L. Laptev, professor at Kazan State University, a noted geometer and historian of mathematics and one of the foremost researchers of Lobachevsky's life and work. In the appendix “On the Iconography of N. I. Lobachevsky” to N. I. Lobachevsky: Scientific and Educational Legacy; Leadership of Kazan University; Fragments; Letters (1976), he wrote: *“The so-called portrait of N. I. Lobachevsky, an oil painting by V. A. Shchegolkov… is not a portrait of the great geometer.”*

It depicts a man in a frock coat with a bas-relief before him. The painting became known as Portrait of the Young Lobachevsky. It was reproduced repeatedly in various publications, including the first volume of Lobachevsky's Complete Collected Works and Professor V. F. Kagan's well-known monograph Lobachevsky (1948). That edition included an article on Lobachevsky's iconography by the Honoured Art Worker Professor P. M. Dulsky. He writes that V. Shchegolkov was one of the first painters to portray Lobachevsky during the great mathematician's period of creative ascent: *“The overall composition offers a very striking bust-length image of the scholar in a three-quarter turn, with his arms crossed on his chest. Lobachevsky wears a black frock coat; his neck is enclosed by a full, close-fitting cravat like a scarf, enlivened by the sparkle of a precious pin. The complexion is emphasized by the snow-white edges of a starched collar, its points standing out against his cheeks. The hands in new white kid gloves draw attention.”*

B. L. Laptev observes: *“...B. V. Fedorenko's lengthy investigations ended in an absolutely convincing conclusion that Lobachevsky could not have been the sitter for this portrait.”* V. A. Shchegolkov painted it from life in 1833 as a trial work. He had graduated from the Arzamas painting school in 1832 and sought a drawing teacher's post at Kazan University's gymnasium, which required approval through the Academy of Arts. The portrait shows a young man, whereas Lobachevsky was forty at the time. A certain resemblance to the scholar's appearance in other portraits later caused confusion. It became widely, but falsely, known as a portrait of the great scientist in his youth.

Now for the continuation of this false portrait's history. In 1992, for Lobachevsky's 200th anniversary, the Bank of Russia issued a commemorative one-rouble coin bearing his likeness. Whom do we see on its reverse?

It is that same false portrait of Lobachevsky. Can it belong in a collection entitled “Physicists and Mathematicians on the World's Coins”?

There are very few portraits of Lobachevsky altogether. The best known were painted by the Kazan artist L. D. Kryukov around 1836–1840. He was a painter and a teacher at Kazan University.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

The bronze bust on the Lobachevsky monument, unveiled in 1896, was made by the artist Maria Dillon. The sculpture was based on Lev Kryukov's portraits.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-8" %}

A likeness made by daguerreotype in December 1855, shortly before Lobachevsky's death, is of considerable documentary importance. It is known as Portrait of Lobachevsky in Old Age.

Some specialists believe that Lobachevsky's portraits differ from his actual appearance. He was tall, lean and slightly stooped, with an elongated face, a penetrating gaze from dark-grey eyes, and drawn-together eyebrows. His thick mass of dark-blond hair curled slightly and stood in unruly tufts in every direction. In youth he was cheerful and vigorous; only towards the end of his life did he become somewhat subdued and frail, as a result of sorrowful events in his life.

L. B. Modzalevsky's Materials for a Biography of N. I. Lobachevsky (1948) mentions how Lobachevsky himself laughed at Kryukov's portrait of him: *“Can that really be me? It is nothing like me!”* Artistic portraits are, of course, not mirror images of their sitters. Artists have a right to some imaginative interpretation, particularly when depicting such a legendary, heroic figure. Similarly, the final likeness taken from the daguerreotype may also have been retouched or drawn over. At that time Nikolay Ivanovich was elderly, ill and blind.

Lobachevsky's appearance became surrounded by legends just as his life and scientific work did. How he looked is nevertheless not the main issue, although grateful descendants should try to establish the truth in his memory. Rejecting truth is foolish. The scientific truth established by this scientist of genius is more precious than all the myths and legends about him. As he himself said: *“Greatness is preserved through the ages by immortality.”*
