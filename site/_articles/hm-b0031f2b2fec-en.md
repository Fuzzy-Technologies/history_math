*Marking the 320th anniversary of his birth*

A Swiss mathematician and physicist, and an early investigator of determinants and systems of linear equations. He became a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1749.

## Education, Travel and Teaching

He was born in Geneva, Switzerland, on 31 July 1704.

He showed considerable mathematical ability from an early age and defended a dissertation at the Academy of Geneva in 1722. In 1724 he and Jean-Louis Calandrini were appointed to the academy’s new chair of mathematics; from 1734 Cramer held the chair alone.

From 1727 to 1729 he made a study tour through Basel, England, Holland and France, meeting European mathematicians. On returning, he continued scholarly correspondence with many of them.

In 1729 he returned to Geneva and resumed teaching. In his free time he wrote numerous articles on geometry, the history of mathematics, philosophy and applications of probability. He also published a work on celestial mechanics in 1730 and a commentary on Newton’s classification of cubic curves in 1746.

## Cramer’s Rule

Cramer’s best-known work is the treatise *Introduction to the Analysis of Algebraic Curves*, published shortly before his death in French as *Introduction à l’analyse des lignes courbes algébriques* in 1750.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

In this treatise, Cramer sets up a system of linear equations and solves it using the algorithm later named after him: Cramer’s rule.

Cramer considers a system of any number of linear equations with a square coefficient matrix. If the determinant of the coefficient matrix is nonzero, the system’s unique solution can be written as a column of fractions with that determinant as their common denominator. Cramer did not yet use the modern term “determinant”: Gauss introduced the word in a different context in 1801, and Cauchy used it in its modern sense in 1812. Cramer gave a precise algorithm for computing the corresponding expression: the algebraic sum of all possible products of matrix entries, with one entry from each row and each column. In Cramer’s formulation, the sign of each term depends on the number of inversions in the corresponding permutation of indices: positive when that number is even. The numerators in the solution column are computed in the same way: the $k$th numerator is the determinant of the matrix obtained by replacing the $k$th column of the original matrix with the column of constant terms.

The methods of determinants were later developed by Bézout, Vandermonde, Cauchy, Jacobi and Cayley. The formation of linear algebra as an independent field took more than a century. Determinant theory found applications in astronomy and mechanics, in solving algebraic systems and in studying forms.

## Editions of the Bernoullis’ Works

Cramer was a pupil and friend of Johann Bernoulli. Around 1740, Bernoulli entrusted him with arranging the publication of his collected works. Cramer published Johann Bernoulli’s four-volume collection in 1742 and a two-volume posthumous collection of Jacob Bernoulli’s works in 1744. In 1745, together with Castillon, he edited the two-volume correspondence between Leibniz and Johann Bernoulli. All these editions made a great impact in the scientific world.
