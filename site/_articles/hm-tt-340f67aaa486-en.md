A histogram (from the Greek ἱστός, “column”, and γράμμα, “writing”) is one way of presenting experimental data graphically. Karl Pearson introduced the concept and its name in 1895.

A histogram is most often drawn as a vertical bar diagram, consisting of adjoining rectangles resting on a straight line. Histograms help visualise data in the initial stages of statistical analysis of a quantity.

In statistics, a histogram provides a visual representation, based on a sample, of the probability density function of a random variable. Its shape allows an analyst to make an initial assessment of the statistical distribution that the variable follows. For example, if all the bars are approximately equal in height, the distribution is uniform; if they form a symmetrical “hill”, it is normal.

To draw a histogram, first divide the set of values that a sample observation can take into several intervals. These intervals are usually equal in width. Mark them on the horizontal axis, then draw a rectangle above each one. The height of each rectangle is proportional to the number of sample observations falling within the corresponding interval. The area of each rectangle is then proportional to the frequency with which that range of values occurs in the population being studied.

Distribution histograms are particularly indispensable when a dataset contains a very large number of values.

If the rectangles are arranged horizontally, this type of histogram is called a horizontal histogram. It is most often used when the names of the indicators are very long and difficult to display legibly along the horizontal Ox axis.

Another name for this kind of histogram-like visualisation is a bar chart. A bar chart usually has gaps between the bars, whereas a histogram does not.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Histograms are widely used in secondary-school mathematics under the new educational standards: pupils learn different ways of presenting numerical information in their lessons.

Histograms can be created electronically. Many tools are available, from simple spreadsheets such as Excel to powerful modern cloud-based business intelligence systems.

Histograms are also used in photo processing, where they show the distribution of brightness levels in a photograph.
