The classical astrolabe is one of the oldest astronomical and surveying instruments, used to determine latitude. It has been known since the ancient Greek astronomers Hipparchus, in the second century BCE, and Ptolemy, in the second century CE. Its operation is based on stereographic projection, which maps circles on a sphere to circles in a plane, a principle discovered by Apollonius of Perga.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-1" %}

Scholars of the Islamic East improved the astrolabe and used it not only to determine the time and the lengths of day and night, but also to perform certain mathematical calculations. From the twelfth century, different types became known in Western Europe. The astrolabe reached the height of its European popularity during the Renaissance, becoming almost a cult object.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The word “astrolabe” comes from the Greek ἄστρον, “star”, and λαμβανω, “to take”. This shows that in antiquity the instrument was used to determine angles on the celestial sphere. Later, it became the principal angle-measuring instrument for measuring angles between directions in a horizontal plane, constructing parallel and perpendicular lines, and surveying a plan of the terrain. Different types could perform different kinds of work. Astrolabes were used by astronomers and mathematicians, sailors and travellers, architects and builders. They were also used for astrological predictions.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

On the astrolabe's flat circular disc, stereographic projection represented points and lines of the celestial sphere that remain fixed during its daily rotation: the celestial pole, the celestial equator, the northern and southern tropics, the celestial meridian and its parallels, the zenith and azimuth circles. Various nomographic scales might also be included, such as tangent scales, the “direct shadow” or *umbra recta*, and cotangent scales, the “inverse shadow” or *umbra versa*.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

A simple astrolabe consists of a metal limb graduated in degrees with a pair of diopters fixed to it. An alidade with a moving pair of diopters rotates at its centre. A socket mounts the limb on a tripod. More advanced instruments have a telescope attached to the alidade for sighting over long distances. They may also have a compass or surveying compass, a vernier for reading fractions of a degree, and a limb for measuring angles in a vertical plane.

To measure an angle, take readings on the instrument's limb after sighting each of the objects between which the angle is required. The difference between the readings gives the angle.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

The astrolabe has long been used for teaching. In the 1950s, Glavuchtekhprom of the RSFSR Ministry of Education manufactured a school astrolabe. Models could also be made by hand, and limb scales and construction instructions were issued for this purpose. Homemade astrolabes can also be built from two school protractors that together form a complete limb. The planisphere, a movable star map, is a modern descendant of the astrolabe. The quadrant and sextant are, in effect, also simple modifications of it.
