*Marking the 135th anniversary of his birth*

> “An engineer or technician works with numbers and must have a good feel for them.”
>
> V. M. Bradis

The year 2025 marks the 135th anniversary of Vladimir Modestovich Bradis’s birth. A Soviet mathematics educator and specialist in teaching methods, he became a corresponding member of the RSFSR Academy of Pedagogical Sciences in 1955, a Doctor of Pedagogical Sciences in 1957 and a professor in 1934. He received the title of Honoured Scientist of the RSFSR in 1957.

## Why Do We Remember Bradis?

In the digital age, a school pupil can find a sine or logarithm in seconds using a calculator. Yet Vladimir Modestovich Bradis’s name lives on in the memory of generations. His *Four-Figure Mathematical Tables* became more than a teaching aid: they were a cultural phenomenon of the Soviet era. But who was the man behind the name? How did a Socialist Revolutionary activist become one of the USSR’s most influential mathematics educators? Why have his tables been reprinted more than a hundred times and remained in print today? Let us explore the life of this remarkable man, whose work changed the teaching of numerical mathematics in schools and universities.

## From School to Teaching

Vladimir Bradis was born on 23 December 1890 in Pskov to Modest Vasilyevich and Yelizaveta Vasilyevna Bradis, both schoolteachers. His surname came from his Estonian paternal grandfather.

In 1901, aged ten, he entered the Pskov Boys’ Gymnasium. In 1907, however, he was expelled for distributing illegal literature. In 1909, after reaching adulthood, he was exiled to Tobolsk Province for three years because of his membership in the Pskov Socialist Revolutionary group.

Returning from exile in 1912, Bradis entered the mathematics division of St Petersburg University’s Faculty of Physics and Mathematics. He completed the four-year course in three years and successfully defended his degree dissertation in 1915. He was retained at the Department of Pure Mathematics while also teaching at the commercial school attached to the Putilov Works.

The revolutionary events of 1917 forced Bradis to leave Petrograd. He moved to Tver, where he began teaching mathematics on courses organized by the provincial zemstvo, a local government body, and later at the workers’ faculty, which prepared workers for university study.

When the Institute of Public Education opened in Tver in 1920, later becoming the Kalinin Pedagogical Institute, Bradis joined its teaching staff. He worked there for more than fifty years, rising from lecturer to professor in 1934 and becoming a corresponding member of the RSFSR Academy of Pedagogical Sciences in 1955.

## Tables and Numerical Calculation

The year 1921 marked a turning point in his career: his *Tables of Four-Figure Logarithms and Natural Trigonometric Values* appeared. Later published as *Four-Figure Mathematical Tables*, they went through about sixty reprints in the Soviet period and some thirty after 1991.

What made the tables special? Bradis did more than bring together existing calculations: he developed a system that minimized tedious work. He selected the functions most useful in engineering calculations, computed their values to four significant figures and arranged them in a convenient form.

Bradis’s principal work addressed the theory and teaching of numerical calculation in secondary schools. In 1957 he defended his doctoral dissertation in education, “Computational Work in the Secondary-School Mathematics Course”. His *Methods of Teaching Mathematics in Secondary School* (1954, general methods) was reprinted many times and became a classic of educational literature.

Besides his world-famous *Four-Figure Mathematical Tables*, he wrote more than a hundred articles, textbooks, teaching aids and studies of mathematics and its teaching.

## Institute Work and Recognition

At Kalinin State Pedagogical Institute, now Tver State University, Bradis held various posts:

- head of the physics and mathematics division from 1922;
- dean of the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics;
- deputy director for academic affairs;
- head of the Department of Algebra and Geometry.

He remained a consulting professor in the mathematics department until the end of his life.

His works were translated into languages of the peoples of the USSR—Ukrainian, Latvian, Belarusian and Moldavian—as well as Bulgarian, Czech, Romanian, German and Chinese.

His achievements earned many honours:

- Outstanding Worker in Public Education, 1947;
- the Order of Lenin, 1953;
- the K. D. Ushinsky Medal, 1954.

### The Tables’ Extraordinary Popularity

Bradis’s tables became a Soviet bestseller. From the 1930s they appeared almost every year for thirty years, in enormous print runs. As contemporaries put it: “Millions read this little book. They did more than read it: they reread it. Pencil in hand, they worked through every page, again and again. Pupils, students, engineers—everyone had Bradis’s tables.”

Before calculators and computers, Bradis’s tables were indispensable:

- they gave quick access to trigonometric functions, logarithms, squares and cubes;
- four significant figures provided enough precision for most practical calculations;
- their convenient organization saved computation time.

Although the tables have now lost their practical importance, they remain:

- an important historical record of the development of numerical mathematics;
- a model of how to teach calculation;
- part of the cultural heritage of Soviet science and education.

Teachers report that some schools still use Bradis’s tables when teaching how to solve triangles.
