A Russian mathematician and researcher in mechanics, one of the founders of the St Petersburg school of mathematics, a member of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences (1830) and many foreign academies.

He was born on 12 September 1801, or 24 September in the Gregorian calendar, in Poltava Governorate.

He studied at Kharkiv University but did not receive a graduation certificate after a dispute over the university’s formal requirements.

Ostrogradsky went to Paris to further his mathematical education (1822–1828), where he was in contact with Laplace, Fourier, Cauchy and Poisson. He returned home with a deserved reputation as a talented scholar. He then taught in St Petersburg at military and engineering institutions and at the Main Pedagogical Institute.

His first papers were published while he was still in Paris. His research ranged across mathematics and mechanics: differential and integral calculus, higher algebra, number theory, geometry, probability, mathematical physics, analytical mechanics, ballistics and the theory of heat.

Many of Ostrogradsky’s theorems and formulas entered courses in mathematical analysis, although his name is not always mentioned. In a paper on the theory of heat, he proved the theorem now known as the Ostrogradsky–Gauss formula (1828). It connects a volume integral with a surface integral. He derived a formula for converting triple integrals into surface integrals and developed a method of changing variables in multiple integrals (1836).

For him, the value of mathematical research lay in the possibility of applying its results in practice. One of his papers on probability, which laid the foundations of statistical methods of acceptance inspection, was used to check goods supplied to the army.

Ostrogradsky was an excellent teacher and organizer. He created the Russian school of applied mechanics. His students included many well-known scholars: I. A. Vyshnegradsky, N. P. Petrov and N. S. Budayev. He wrote numerous textbooks, including *An Elementary Geometry Manual*, *Lectures on Algebraic and Transcendental Analysis*, *A Programme and Synopsis of Trigonometry for Military Educational Institutions* and *A Course in Celestial Mechanics*.

He developed a system of educational views and approaches to teaching mathematics. Under his influence, teaching manuals presenting progressive methods appeared in the mid-nineteenth century.

His contemporaries regarded Ostrogradsky as a Russian mathematician of the first rank. Nevertheless, he did not understand the ideas of non-Euclidean geometry and strongly opposed Lobachevsky’s work.
