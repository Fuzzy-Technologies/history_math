*Marking the 210th anniversary of his birth*

He was called the Adam of the mathematical world: he coined “matrix” and invented dozens of terms still in use. Sylvester is regarded as one of the creators of the formal language of modern science.

A distinguished English mathematician and university teacher. He became a professor in 1841, a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1841 and a foreign corresponding member of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences in 1872.

## Education and University Career

He was born on 3 September 1814 in London.

James began studying mathematics at St John’s College, Cambridge, in 1831. He could not receive a bachelor’s degree, however, because it required assent to the doctrines of the Church of England, which Sylvester refused to give.

In 1841 he received bachelor’s and master’s degrees from Trinity College, Dublin.

He moved to the United States to become a professor at the University of Virginia, where he worked from 1841 to 1843.

He soon returned to England. He was a professor at the Royal Military Academy from 1855 to 1870.

In 1877 Sylvester returned to America to become the first professor of mathematics at the new Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore. He played a major role in establishing the American mathematical school.

In 1878 he founded the *American Journal of Mathematics*, which is still published today.

In 1883 he returned to England to take the chair of geometry at Oxford University, which he held until his death.

## University Freedom and Research

Sylvester spent many years fighting for university freedom and for scholars’ rights and convictions. Breaking through the barrier of antisemitism, he became the first Jew at Oxford and the first Jewish professor in the United States.

He wrote about 180 research papers on algebra, number theory, combinatorics, probability theory, mathematical physics and mechanics.

Two of his many contributions to algebra became classics: the theory of elementary divisors (1851) and the law of inertia of quadratic forms (1852).

He worked on invariant theory and became one of its leading specialists.
He introduced the terms “invariant”, “covariant”, “commutant” and “discriminant”.

## Concepts Bearing Sylvester’s Name

His name is attached to a matrix used to calculate the resultant of two polynomials, a criterion for the definiteness of a symmetric square matrix and a theorem about configurations of lines in the plane.

The Royal Society’s bronze Sylvester Medal, awarded since 1901 for outstanding contributions to mathematics, is named after him.

## Notes

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Poetry was one of Sylvester’s lifelong passions. He read and translated works from the original French, German, Italian, Latin and Greek. Many of his mathematical papers contain illustrative quotations from classical poetry.
His book *Laws of Verse* appeared in 1870.

Sylvester said of mathematical eloquence: “An eloquent mathematician must, by the nature of things, remain as rare a phenomenon as a talking fish.”
