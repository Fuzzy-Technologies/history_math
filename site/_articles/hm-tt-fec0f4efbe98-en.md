Johann Bernoulli was the second brother in the first generation of the remarkable Bernoulli family of Basel. A mathematician, mechanician and physician, he was the family's most famous representative, the younger brother of Jacob Bernoulli and the father of Daniel Bernoulli.

He was one of the founders of calculus and, after the deaths of Newton and Leibniz, the leader of European mathematicians.

He was a foreign member of the academies of sciences in Paris (1699), Berlin (1701) and Saint Petersburg (1725), and a Fellow of the Royal Society of London (1712).

Johann Bernoulli was born on 27 July (6 August) 1667 in Basel, Switzerland.

He became a master of arts at 18, then turned to medicine while also developing a passion for mathematics. He did not abandon medicine: after graduating from the University of Basel, he practised as a physician throughout his life. With his brother Jacob, he studied Leibniz's first papers on differential and integral calculus and began profound investigations of his own.

Johann Bernoulli achieved major results in developing analysis and the theory of differential equations. He collaborated actively with Leibniz.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

In 1691–1693 he taught in Paris, establishing the French branch of Leibniz's school: l'Hôpital, Varignon and Clairaut.

He returned home in 1692. In 1694 he received a doctorate in medicine after defending a dissertation, “On the Motion of Muscles”, which was more mathematical than medical.

On Huygens's recommendation, he became professor of mathematics at Groningen in the Netherlands, serving from 1695 to 1705.

In 1705, Johann returned to the University of Basel as professor of Greek. He was elected dean of the faculty of philosophy eight times and rector of the university twice. Immediately after Jacob's death in 1705, Johann was invited to take his brother's chair at Basel, which he held until his own death in 1748. Besides his sons Daniel and Nicolaus, his pupils included L. Euler and G. Cramer.

Johann Bernoulli defined a function as an analytic expression. He produced the first systematic account of differential and integral calculus. Notes from his Paris lectures formed the basis of *Analysis of the Infinitely Small for the Understanding of Curved Lines* (*Analyse des infiniment petits pour l'intelligence des lignes courbes*, 1696), compiled by Marquis G. F. de l'Hôpital: the first textbook of calculus. Almost all the material presented by l'Hôpital came from Leibniz and Johann Bernoulli, whose authorship was acknowledged in general terms in the preface. The Bernoulli–l'Hôpital book was a resounding success with a wide readership and went through 4 editions.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

He developed the theory of the exponential function, derived a rule for evaluating indeterminate forms of type 0 / 0, known as “l'Hôpital's rule”, and described methods for integrating rational functions.

In 1742, Johann Bernoulli published a *Course of Integral Calculus*.

Johann was the first to publish the brachistochrone problem, in 1696: find the shape of a curve along which a particle descends from one given point to another in the shortest time. This was the first variational problem in the history of dynamics. Johann, Jacob Bernoulli and Newton solved it simultaneously. The curve proved to be a cycloid.

Shortly before his death, he published his correspondence with Leibniz, a collection of immense historical interest.

The bitter dispute between Jacob and Johann is another historical fact. It began in 1694 when Johann unjustifiably emphasised his own achievements at the expense of Jacob's. The quarrel exhausted them both. Many years later, Johann acknowledged his mistakes. Yet even this groundless dispute helped to pose new problems and advance analysis.

When the priority dispute over the discovery of calculus erupted with the English mathematicians, Johann joined it energetically. This was natural: he was defending the standing of the triumvirate as well as Leibniz. After Leibniz's death, Johann led the struggle.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

Voltaire wrote this quatrain for Johann Bernoulli's portrait:

*His mind saw truth,*

*His heart knew justice.*

*He is the pride of Switzerland*

*And of all humankind.*
