Developing the foundations of mathematics and clarifying the basic concepts of analysis were among the major achievements of nineteenth-century mathematics. One of the architects of rigorous analysis was the well-known German mathematician Karl Theodor Wilhelm Weierstrass, who became a professor of mathematics at the University of Berlin and was elected its rector in 1873.

He was a member of the Berlin Academy of Sciences (1856), a foreign member of the Paris Academy of Sciences (1879) and the Royal Society of London (1881), and a foreign corresponding member (1864) and honorary member (1895) of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences.

He was born on 31 October 1815 in Ostenfelde, Westphalia, in the Kingdom of Prussia. At his father’s insistence, he entered the law faculty at the University of Bonn in 1834. After four years, during which he studied mathematics intensively instead of law, he left and entered the Westphalian Wilhelm University in Münster.

After completing his studies in 1842, he became a teacher at a provincial lower secondary school, where he worked for fourteen years. His teaching experience later helped him become one of Germany’s best-known mathematics lecturers.

He published a paper on Abelian functions in 1854, for which the University of Königsberg promptly awarded him a doctorate *honoris causa*, an honorary doctorate without a dissertation defence. He became a professor at the University of Berlin in 1856 and was elected to the Berlin Academy of Sciences that same year. He devoted forty years of his life to the University.

His meticulously prepared lectures were renowned. He embodied mathematical scrupulousness. Weierstrass made a decisive contribution to the rigorous foundations of mathematical analysis. His approach rested on the theory of real numbers and the so-called $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ language.

In his lectures, he rigorously defined the basic mathematical concepts of function, continuity, derivative and extremum.

He constructed an example of a continuous function that has no derivative anywhere. He proved that every finite-dimensional commutative associative unital algebra over the real numbers with no zero divisors is isomorphic either to the real numbers or to the complex numbers. In geometry, he created the theory of minimal surfaces and contributed to the theory of geodesics. In linear algebra, he developed the theory of elementary divisors.

During his lifetime, students from many countries spread his ideas. His pupils included mathematicians who themselves became famous, among them Cantor, Frobenius, Mittag-Leffler and Kovalevskaya.

The multi-volume edition of Weierstrass’s collected works continued to appear after his death. It covers mathematical analysis, analytic function theory, the calculus of variations and linear algebra.
