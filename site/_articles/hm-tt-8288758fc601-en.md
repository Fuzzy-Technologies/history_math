> To make your love reciprocated, write the numbers 220 and 284 on something, give the smaller one to the person you love, and eat the larger one yourself.  
> A recipe from an old treatise

According to the ancient philosopher Iamblichus, when the great Pythagoras was asked whom he considered a friend, he replied: *“Someone who is my second self, like the numbers 220 and 284.”*

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

In the Middle Ages, talismans engraved with the numbers 220 and 284 circulated as supposed aids to strengthening love.

Amicable numbers are a pair of distinct positive integers, each equal to the sum of the other's proper divisors. Amicable numbers were discovered in the Pythagorean school around 500 BCE. The Pythagoreans found the smallest amicable pair, 220 and 284:

$$
1 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 10 + 11 + 20 + 22 + 44 + 55 + 110 = 284
$$

$$
1 + 2 + 4 + 71 + 142 = 220
$$

In the eighteenth century, Leonhard Euler found a sufficient criterion for constructing amicable pairs, and his list already contained 90 pairs. Here are some of those he found:

2620 and 2924 (1747)

5020 and 5564 (1747)

6232 and 6368 (1750)

10744 and 10856 (1747)

...

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Euler “overlooked” a much smaller amicable pair. In 1866, a 16-year-old Italian, Niccolò Paganini, discovered the pair 1184 and 1210.

In the twentieth century, computers helped to find tens of millions of pairs. Amicable pairs form sequence A063990 in the OEIS (On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences). Yet there is still no efficient general method for finding all such pairs.

Whether there are infinitely many of these numbers also remains unresolved: it is unknown whether the number of amicable pairs is finite or infinite. By April 2016, more than 1000000000 amicable pairs were known. Another question remains open: is there a general formula describing all amicable pairs?

Read about amicable numbers and Pythagorean arithmetic in [*History of Mathematics*](https://ridero.ru/books/istoriya_matematiki/).
