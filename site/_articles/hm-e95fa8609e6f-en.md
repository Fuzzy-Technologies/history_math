Pafnuty Lvovich Chebyshev, an academician and founder of the St Petersburg mathematical school, stood at the forefront of Russian mathematics in the middle and second half of the nineteenth century.

Chebyshev was elected to several foreign academies and learned societies, including the Berlin Academy, the Paris Academy of Sciences, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences and the Royal Society in London.

He was born on 4 May, or 16 May in the Gregorian calendar, in 1821 in the village of Okatovo, in what is now Kaluga Region. He studied at Moscow University. In 1847 he began teaching at St Petersburg University, where he worked for 35 years. Alongside his teaching, he carried out extensive research at the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences. From 1856 to 1873 he also served on the Learned Council of the Ministry of Public Education.

A defining feature of Chebyshev’s work was the close connection between theory and practice, which he repeatedly emphasised. In analysis, he studied the integration of algebraic functions. He constructed a general theory of orthogonal polynomials. Many of his papers concerned number theory, where he obtained important results on the distribution of prime numbers.

Chebyshev’s work in probability theory was also highly significant. He proved general versions of the law of large numbers.

Chebyshev also designed machines and mechanisms. In total he created more than forty new devices and improved more than eighty, including a mechanical calculator, a steam engine and a centrifugal governor. Many were exhibited in Paris and Chicago. His work laid the mathematical foundations of mechanism design in Russia. In 1854 he published one of his foundational works under the title *Theory of Mechanisms Known as Parallelograms*.

During his long teaching career, a strong mathematical school formed around Chebyshev. Its members included A. A. Markov, A. M. Lyapunov, Ye. I. Zolotaryov, A. N. Korkin and D. A. Grave. Markov recalled that once a week Chebyshev received colleagues and students, discussed their research and offered ideas for further work. This practice offers an example of scientific mentorship.
