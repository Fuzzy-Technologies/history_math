*Marking the 120th anniversary of his birth*

Adolf Pavlovich Yushkevich (2 [15] July 1906–17 July 1993), known privately as Andrei Pavlovich, was a Soviet historian of mathematics and one of the founders of the Soviet school in the discipline. A Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences from 1940 and an Honoured Scientist of the RSFSR from 1966, he showed that the history of mathematics requires rigorous work with sources, texts and context, attending to dates, terms and the logic of developing ideas.

## Sources and an Academic Career

He was born in Odessa in the family of the philosopher and historian Pavel Solomonovich Yushkevich. He graduated from Moscow State University’s Faculty of Physics and Mathematics in 1929. The university Chronicle records his Candidate degree, awarded without a dissertation defence in 1935, and his defence of the Doctoral dissertation *Russian Mathematical Literature of the Eighteenth Century* in 1940. Bibliography, historical language and an understanding of how scientific communities form come together in that subject.

From 1930 to 1952 he taught at the Moscow Higher Technical School, now Bauman Moscow State Technical University, and other institutions. During the early 1950s campaign against cosmopolitanism he was forced to leave the technical school, retaining his work at the Institute for the History of Science and Technology, IIET. His university connection was also sustained: he worked at Moscow State University from 1944 and was a professor in the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics in 1965–1968. His principal scientific home was the USSR Academy of Sciences’ Institute for the History of Science and Technology, now the S. I. Vavilov Institute of the Russian Academy of Sciences, where he worked from 1945 until his death.

## Research, Editions and Books

He could turn scattered research into lasting scholarly infrastructure. In 1948, with G. F. Rybkin, he founded *Istoriko-Matematicheskie Issledovaniya*, *Studies in the History of Mathematics*, and shaped its scholarly standards for many years as an editor. Editions of primary texts, archival discoveries and surveys published in the series helped set the discipline’s direction and professional standards.

His research was wide-ranging. He studied medieval mathematics, including work from the Islamic world and other Eastern traditions, making the rigour of science before the Renaissance clear to his readers. A central book was *History of Mathematics in the Middle Ages* (1961), alongside studies of Eastern authors such as *Omar Khayyam* (1965), coauthored with B. A. Rosenfeld. Another major field was the history of mathematics in Russia and the history of analysis. *History of Mathematics in Russia before 1917* (1968) became a reference for researchers, while his part in the collective *History of Mathematics from Ancient Times to the Beginning of the Nineteenth Century* established him as an editor capable of keeping an entire age in focus.

Work on the great mathematicians’ legacies formed another strand. He edited and published their writings and wrote surveys and biographical profiles, including the short popular book *Leonhard Euler* (1982) and studies of Newton’s manuscript legacy. He also wrote for teachers: his series *Mathematics and Its Teaching in Russia in the Seventeenth to Nineteenth Centuries* appeared in *Mathematics at School* in the late 1940s.

## International Recognition and a Scholarly School

His contribution won international recognition. He was president of the International Academy of the History of Science in 1965–1968. His honours included the Order of the Red Banner of Labour in 1951, the Alexandre Koyré Medal in 1971, the US History of Science Society’s George Sarton Medal in 1978, and the first Kenneth O. May Prize in the history of mathematics in 1989, jointly with Dirk Struik. He also received awards in the German Democratic Republic in 1978 and 1983 and from the French Academy of Sciences in 1982.

He is remembered for more than two hundred publications and for the school he created: learning to read a primary source, translate it, comment on it and check every detail. After his death his personal library was transferred to IIET. The books and texts he gathered continue to serve what mattered most to him: active historical research.
