A curvimeter, from Latin *curvus*, “curved”, and ancient Greek μέτρον, “measure”, is a measuring-wheel instrument for finding the length of winding lines, usually on maps, plans and drawings.

Its main component is a rotating wheel connected to a moving dial or counter. To measure a curve, roll the curvimeter's wheel along it. The distance travelled is recorded on the dial. The result is usually expressed in centimetres or inches. The scale of the plan or map is then used to determine the actual distance on the ground.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Electronic curvimeters have LCD displays showing the measurements. Their measurement error is usually about 0.2%, depending on the manufacturer and model. Mechanical instruments generally have a larger error, commonly reaching 0.5%.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

The patent for the pocket instrument is known to belong to the Englishman Edward Morrison, who devised an “innovative instrument for measuring distances” in 1873. Russian production began 30 years later at Pavel Bure's watchmaking establishment in St Petersburg.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

One type of curvimeter is the road measuring wheel, which measures and calculates distances directly on the ground where other measuring methods cannot be used.
