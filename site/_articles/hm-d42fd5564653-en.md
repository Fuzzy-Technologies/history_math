*Marking the 240th anniversary of his birth*

William George Horner (9 June 1786–22 September 1837) was a British mathematician and teacher who wrote on approximation methods and algebraic equations. His surname became the name of an algorithm: Horner’s scheme remains in use in school algebra, numerical calculation and programming.

## A School in Bath and the Craft of Computation

He was born in Bristol and educated at Kingswood School. Accounts differ in their details, but the broad story is that he became an assistant teacher while still a teenager and took charge of the school at a remarkably young age. In 1809 he moved to Bath and opened his own school, the Classical Seminary, where he taught for the rest of his life.

His scientific reputation grew out of practical computation rather than a university chair. In 1819 he published a Royal Society paper on solving numerical equations of all orders by continuous approximation. Its concern was computational discipline rather than elegant formulas: how to organize a calculation so that a root can be found reliably with as little arithmetic as possible.

The scheme that bears his name has a long prehistory. Similar ideas appeared in Ruffini’s work and in much earlier algebraic traditions. In England, Theophilus Holdred published a related method before Horner, while De Morgan later helped establish Horner’s name. The modern algorithm therefore cannot be reduced to the 1819 paper alone: its name conceals a more complicated history of computational methods.

## Optics and Early Animation

His interests extended beyond a single method. He published on functional equations, number theory and approximation, as well as optics, including notes on the camera lucida, an optical instrument that helps an artist transfer the outlines of a subject onto paper.

He also left an unexpected mark on cultural history. In 1834 he described the daedaleum, a rotating cylinder with slots through which a sequence of drawings appears as a moving scene. It was a direct forerunner of the zoetrope and part of the early history of animation.

He lived only fifty-one years, yet achieved something rare for a scholar without a university chair: leaving behind a tool that would outlast his age. His approach respects computation as a craft and mathematics as something done by hand.
