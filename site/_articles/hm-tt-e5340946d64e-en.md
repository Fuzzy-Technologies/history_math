(On the 1050th anniversary of his birth)

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-1" %}

UNESCO decided to celebrate the 1050th anniversary of Abu Rayhan al-Biruni's birth in 2022–23. Anniversary events are taking place not only in Uzbekistan, but also in Iran, Tajikistan and Turkey. Al-Biruni left an enormous mark on the cultural history of Central Asian peoples and had a profound influence on world culture as a whole.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Al-Biruni was a medieval polymath and thinker who left a rich scholarly legacy. He produced about 150 works covering almost every exact science, natural science and humanities subject of his time: history, geography, philology, astronomy, mathematics, mechanics, surveying, mineralogy, pharmacology and geology. His works have been translated into many languages and remain relevant today.

Al-Biruni was born on 4 October 973 in Kath, the ancient capital of Khwarazm, now the town of Beruniy, renamed in his honour in 1957. His name has various spellings: Beruni, Biruni and Beyruni. Beruni is now the most widespread form in his homeland. According to the *Encyclopaedia of Islam*, he was born into an Iranian family. The tenth century would later be called the golden age of Arab-Islamic culture, and al-Biruni's contribution to its rise was immense.

Abu Rayhan received a broad mathematical and philosophical education. He knew many languages: Khwarazmian, Persian, Arabic, Hebrew, Syriac, Greek and Sanskrit.

As early as 995 he made the first globe in Central Asia, enabling the geographical coordinates of settlements to be determined with unprecedented accuracy for the time.

Al-Biruni worked in Gurganj at the courts of the Khwarazmshahs Ali and Ma'mun II, and was one of the leading scholars of the Ma'mun Academy.

In 1004 he returned to Khwarazm's new capital, Urgench. A scholarly academy had been established there; Abu Ali ibn Sina, or Avicenna, also worked in it.

In 1017, after Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni conquered Khwarazm, al-Biruni and other captive scholars were forced to move to Ghazni, where he worked at Mahmud's court.

In his very first work, *Chronology, or the Remaining Traces of Past Generations* (1000), al-Biruni described all the calendar systems known in his time among the world's peoples and compiled a chronological table of all eras, beginning with the biblical patriarchs.

In *India, or a Book Explaining the Doctrines of the Indians, Whether Accepted by Reason or Rejected*, completed in 1030, he gave a detailed scholarly account of Indian life, culture and science. It also contains the legend of the inventor of chess familiar from the history of mathematics.

He devoted 45 works to astronomy. *The Book of Instruction in the Elements of the Art of the Stars* (1029) is a popular introduction to astronomy, consisting of 530 questions and answers on geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, geography, chronology, the construction of the astrolabe and astrology.

His main astronomical work is *The Mas'udic Canon on Astronomy and the Stars*, named after an Asian ruler. In this treatise, he used astronomical observations to calculate the Earth's radius and circumference. His results differed from modern values by only tens of kilometres.

Columbus is usually credited with discovering America. In *The Mas'udic Canon* (1037), al-Biruni had already drawn theoretical conclusions that people lived on this undiscovered land. In that sense, Columbus discovered America not first but last — and this time for good.

Al-Biruni paid great attention to mathematics, especially trigonometry. Besides a substantial part of *The Mas'udic Canon*, he devoted to it *On Determining Chords in a Circle by Means of an Inscribed Broken Line*, discussing several theorems of Archimedes and their generalizations. *On Indian Rashikas* discusses the rule of three, or solving proportions. Other mathematical works include *Spherics*, *The Book of Pearls on the Plane of the Sphere*, *Shadows*, several treatises on the astrolabe and other astronomical instruments, and works on surveying and mathematical geography.

In theoretical arithmetic, al-Biruni treated numbers as in the Pythagorean school: even, odd, prime, coprime, perfect and figurate numbers. He also extended the domain of numbers to include irrationals.

In practical arithmetic, he considered different computational methods: 8 arithmetic operations and their properties, and the concepts of rational and irrational roots. He described a rational root as *“one whose essence can be expressed in words; it is also called speaking, absolute and open.”* Examples are 3 for 9 and 4 for 16. An irrational root is *“one that cannot be expressed in words… It is called deaf, because it does not answer the person seeking it, and can only be found approximately.”*

Al-Biruni treated rhetorical algebra in the spirit of al-Khwarizmi, including his *al-jabr*, the theory of solving equations.

His geometry was based on proof. He gave new proofs of many Greek theorems and extended them. In particular, he proved a theorem equivalent to the “generalized Pythagorean theorem,” or law of cosines.

In *The Mas'udic Canon*, chord trigonometry, previously considered an auxiliary science of astronomy, was effectively presented as an independent science. Al-Biruni introduced an innovation: he replaced the circle radius of 60 units, used since Ptolemy, with a radius of 1. He was among the first to define all six trigonometric lines in a circle, moving from the trigonometry of chords to that of sines and shadows, or tangents.

His contemporaries were always impressed by his drive to keep learning. It is said that, when the 75-year-old scholar felt his end approaching, he asked a friend about the system for calculating illicit profits that the friend was developing. When the friend said this was not what he should think about at such a moment, the dying scholar replied that he did not wish to leave life in ignorance.

Monuments to al-Biruni stand in Tashkent, Uzbekistan, and Tehran, Iran. His 1000th anniversary was widely celebrated in the USSR in 1973.
