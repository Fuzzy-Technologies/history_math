*Marking the 180th anniversary of his birth*

Nikolai Ivanovich Bilibin (1 November 1846–12 December 1914) was a Russian mathematics educator, professor, and author and translator of textbooks in algebra, geometry and trigonometry. His name is seldom heard in the history of major research, yet it matters in the history of Russian mathematics education, through books that lay on school desks for decades.

## Teaching as a Vocation

Bilibin belonged to a nineteenth-century tradition of scholars for whom teaching was the principal vocation rather than a duty alongside research. He worked in an environment where school mathematics was gradually becoming a rigorous discipline: a system of concepts and proofs suitable for broad education rather than a collection of rules.

A substantial part of his life was connected with St Petersburg and new kinds of educational institutions. He taught mathematics at the Higher Women’s Bestuzhev Courses and at the Imperial Institute of History and Philology. Institutions such as these helped create a professional culture of mathematics teaching close to that of the university.

## Textbooks and Pupils

Bilibin was an author and a mediator of European traditions. He translated and reworked teaching texts, particularly those associated with Joseph Bertrand, making the material useful for Russian schools through a clear structure, carefully designed exercises and coherent transitions between topics.

His principal book was *Algebra, Compiled Chiefly after Bertrand*. It went through many editions and received the small Emperor Peter the Great Prize. Alongside it were geometry textbooks and a substantial two-part trigonometry course, a project of unusual scope for the school readership of the time.

Another part of his legacy lies in his pupils and influence on teaching. Sources specifically identify Bilibin as a teacher of Pelageya Yakovlevna Polubarinova-Kochina and Lyubov Nikolaevna Zapolskaya. That is a telling measure of teaching quality: a mathematical school becomes visible behind a teacher’s work.

This is sometimes how mathematics endures: through a well-constructed text that teaches people to reason, prove and calculate carefully for years, rather than through a single celebrated theorem.
