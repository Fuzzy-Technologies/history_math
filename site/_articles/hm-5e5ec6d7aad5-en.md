*Marking the 445th anniversary of his birth*

Claude-Gaspard Bachet de Méziriac (9 October 1581–26 February 1638) was a French mathematician, poet, linguist and translator, a striking figure in the scholarly Europe of the early seventeenth century. He worked at the meeting point of humanistic culture and mathematics, equally at home with ancient texts and problems in number theory.

## Scholar and Problem Writer

Bachet was born in Bourg-en-Bresse and received a classical education in languages, poetry and ancient authors. He is often described as a scholar of exceptional learning who deliberately chose encyclopedic breadth over narrow specialization. This later shaped his principal undertaking: translating and commenting on ancient mathematical books.

His best-known book is the collection *Pleasant and Entertaining Problems Solved with Numbers* (*Problèmes plaisans et délectables…*). The first edition appeared in 1612, followed by an expanded edition in 1624. It helped establish the tradition of mathematical recreations: problems in which real arithmetic lies behind the play.

## Weights, Numbers and Diophantus

Bachet liked problems where an elegant solution mattered more than lengthy calculation. The famous four weights that can measure any integer weight from 1 to 40 on a balance lead to a practical kind of ternary thinking: weights of 1, 3, 9 and 27 work because they may be placed on either pan. Such examples make his book surprisingly modern, revealing an engineer’s way of thinking.

He also pursued a serious line of work in number theory. Biographers note his use of continued fractions to solve indeterminate equations, in the spirit of Pell-type problems. In effect, he advanced computational methods in a subject where others often relied on luck and isolated techniques.

Another contribution was his translation of Diophantus’s *Arithmetica* from ancient Greek into Latin, first published with commentary in 1621. This book became legendary in mathematical history: in the margin of a copy, Fermat wrote the celebrated note that led to Fermat’s Last Theorem and an entire era of number theory.

## Literature and Legacy

Bachet was prominent in literary life too. Elected to the Académie française in 1634, he was among its earliest Immortals as the institution was taking shape.

Bachet’s name is recalled less often than those of nineteenth-century mathematicians, yet it is hard to imagine the early history of number theory without him. He connected the ancient algebra of Diophantus, Europe’s scholarly tradition of commentary and the living practice of problems that teach reasoning rather than calculation alone.
