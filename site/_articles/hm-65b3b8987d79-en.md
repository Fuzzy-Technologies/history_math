Alan Mathison Turing (1912–1954) was a British mathematician, logician and cryptographer, and the father of modern computer science. His “Turing machine” (1936), a model of a general-purpose computer, became a foundation for understanding algorithms and computability.

Turing was a great admirer of Isaac Newton. Once he decided to recreate the famous falling-apple episode. He suspended an apple from a string and began swinging it, studying its trajectory. When his astonished colleagues asked what he was doing, Turing replied:

“I want to understand why apples always fall down, not up. Could it be a bug in the universe’s program?”

He did like to pose questions in unusual ways. During his student years at Cambridge, Turing became known for walking through the streets reading a book without looking where he was going. Once he walked into a lamppost, after which he wrote in his notebook:

“Perhaps the algorithm for spatial attention could be improved.”
