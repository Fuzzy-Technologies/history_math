An ancient Greek mathematician and geometer, and the author of the *Elements*, one of the earliest surviving systematic mathematical treatises.

Euclid was one of the first known scholars of Alexandria. One of the most influential mathematicians of all time, he is called the “father of geometry”. Yet there is no reliable information about his life. He probably lived during the reign of Ptolemy I. His activity is usually associated with Alexandria under Ptolemy I.

Euclid’s name is almost always associated with his best-known work, the *Elements*, an outstanding mathematical achievement. It systematises earlier mathematics. In Felix Klein’s words: “The great historical significance of the Elements is that they passed on to later ages the ideal of a fully logical treatment of geometry.” The *Elements* had a lasting influence on mathematical education. The *Elements* formed the basis of geometry teaching for more than 2,000 years. Much of school geometry comes from its first six books, and Euclidean geometry also underpinned classical mechanics. Its contents extend beyond elementary geometry.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The *Elements* consists of thirteen books. Books I–IV treat plane geometry, while Books VII–IX concern number theory. They include Euclid’s algorithm for calculating the greatest common divisor and his theorem on the infinitude of prime numbers. Books XI–XIII deal with solid geometry. The exposition ends with a complete description of the five regular polyhedra.

The first book gives five postulates and five common notions, which Euclid distinguished. The exposition proceeds through definitions and propositions that build on earlier results.

The fifth postulate has a special place in the *Elements*. Euclid gave mathematics another problem, that of his fifth postulate: “If a straight line crossing two straight lines forms interior angles on the same side whose sum is less than two right angles, then, when extended indefinitely, the two lines meet on the side where the sum is less than two right angles.” It is equivalent to the parallel axiom. As you know, many mathematicians tried to resolve this problem. Its dramatic history ended only in the nineteenth century, with Lobachevsky’s discovery of non-Euclidean geometries.

Euclid’s name is associated with the famous reply, “There is no royal road to geometry.” Ptolemy I, king of Egypt, had heard of Euclid’s extraordinary wisdom and asked him to explain the contents of his books. After listening to proofs of the first theorems, he asked whether there was a shorter way to learn geometry. Euclid’s reply thus entered history.
