Once, at a wedding, Eris, the goddess of discord, threw an apple among the guests. It bore the inscription “to the fairest”.

The apple caused a quarrel between Athena, goddess of wisdom and just warfare; Aphrodite, goddess of love and beauty; and Hera, Zeus’s sister and wife. They asked Zeus, father and ruler of gods and mortals, to decide who should receive it. Through Hermes, the messenger of the gods, Zeus sent them to Mount Ida, where the young Paris, son of King Priam of Troy, was tending his flocks.

Paris had to decide which goddess was the most beautiful. Each tried to win him over: Athena offered wisdom and military glory, Aphrodite the most beautiful woman on earth as his wife, and Hera power and wealth.

Paris, resting by the roadside, did not even bother to remove the cloth he had placed over his eyes against the bright sun. But the goddesses were persistent, and he had to decide which was the most beautiful.

Paris made his choice and held out the apple to Aphrodite. As the myth tells us, she had promised him the love of Helen, wife of the Spartan king Menelaus—Helen of Troy, daughter of Zeus. Paris’s abduction of Helen brought about the Trojan War. He became Aphrodite’s favourite, and she helped him in everything. Hera and Athena came to hate Paris and all the Trojans.

This ancient myth has inspired many paintings. It also appears in a well-known old mathematical problem, “The Judgement of Paris”.

## The problem

Hera, Aphrodite and Athena came before the young Paris to ask which was the most beautiful. They made these statements:

1. Aphrodite: “I am the most beautiful.”
2. Athena: “Aphrodite is not the most beautiful.”
3. Hera: “I am the most beautiful.”
4. Aphrodite: “Hera is not the most beautiful.”
5. Athena: “I am the most beautiful.”

Paris assumed that every statement made by the most beautiful goddess was true, and every statement made by either of the others was false. How could he decide which goddess was the most beautiful?

How would you solve it? Our answer is below.

Note: the problem has a unique solution.

## Solution

Answer: Aphrodite.

This logic problem can be solved by examining the possible truth values of the statements.

Suppose Paris assumes that Athena is telling the truth. She must then be the most beautiful goddess, and statement (4) must be false. This is a contradiction: Hera cannot be the most beautiful if Athena is. The initial assumption is therefore false.

Suppose Paris assumes that Hera is telling the truth. She must then be the most beautiful goddess, and statement (2) must be false. Again there is a contradiction: Aphrodite cannot be the most beautiful if Hera is! This assumption is false too.

Finally, suppose Paris assumes that Aphrodite is telling the truth. Aphrodite is then the most beautiful goddess. The negations of statements (2), (3) and (5) are true and show that Aphrodite is the most beautiful. Thus Paris’s judgement declares Aphrodite the fairest.

## Notes

Louis-Jean-François Lagrenée the Elder’s [*The Judgement of Paris*](https://gallerix.ru/storeroom/1280460497/N/1993/) (1758), oil on canvas. The original is held by the State Tretyakov Gallery, Moscow, Russia.

[Read about the painting on the Tretyakov Gallery website](https://my.tretyakov.ru/app/masterpiece/21077) (in Russian).

The subject comes from ancient myth. Aphrodite, Hera and Athena quarrelled over which of them was the most beautiful. Paris, son of King Priam of Troy, was to judge. Hermes, the messenger of the gods, brought the quarrelling goddesses to him on the slopes of Mount Ida. Perhaps exhausted by his difficult task, Hermes is shown flying away. He can still be recognised by his attributes: winged sandals, the caduceus in his hand and his distinctive petasos, a hat with two wings on its brim.

Paris had been brought up among shepherds from an early age, so the goddesses descending from Olympus found him tending cattle. His herd appears in the lower right corner. The judge himself sits on a leopard skin on the left, a detail indicating his royal birth.

Lagrenée depicts the climax of the myth, when Paris has finally made his choice. He offers the apple to Aphrodite, at the centre of the composition. The pink drapery forming a triumphal arch above her head emphasises her victory. Her constant companion, Cupid, is beside her.

Hera wears a golden diadem marking her special position among the goddesses as the wife of the supreme god Zeus. Yet she has lost the contest. Her traditional companion, the peacock, echoes her indignation with an abrupt, expressive movement.

Athena, wearing a helmet with a magnificent plume, turns her back on the viewer in anger. She had promised Paris the glory of a great commander. The armour discarded at her feet hints at her rejected offer.

As the myth tells us, Aphrodite promised Paris the love of Helen, wife of the Spartan king Menelaus, in return for victory. The pairs of doves and dogs allude to this offer. In painting, pairs of these animals usually symbolised marital love and fidelity and were shown gazing tenderly at one another. Here, however, the doves are ready to fight and the dogs look in different directions: Lagrenée gives them precisely the opposite meaning. The artist hints that the hero’s love will bring endless disasters. Paris’s abduction of Helen led to the Trojan War.

Painted in the Baroque tradition, the work is deliberately decorative. Its pomp and movement recall a colourful spectacle. Light, bright tones predominate. The artist conveys the scene’s emotional intensity.

Louis-Jean-François Lagrenée the Elder was an outstanding history painter and a leading representative of the Baroque style. In 1760–1762 he worked at the Academy of Arts in St Petersburg and played an important part in the development of Russian painting. This canvas was painted in 1758, before he came to Russia. The noted patron and art collector Ivan Shuvalov bought it for its artistic merit and presented it to the Academy, where it served students as a model for copying for many years.
