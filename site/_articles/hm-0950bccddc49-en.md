*Marking the 345th anniversary of his birth*

Christian von Wolff was a celebrated German polymath, philosopher, jurist, mathematician and physicist. He received his doctorate in philosophy in 1703.

A representative of the early Enlightenment, he helped establish the language of German philosophy. He was a pupil and follower of G. W. Leibniz.

He was a foreign member of the Berlin (1711), Paris (1733) and St Petersburg (1725) academies of sciences and a Fellow of the Royal Society of London (1710).

## Education, Teaching and Exile

He was born on 24 January 1679 in Breslau, Silesia, in the Holy Roman Empire, now Wrocław, Poland.

He studied mathematics, physics and philosophy at the universities of Jena and Leipzig.

He was a Privatdozent at Leipzig University in 1703–1706 and professor of mathematics and natural philosophy at Halle in 1706–1723. Accused of atheism, he was forced to leave Prussia. He worked as a professor of philosophy at Marburg University in 1724–1740.

Wolff’s students in Marburg in 1736–1739 included Mikhail Lomonosov and Dmitry Vinogradov, the founder of porcelain production in Russia.

In 1740 King Frederick II invited Wolff back to Prussia. He resumed teaching at Halle University and became its chancellor in 1743, remaining in that post until the end of his life.

## Mathematics and Scholarly Connections

As a philosopher, Wolff was strongly influenced by Leibniz, whom he knew personally and with whom he corresponded.

Wolff’s learning was truly encyclopaedic. He wrote several mathematics manuals that strongly influenced the organization of mathematics teaching in Germany and Russia. His *Mathematisches Lexikon*, published in Leipzig in 1716, was particularly important.

The terms “minuend”, “subtrahend” and “exponentiation” first appear in Wolff’s mathematical writings, which systematically use a dot for multiplication and a colon for division. He is also credited with the term “quadratic equation” (1710).

He played a major role in establishing the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences by helping invite prominent scholars to the city.

## The Physics Textbook and Lomonosov’s Translation

Lomonosov knew Wolff’s textbook of experimental physics well. He considered it the best in Europe and therefore chose it for translation.

In 1746 the first Russian physics textbook appeared: *Wolffian Experimental Physics, Abridged in Latin from the German Original, from Which It Was Translated into Russian by Mikhail Lomonosov, Member of the Imperial Academy of Sciences and Professor of Chemistry* (the Russian title translated here). For a long time it remained the only textbook of experimental physics in Russian.

This translation gave Russian physics both its first Russian-language textbook and foundations for a Russian scientific vocabulary. Many terms proposed by Lomonosov became firmly established in Russian, including those for air pump, piston, elasticity, atmosphere, hydrometer, barometer, meteorology, periphery and magnetic pole.

## Notes

*Wolffian Experimental Physics, Abridged in Latin from the German Original, from Which It Was Translated into Russian by Mikhail Lomonosov, Member of the Imperial Academy of Sciences and Professor of Chemistry* (Russian title translated here): the first Russian physics textbook.

An edition of his well-known encyclopaedic work published during his lifetime is now a rare book:

Christian Wolff, *Foundations of Universal Mathematics* (*Elementa matheseos universae*; the title translated here). Latin edition in five volumes, vols. 1–5. Magdeburg, 1730–1741.

- Vol. 1: Mathematical methods, arithmetic, geometry and trigonometry. 678 pp.
- Vol. 2: Mechanics, statics, hydrostatics and hydraulics. 506 pp.
- Vol. 3: Optics, perspective, dioptrics and astronomy. 768 pp.
- Vol. 4: Geography, hydrography and architecture. 488 pp.
- Vol. 5: Commentaries. 118 pp.

Lomonosov admired not only his teacher’s encyclopaedic learning but also his kindness to others. Wolff helped his Russian students, who were living in a foreign country, settle debts to creditors including tailors, dancing and fencing teachers, shoemakers and booksellers. There is a legend that, on the day the Russian students left, Wolff invited all their creditors to his home and paid their debts before the astonished students’ eyes, moving Lomonosov to tears.
