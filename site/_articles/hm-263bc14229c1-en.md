The word “algorithm” was born not in an IT office but in the translation workshops of medieval Europe.

Around 825, the Baghdad scholar Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (c. 780–850) wrote a treatise on calculation with Indian, or Hindu-Arabic, numerals. It was essentially a collection of clear “recipes” for addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, fractions and calculation without an abacus.

When this text was translated into Latin in the twelfth century, the author’s name became Algoritmi, a Latinisation of “al-Khwarizmi”, and one Latin version opens with these solemn words:

“Dixit Algoritmi”<br>
(“Thus spoke Algoritmi.”)

The steps of a calculation followed—precisely what we would now call an algorithm. The Latinised form of the author’s name gave rise to the medieval term *algorismus*, a name for a method of reckoning. This eventually became “algorithm” in its modern sense.

Another historical fact: a different work of his gave us the word “algebra”, from al-jabr in its title. So one man managed to leave his mark in two parts of the school curriculum—and in every computer.
