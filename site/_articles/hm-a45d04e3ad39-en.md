Sometimes the most frightening mathematical horror stories are written in letters rather than on a blackboard. On 4 April 1820, the Hungarian mathematician Farkas (Wolfgang) Bolyai wrote to his son János, begging him not to attempt a proof of Euclid’s parallel postulate.

“Do not try to conquer the theory of parallels, whether by the method you wrote to me about or by any other. I have explored every path. I have passed through the whole bottomless darkness of that night, and buried every light and every joy of life in it. For God’s sake, I beg you, leave this subject alone, for it can rob you of all your time, your health, your peace and all the happiness of your life.”

The irony is that the “night of parallels” did come to an end — through a new geometry rather than a proof. In Kazan, our great Lobachevsky had already published his first papers in 1829–1830, replacing the parallel axiom and opening the way to non-Euclidean geometry.

We wrote about him in [“What Did Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky Really Look Like? Myths and Reality”](https://teletype.in/@history_math/Kakoj-on-Nikolaj-Ivanovich-Lobachevskij-mify-i-realnost-10-05).

## Notes

An abridged English rendering: “You must not attempt this approach to parallels… I have traversed this bottomless night, which extinguished all light and joy in my life. I entreat you, leave the science of parallels alone… Learn from my example.”

Source: Farkas Bolyai’s letter to János Bolyai, 4 April 1820, reproduced in English translation in reference works and histories of mathematics.
