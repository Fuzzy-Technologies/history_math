Diophantus was the last of antiquity’s great mathematicians and one of the founders of algebra, nicknamed the father of algebra in the Middle Ages. He lived and worked in Alexandria. Almost nothing is known about his life: it remains a mystery in the history of science. His mathematical works are unexpected both in their choice of problems and in their methods of solution.

His principal work, *Arithmetica*, comprised thirteen books. Six survive in Greek, and four more are known through a medieval Arabic translation. With Diophantus, ancient algebra reached its highest point. He based his reasoning on arithmetic rather than geometrical constructions and used syncopated notation: special signs and abbreviations for the unknown, its powers, reciprocal powers and subtraction. He formulated rules for algebraic operations, powers and signs.

He posed and solved problems leading to indeterminate equations and systems, usually seeking positive rational solutions. Today an algebraic equation is called Diophantine when integer solutions are required; the number of unknowns need not exceed the number of equations.

Book VI of *Arithmetica* studies problems involving right-angled triangles with rational sides. Integer triples $(a,b,c)$ satisfying $a^2+b^2=c^2$ are called Pythagorean triples. In modern notation, the formulas $a=m^2-n^2$, $b=2mn$, $c=m^2+n^2$, with $m>n>0$, coprime parameters of opposite parity, give all primitive Pythagorean triples up to exchanging the legs; all other triples are their positive integer multiples. The corresponding construction is present between the lines of Diophantus’s work.

Part of *Arithmetica* was translated into Arabic, and mathematicians in Islamic countries, including Abu Kamil, continued some of Diophantus’s investigations. In Europe, Rafael Bombelli translated portions into Latin but did not publish his translation. The text circulated in print through the Latin editions of Xylander (1575) and Bachet de Méziriac (1621).

Diophantus’s writings provided a starting point for number-theoretic investigations by Leonardo Fibonacci, François Viète, Pierre de Fermat, Leonhard Euler, Carl Gauss and others.

When Fermat read *Arithmetica* in Bachet de Méziriac’s 1621 edition, he stated that the equation $x^n+y^n=z^n$ has no solutions in nonzero integers when the integer exponent $n>2$. He noted in the margin that he had found “a truly wonderful proof of this theorem… but the margins of the book are too narrow to contain it”. This statement is now known as Fermat’s Last Theorem.
