He was born on 27 December 1571 in Weil der Stadt in the Holy Roman Empire, now in Baden-Württemberg, Germany. He studied at the University of Tübingen and worked in Graz, Prague and Linz.

The great German astronomer Johannes Kepler, who worked in Prague, set out the first two laws of planetary motion in *New Astronomy* (1609) and the third in *The Harmony of the World* (1619). These laws laid the foundations of celestial mechanics. He was a convinced supporter of Copernicus’s theory and worked amid the sharp confessional conflicts of his time. His *Epitome of Copernican Astronomy* was placed on the Index of Forbidden Books.

Kepler developed mathematical methods for variable quantities, initially in connection with astronomical problems and later with geometry. He calculated the volumes of solids formed by rotating conic sections in *New Stereometry of Wine Barrels* (1615). Drawing on Archimedes’s idea of the method of indivisibles, Kepler used original methods of integration to find the volumes of 92 solids of revolution. He, too, therefore stands among the pioneers of mathematical analysis.

Kepler also belongs to the prehistory of projective geometry: in his optical work he used the idea of a point at infinity.

In *The Harmony of the World*, he developed a theory of convex and star polyhedra and reflected on the music of the spheres and the aesthetic structure of the universe. Earlier, in *Mysterium Cosmographicum* (1596), Kepler had tried to explain the distances between the orbits of the six known planets by nesting the five Platonic solids between the corresponding spheres.

Kepler did much to encourage Protestants to accept the Gregorian calendar (1613).

After twenty-two years of work, Kepler published astronomical tables in 1627, naming them the *Rudolphine Tables* in honour of the emperor. Demand was enormous: astronomers and navigators continued to use them until the early nineteenth century. The work included tables of logarithms convenient for calculations.
