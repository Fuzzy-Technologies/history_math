> I have always tried, as far as my strength and abilities allowed, to free people from the difficulty and tedium of calculation, whose tiresomeness commonly discourages so many from studying mathematics.  
> John Napier, Scottish baron, mathematician and inventor

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

At the end of the sixteenth century, the Scottish mathematician John Napier (1550–1617), the celebrated inventor of logarithms, devised a simple and ingenious instrument: “Napier’s bones”. He described them as “counting rods” in *Rabdology, or the Art of Calculation with Rods*, published in Edinburgh in 1617. The instrument is based on lattice multiplication. Napier’s bones are regarded as a precursor of early calculating devices: the slide rule, arithmometer and calculator.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Napier’s bones are a simple aid for multiplying multidigit numbers. The instrument consists of 10 rods, made from cardboard strips or wood, with digits marked on them.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

At the top of each rod is a digit from 0 to 9. Below it, cells divided diagonally contain the products of that digit and each single-digit number from 1 to 9. The tens digit of a two-digit product is above the diagonal, and the units digit below it.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

Napier’s instrument is used for multiplication as follows. To multiply 8365 by 7, select the appropriate rods and place them side by side so that their top squares show 8, 3, 6, 5. This row gives the first factor. The seventh squares on the strips then give the partial products of 7 and each digit of the multiplicand. (The 7th row, for the multiplier 7, is selected using a special vertical rod on the left, numbered from one to nine.) To obtain the final result, add these partial products in the “table” from right to left along the diagonals. The sums give the digits of the product, 58555. Addition proceeds by place value, carrying any overflow to the next higher place. Since digits in the multiplicand may repeat, a set must always include extra rods for each digit position.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

Napier’s bones thus give the products of any multidigit number and any single-digit number, but can also multiply two multidigit numbers. Use the rods to obtain the products of one factor and each digit of the other, then add the partial products on paper, or using an abacus, arithmometer or calculator. The diagram shows the example 493 × 85 = 41905.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

Napier’s bones do not fully mechanise multiplication, but they do reduce the time required.

Division with the instrument is much less convenient. To divide one multidigit number by another, use the rods to form the divisor’s products with all single-digit numbers, then reduce the operation to successive subtractions, again on paper.

Napier’s bones could also be used to calculate a number’s square root.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-8" %}

Some shortcomings were addressed in a modification proposed in 1901 by the engineer I. A. Grigorovich. His device looks like a small book, about 15 cm by 20 cm, and was sold as a “Calculating Book for Multiplication and Division of Multidigit Numbers”. It is very easy to make using an ordinary notebook.

Napier’s bones were much in demand in their time.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-9" %}

## Historical background {#section-1}

Napier was probably familiar with the multiplication method described as *gelosia* in Luca Pacioli’s well-known medieval treatise *Summa de arithmetica*. The Indians had known it long before Pacioli as lattice multiplication. The outstanding Arab mathematician al-Khwarizmi also described it in his *Book on Indian Calculation*.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-10" %}

The method works as follows. A counting board, or simply a blank sheet of paper, is ruled into a grid of rectangles divided by diagonals. The factors are written along the top and right sides of the grid, and the intermediate products placed in the rectangles so that the diagonal separates the units and tens, usually with tens in the upper triangle and units in the lower one. Add the numbers along the diagonals. The example is 23 × 54 = 1242.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-11" %}

According to Pacioli, the written calculations resemble latticed window shutters that hide women sitting at the windows from passers-by. Such shutters were called *gelosia*, or blinds, in connection with the word’s other meaning: jealousy.

Lattice multiplication is also known as the Italian method, the Chinese method, the Chinese lattice, gelosia multiplication, sieve multiplication, *shabakh*, and Venetian squares.
