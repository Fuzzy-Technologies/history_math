A surveyor's level, whose Russian name *nivelir* derives from French *niveler*, “to level,” is a surveying instrument for determining the difference in elevation between two points in a vertical plane.

When choosing plots of land, we need to know the shape of the terrain. This calls for an elevation survey, or levelling. A level is an essential instrument for professional surveyors and builders. It is used in design, structural installation and levelling large areas.

By accuracy, levels are classified as high-precision, precision or engineering instruments.

The simplest level is an optical instrument consisting of a tubular bubble level, a magnifying telescope and a line of sight. Its construction is shown in the following diagram.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The levelling screws in the base set up the instrument; the tilting screw brings the line of sight precisely horizontal when a reading is taken. The straight line joining the optical centre of the objective lens to the intersection of the crosshairs is called the telescope's line of sight.

Modern optical levels have a compensator, a device that automatically sets the instrument's line of sight horizontally. Most also have a circular spirit level for approximate levelling.

A level can be mounted on a tripod with a graduated circle, allowing approximate measurement of horizontal angles.

Engineering levelling usually uses long, three- or four-metre staffs with alternating marked blocks. They may be one-piece or folding and made from wood or aluminium. The scale numbers may be upright or inverted: older levels produced an inverted image.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Centimetre and decimetre divisions are painted in different colours on the broad faces of the staff. The other side is graduated in inches.

All optical levels have stadia hairs for determining distances from staff readings. A trained operator normally adjusts the telescope, using the position of the object being surveyed as a guide.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

Alongside optical levels, digital levels have become widespread in recent years. They use a special barcoded staff, making automated readings possible. Digital levels usually allow observation results to be saved.

Laser instruments differ considerably from optical and digital levels. They work by projecting laser beams.
