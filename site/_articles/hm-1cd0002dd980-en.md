*Marking the 200th anniversary of his birth*

Bernhard Riemann (Georg Friedrich Bernhard Riemann, 17 September 1826–20 July 1866) was a German mathematician and physicist whose ideas radically changed the language of analysis, geometry and number theory. His name is now linked to the geometry of space, the distribution of prime numbers and the way we understand integration itself.

## Education and Early Research

He was born in Breselenz, in the Kingdom of Hanover, to a Lutheran pastor. Initially intending to study theology, he soon turned to mathematics at the University of Göttingen. It was not computational technique that drew him, but the underlying logic of constructions and proofs.

His studies and early research connected Göttingen and Berlin. In 1851 he defended his doctoral dissertation on complex function theory at Göttingen under Carl Friedrich Gauss. These early works already contained themes that would later become familiar mathematical perspectives: Riemann surfaces, a careful union of analysis and geometry, and a desire to see a function as an object living on its natural space.

## Geometry and Göttingen

His habilitation, a higher academic qualification, in 1854 was a turning point. At Gauss’s request, Riemann delivered the lecture *On the Hypotheses Which Lie at the Bases of Geometry* (*Über die Hypothesen, welche der Geometrie zu Grunde liegen*), which became a mathematical classic. He proposed thinking of space as a manifold with a metric: distance is determined by a rule, and curvature can be a property of the space itself rather than a defect. From this grew Riemannian geometry, a language essential to modern differential geometry and mathematical formulations of general relativity.

Riemann began lecturing in Göttingen in 1854, became an extraordinary professor in 1857 and succeeded Dirichlet in the chair in 1859. From the outside this looks like a steady academic career, but illness continually intervened. Tuberculosis forced him to seek milder weather and undertake extended journeys, particularly to Italy.

## Integration and Prime Numbers

Riemann published relatively few works, yet almost every one opened a new subject. In analysis, his name is linked to a rigorous formulation of integration. His research on representing functions by trigonometric series established what we now call the Riemann integral: a clear criterion for discussing the area under a curve without vague qualifications. This became a foundation for greater rigour in analysis and for the theory of Fourier series.

In number theory, the pivotal work was his short 1859 paper *On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude* (*Über die Anzahl der Primzahlen unter einer gegebenen Grösse*), published in the *Monatsberichte der Berliner Akademie*. In a paper written in 1737, Euler related the corresponding series for real arguments to a product over primes. In his own paper, Riemann analytically continued the zeta function to the complex plane except at $s=1$, where it has a simple pole, and linked it to the distribution of primes. These ideas laid the groundwork for modern analytic number theory. In the same paper, Riemann conjectured that every nontrivial zero of the zeta function $\zeta(s)$ has real part $1/2$. This remains one of mathematics’ central open questions.

## Final Years and Legacy

Riemann’s private life was quiet. He married in 1862, and he and his wife had a daughter. In the summer of 1866, while in Italy, he died in Selasca on Lake Maggiore, not yet forty. His early death did not prevent his ideas from spreading: much of mathematics in the later nineteenth century and throughout the twentieth developed through Riemannian approaches.

Riemann is remembered for a rare ability to ask the right questions. What is space if it can be curved? How can quantities be measured when an object is infinitely complex? Why are prime numbers connected with a function of a complex variable? These questions still sound contemporary, a working programme for those building the mathematics and physics of the future.
