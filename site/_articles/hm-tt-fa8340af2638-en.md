> “Mathematics is the language in which the book of nature is written.”  
> Galileo Galilei

Galileo was born on 15 February 1564 in the city of Pisa, Florence. An outstanding Italian scholar of mechanics, astronomer, mathematician and philosopher, he was one of the founders of exact natural science. Galileo Galilei vigorously advocated mathematical methods for studying natural phenomena and the unity of experiment and theory.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

He was one of the creators of the scientific revolution of the early modern period, which began a new phase in mathematical development: the mathematics of variable quantities. In the seventeenth century, laws expressed analytically as functional relationships between quantities that varied together came to the forefront of the mathematical and physical picture of the world. Measuring quantities and seeking laws expressed in algebraic formulas became central to physics. Mathematics consequently turned to the study of variables and functions as counterparts to mechanical motion and quantitative change in general. This created a need to study the rules for using infinitesimal quantities.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Galileo studied and worked at the University of Pisa, one of Europe’s oldest universities, and later at the University of Padua. In his famous *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, Ptolemaic and Copernican* (1632), he brilliantly developed Copernicus’s account of the Earth’s motion.

The work angered the Inquisition, leading to the book’s prohibition and demands that Galileo recant his teachings. He was known as an active supporter of the heliocentric system.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

In 1609, Galileo built the first telescope with his own hands. He discovered mountains on the Moon. The Milky Way resolved into individual stars. His discovery of four moons of Jupiter particularly astonished his contemporaries.

Galileo became Europe’s most famous scientist, honoured in odes comparing him with Columbus. He discovered the phases of Venus, sunspots, and subsequently the Sun’s rotation on its axis.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

Galileo was a founder of experimental physics. His work establishing the principles of mechanics was highly important. He discovered the law of inertia and the laws of free fall and pendulum oscillation. He calculated a projectile’s parabolic trajectory and found its height and range in terms of its initial speed and elevation angle. He also studied the motion of a body rolling down an inclined plane and the composition of motions and velocities. He thus initiated the new dynamics that Newton developed and completed. Galileo’s last book, *Discourses and Mathematical Demonstrations Relating to Two New Sciences* (1638), presents the foundations of kinematics and the strength of materials.

Galileo arrived at a mathematical study of motion and the relationships between distance, speed and acceleration. Studying accelerated motion led him to the concept of instantaneous velocity.

In mathematics, Galileo was a precursor to Cavalieri in the development of the calculus of indivisibles. Many scholars, beginning with Kepler and Galileo, contributed to the creation of infinitesimal analysis.

Galileo’s writings also contain elements of probability theory: *On the Outcomes of Dice Games*, 1655.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

Galileo Galilei died in his bed on 8 January 1642, in his 78th year. Pope Urban forbade his burial in the family vault in Florence. He was buried in Arcetri without honours, and the Pope also refused permission for a monument. In 1737, his remains were transferred, as he had requested, to the Basilica of Santa Croce, where he was ceremonially buried beside Michelangelo.

The moons of Jupiter that Galileo discovered, the CGS unit of acceleration called the “gal” (Gal), and the airport in Pisa are named after him. Einstein called him the “father of modern science”. His image appears on banknotes and coins.

In 1758, Pope Benedict XIV ordered works defending heliocentrism to be removed from the *Index of Forbidden Books*. In 1992, at a meeting of the Pontifical Academy of Sciences, Pope John Paul II officially acknowledged that the Inquisition had erred in 1633 by forcing the scholar to renounce Copernicus’s theory.

A well-known legend says that, after publicly recanting his teaching about the Earth’s motion at his Inquisition trial, Galileo declared: “And yet it moves!” There is, however, no evidence whatsoever for this account.
