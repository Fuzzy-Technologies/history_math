The fifteenth and sixteenth centuries entered the history of late medieval Europe as the Renaissance: the rebirth of the high level of culture achieved in the ancient world. This was the flourishing of the Renaissance in Italy. Natural science began to free itself from theology.

Luca Pacioli was an Italian mathematician and theologian, the leading European algebraist of the fifteenth century, and one of the founders of modern accounting principles and architectural proportion.

He was born around 1447 in Borgo Sansepolcro, Tuscany.

He studied and worked with the painter and scholar Piero della Francesca. As a young man, Luca moved to Venice and became tutor to the sons of the merchant Antonio Rompiasi. There he encountered both the most advanced mathematics of his time and principles of financial record-keeping. In 1470 he completed his first book, a *Textbook of Commercial Arithmetic*, written for his pupils.

Pacioli later took religious vows and became a Franciscan. He taught algebra and geometry in Perugia in 1477–1480 and later taught mathematics in other Italian cities. He collaborated with Leonardo da Vinci and became his friend.

Renaissance mathematicians achieved their greatest successes in algebra. Pacioli’s principal treatise was the encyclopedic *Summa of Arithmetic, Geometry, Ratios and Proportionality* (1494), one of the earliest printed mathematics books. It brought together a substantial part of the knowledge then available in arithmetic, algebra and practical geometry. Written in Italian, it helped spread Hindu-Arabic numerals.

Pacioli called algebra *arte maggiore*, the great art, a name that continued to appear later. His important innovation was the systematic use of syncopated, or shortened, algebraic notation, a forerunner of symbolic calculation. He used a system of abbreviated algebraic notation: the unknown was `co`, from *cosa* (thing); its square was `ce`, from *censo* (square); and its cube was `cu`, from *cubo* (cube).

Under da Vinci’s influence, Pacioli wrote *Divine Proportion* (1509), named after the golden ratio. He sought to derive the proportions of the human figure and architectural canons. He did not invent double-entry bookkeeping, but his *Treatise on Accounts and Records* gave its first printed description, setting out a method already used by Venetian merchants. Some of his works concern chess theory, including a *Treatise on the Game of Chess*.

The Franciscan order was officially mendicant, and when Luca Pacioli became Fra Luca di Borgo, he took a vow of poverty. There is deep symbolism in this act: the father of accounting takes a vow of selflessness and poverty on behalf of himself and his future colleagues.
