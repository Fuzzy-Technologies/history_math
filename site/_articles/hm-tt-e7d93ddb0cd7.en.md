An anaglyph (from the Greek αναγλνφον, meaning “relief” or “bas-relief”) is a stereoscopic drawing. Unlike an ordinary drawing, it consists of two parts (drawings) superimposed on one another and printed in two different colours, pale red and pale green. An anaglyph is viewed through special glasses with coloured filters (stereoscopic glasses), usually red for the left eye and blue-green for the right.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Under familiar laws of physics and physiology, each eye sees only one of the two images, because each filter in the glasses absorbs the other image. When the anaglyph is viewed with both eyes together (binocularly), the two images therefore merge into a single stereoscopic, three-dimensional image.

Anaglyphs are used for illustrations in solid geometry and crystallography, for three-dimensional animation and stereoscopic films, and for three-dimensional terrain models.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

In 1963, V. G. Vladimirsky’s album *Stereometric Drawings for Geometry* was published in two parts as a teaching aid for senior secondary-school pupils. The first part illustrates A. P. Kiselev’s solid-geometry textbook in its 1951 and later editions. The second contains diagrams for problems by N. A. Rybkin included in the 1955 and later editions of his *Collection of Geometry Problems*.

Today, special computer programs can simulate the use of coloured filters. Tools for creating anaglyphs are available, for example, in the popular professional software Adobe Photoshop. We used this method to make our own anaglyph, shown at the beginning of the article.
