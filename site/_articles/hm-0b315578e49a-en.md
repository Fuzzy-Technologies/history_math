Eratosthenes (276–194 BC) was one of those rare people who managed to be a poet, philologist, geographer, astronomer and mathematician — all in the age of the Library of Alexandria.

But history has a sense of humour. The Byzantine *Suda*, a tenth-century lexicon preserving ancient traditions and nicknames, says that Eratosthenes was called “Βῆτα” (Beta), or “second”, because in every field he fell a little short of those at the top.

“Because he came second in every branch of learning… he was nicknamed Beta.”

That chimes nicely with Strabo’s barbed description:

“…he is a mathematician among geographers, and yet a geographer among mathematicians…”

There is, however, one small detail: the “eternal runner-up” did the things people have remembered first for two thousand years. He measured the Earth using shadows in Alexandria and Syene, and gave us the famous sieve of Eratosthenes for finding primes.
