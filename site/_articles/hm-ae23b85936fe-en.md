*Marking the 290th anniversary of his birth*

Edward Waring was an English mathematician who researched number theory and algebraic equations.

He was born in 1734, or possibly 1736, in Shrewsbury, England.

He studied at Magdalene College, Cambridge. In 1760 he became a professor at Cambridge University. He became a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1763.

## Waring’s Problem

In 1770 he published *Meditationes algebraicae*, a second edition of the work first published in 1762 as *Miscellanea analytica*. In it he formulated a conjecture that became known as Waring’s problem. It became one of the well-known problems of number theory and a subject of research for mathematicians in many countries.

The general formulation asks whether, for every positive integer $n$, there is a number $k = g(n)$ such that every positive integer $N$ is a sum of at most $k$ terms that are $n$th powers of positive integers. As a conjecture, it can also be stated as follows: every positive integer $N$ can be represented as a sum of $k$ $n$th powers of nonnegative integers (1770).

$$
N = x_1^n + x_2^n + \cdots + x_k^n
$$

Until the twentieth century, the problem could be solved only in special cases. The function $g(n)$ was introduced by the English mathematicians Godfrey Harold Hardy and John Edensor Littlewood.

For example, $g(2) = 4$: every positive integer can be represented as a sum of at most four squares. Lagrange proved the four-square theorem in 1770.

Likewise, $g(3) = 9$: every positive integer can be represented as a sum of at most nine cubes.

## The General Solution and Later Research

The well-known German mathematician David Hilbert first solved Waring’s problem using sophisticated analytic methods in 1909. The Soviet mathematician Yuri Vladimirovich Linnik (1914–1972) found a proof based on elementary methods in 1942.

Research into Waring’s problem led to the development of powerful analytic methods. The solution of the problem in its original formulation was completed only in 1984 with the use of computers.
