*On the 220th anniversary of Russian higher and secondary education*

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-1" %}

The 300th anniversary of the Russian Academy of Sciences, founded in 1724, is a suitable occasion to recall other anniversaries that receive little attention.

The year 2024 marks the 220th anniversary of Russian higher and secondary, or gymnasium, education, and of the awarding of academic degrees in our country.

The state reforms of the early nineteenth century also affected education. In Russia, they were carried out by the government of Alexander I, who came to the throne in 1801. In 1802 the “Ministry of Public Education, the Upbringing of Youth and the Dissemination of the Sciences” was created. Six educational districts were established, each headed by a university. Universities already existed in three: Moscow, since 1755; Vilna, established in 1803; and Dorpat, established in 1803. Universities were founded in Kazan and Kharkiv in 1804; Kharkiv University officially opened in 1805. A Pedagogical Institute was established in St Petersburg and became a university in 1819.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

A Main Board of Schools was created within the ministry. Under a decree it prepared in 1803, “for the moral education of citizens, in accordance with the duties and benefits of each estate, four kinds of educational institution are designated: 1) parish schools, 2) district schools, 3) provincial schools, or gymnasiums, 4) universities.” The “Statute of Universities of the Russian Empire” and the “Statute of Educational Institutions Subordinate to Universities” were adopted in 1804.

The university statute added a Faculty of Physics and Mathematics to the three existing faculties. A university thus had four: “moral and political sciences, physical and mathematical sciences, medical sciences, and the humanities.” Previously, selected higher-mathematics courses had been taught only in certain professional institutions: Moscow University and military, naval and engineering schools. They had no mathematics departments and did not train mathematicians of the highest qualifications. Moscow University was Russia's only functioning university; its Physics and Mathematics division also opened in 1804. The year 1804 is therefore regarded as the birth of higher mathematical education, with universities as its cradle.

In the first third of the nineteenth century, Russian university rectors were often mathematicians, who organized and supervised mathematics education, above all at school level. Examples include T. F. Osipovsky at Kharkiv University, 1813-1820; N. I. Lobachevsky at Kazan University, 1827-1846; and D. M. Perevoshchikov at Moscow University, 1848-1851.

Under the statute, universities headed educational districts overseen by trustees. Trustees were responsible for opening a university or reorganizing an existing one on new principles, and for directing educational institutions through the university rector. Professors elected the rector at a general meeting. Subordinate to the trustee, the rector headed the university and managed the district's educational institutions. Rectors were responsible for training and selecting administrative and teaching staff for parish schools, district schools and gymnasiums, and for supervising their work.

The academic degrees of Candidate, Master and Doctor were introduced for the first time. The academic titles of adjunct and academician, and the title of professor, had been awarded since the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences was created in 1725. Thus 2004 can also be considered the 220th anniversary of the awarding of academic degrees in Russia.

The year 1804 can also justifiably be regarded as the birth of gymnasium education in Russia. Gymnasiums had existed earlier, at the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences and Moscow University and in some provincial towns, including Kazan since 1758. They were not, however, united in a single network under common direction. Only in 1804, under the “Statute of Educational Institutions Subordinate to Universities,” did gymnasiums officially become the principal type of secondary school. They admitted “pupils of every rank who have completed their studies in district schools or other educational institutions, or at home, provided they have sufficient knowledge to continue the subjects taught in gymnasiums.” An important feature of the new system was continuity: a pupil could move from district school to gymnasium, and gymnasium preparation was sufficient to begin university studies.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Interestingly, unlike today, a teacher was considered a state official, whose rank depended on the importance of the subject taught. Junior teachers included drawing teachers, in the twelfth class, and language teachers, in the tenth. Teachers of other subjects, including mathematics, belonged to the ninth class of state officials and were called senior teachers.

Universities thus became both the cradle of Russian higher education and the organizers of primary and secondary education.

In Yelabuga, a district school opened in 1809 in accordance with the statute of 1804. It was a two-class school intended to “provide children of different estates with necessary knowledge.”

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

Thus, in 1804 the first step was taken toward a system of higher and secondary mathematical education in Russia. The two centuries that followed showed that our system of mathematics education was among the world's best.
