> “Mathematics should be studied if only because it puts the mind in order.”

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

> “Anyone who does not know mathematics cannot learn any other science, or even discover their own ignorance.”

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Everyone who attends school studies mathematics, or more precisely its branches: arithmetic, algebra, geometry, trigonometry and probability. Yet school does not examine what kind of science it is. Ask a nonspecialist, and they will begin explaining how useful it is, but give no clear definition.

Is such a definition even possible? Mathematics teachers, at least, should understand that only a descriptive definition can be given. Whatever definition we offer, aspects of the subject always lie beyond it. The question of what mathematics ultimately is remains open. Nevertheless, let us try to understand and explain it.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

Scientific and educational literature offers many different definitions.

> 1\. Academician A. N. Kolmogorov's celebrated article “Mathematics” (1954) in the *Great Soviet Encyclopedia* begins with this definition of its subject: “Pure mathematics has as its object the spatial forms and quantitative relations of the real world, and thus very real material.” He also emphasizes that the stock of quantitative relations and spatial forms studied in mathematics continually expands in response to the needs of natural science, enriching this definition's content.

> 2\. Mathematics, from ancient Greek μάθημα, “learning” or “science”, is the science of structures, order and relations, historically arising from counting, measuring and describing the shapes of objects.

> 3\. Mathematics is a fundamental science that supplies other sciences with a general language.

> 4\. In everyday speech, “mathematics” means precise calculation and carefully planned actions required for some purpose.

Mathematics as a deductive science, founded on proof, arose in ancient Greece from the sixth century BCE.

Mathematics belongs neither to the natural sciences nor to the humanities, but is widely used in both to formulate their content precisely and obtain new results. Mathematical objects are created by idealizing the properties of real or other ideal objects and recording them in a formal language. Mathematics thereby reveals their structural interconnections and helps discover the most general laws of nature.

Mathematics serves all the other natural sciences. Today, at any rate, it exists as a distinct field, independent of other sciences and of its applications.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

Traditionally, mathematics is divided into pure mathematics, which deeply analyses internal mathematical structures, and applied mathematics, which supplies models to other sciences and engineering disciplines. Some disciplines occupy a boundary with mathematics. Formal logic, for instance, can be considered part of both philosophy and mathematics; mechanics belongs to both physics and mathematics; and computer science, computing technologies and algorithm theory belong to both engineering and mathematics.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

As a subject of instruction in Russia, mathematics is divided into elementary mathematics, studied at secondary school, and higher mathematics, studied in nonmathematical university programmes. The higher mathematics taught in mathematics programmes differs from this in both content and method.

Historically, “elementary mathematics” means the mathematics of constant quantities created between the sixth–fifth centuries BCE and the end of the sixteenth century CE. The period of higher mathematics, from the early seventeenth to the mid-nineteenth century, was the period in which the mathematics of variable quantities developed.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

Mathematics differs from other sciences in that its final conclusions are unconditional. The theories and concepts of physics, for example, depend on when they arose, the scientist who established them, experimental conditions and many other circumstances. Mathematical statements exist in their own right: only the logical soundness of their derivation matters. In the natural sciences, approaches repeatedly change; things appear in a completely new light, and old ideas are abandoned or fundamentally revised. This does not happen in mathematics. A proved mathematical statement always remains true. A new theory can only add something new to it.

Many people think mathematics is merely arithmetic: the study of numbers and operations on them. It is actually much more. It is a way to describe the world and how one part relates to another. Relationships between numbers are expressed in mathematical symbols that describe the universe we inhabit.

The famous German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss, known as the “king of mathematics”, once said: *“Mathematics is the queen of the sciences, and arithmetic is the queen of mathematics.”*

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-8" %}

“Arithmetic” comes from the Greek ἀριθμός, “number”. It is therefore the branch of mathematics studying numbers and operations on them. Any normally developing child can succeed in arithmetic, because “number sense” is an innate ability. Some effort and time are, of course, required.

Being able to calculate is not enough. A child must also express their thoughts well in order to understand problems and connect facts stored in memory. Numbers are only part of a special mathematical language, and the best way to learn any language is to use it.

Mathematics uses the method of abstraction. By what laws is it constructed? Here we must turn to the laws of thought. Consider the following analogy.

Children's games devised by adults and scientific theories follow the same laws of our thinking. Aristotle already used these laws to describe scientific theories. Mathematical theories are constructed by them too.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-9" %}

Let us follow these rules in an example.

I. Chess

II. Mathematics

1\. First, introduce primitive concepts without definitions:

- Knight, bishop, rook, ...
- Point, straight line, plane, number, ...

2\. Then establish the rules of the game, or axioms:

- The knight moves in an L shape. The rook moves along ranks and files. ...
- Through any two points there is one and only one straight line. For every natural number, there is a next natural number. ...

3\. Define new, derived concepts using the primitive ones:

- Check, checkmate, castling, gambit, ...
- Segment, ray, triangle, circle, zero, ...

4\. Prove properties of the primitive and new concepts — theorems — using the axioms and previously proved theorems:

- How should one play to win? If checkmate is possible in a position, it can be written down and carried out. For example, in an endgame a king supported by a rook wins against a lone king. Chess truth does not depend on the board or player. ...
- Can a statement be proved? For example, if a straight line passing through none of a triangle's vertices intersects one side, it intersects exactly one of the other two sides. ...

Mathematical truths do not depend on personalities or languages. Axioms and theorems outwardly share the form “if …, then …”. Theorems, however, are proved. They carry an additional question: “Why?” Mathematics requires a complete argument.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-10" %}

A famous example of an axiomatic theory is Isaac Newton's principal work, *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*. It begins with definitions and axioms about the world: What is force? What is mass? What are the fundamental laws of mechanics? A model of the world is derived from them by strict deduction.

In Newton's time, mathematics developed empirically in response to the need to solve emerging physical problems. Newton himself discovered infinitesimal calculus as a means of describing and investigating the motion of celestial bodies.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-11" %}

How much mathematics does a person need? Do we really need quadratic equations, function graphs and integrals? Is it not enough to calculate and know the multiplication table?

1\. Mathematics helps solve concrete real-world problems, from mental calculation at a shop counter to long-term financial plans such as buying a house. If you cannot assess the size of a debt, it becomes a dangerous gamble.

2\. Mathematics develops the intellect. Solving mathematical problems requires persistence and creativity.

3\. The world is organized according to mathematical principles. Anyone seeking to understand its underlying nature must be familiar with numbers, geometrical figures and probability.

Unfortunately, teaching in today's schools is limited to technical calculation procedures. Students trained in this way often never encounter the essence of mathematics.

> *“Mathematics is compared to a great branching tree that continually puts out new shoots. Each branch is a field of mathematics.”*
>
> — B. V. Gnedenko, Soviet mathematician and specialist in probability theory and mathematical statistics.

> *“So what is mathematics? First, it is one of the oldest sciences. Second, it is a special artificial language and a system for operating with that language. Third, it is a distinctive world based on an abstract model of particular properties of the surrounding world, chiefly its quantitative and spatial forms, but also containing its own concepts with no counterparts in the external world.”*  
> — L. M. Fridman, a well-known specialist in educational and mathematical psychology.
