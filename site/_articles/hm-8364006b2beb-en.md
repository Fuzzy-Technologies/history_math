*Marking the 160th anniversary of his birth*

Vladimir Akimovich, also known as Ioakimovich, Zimmerman (15 [27] August 1866–17 November 1939) was a Russian mathematician and educator, a professor at the Imperial Novorossiysk University in Odessa and editor of one of Russia’s first popular physics and mathematics journals, the *Herald of Experimental Physics and Elementary Mathematics*.

## Odessa: University and School

He was born in Odessa to Akim Konstantinovich Zimmerman, a lawyer and headmaster of the city’s Second Gymnasium. His family environment was academic in both its educational background and its attention to schools. In 1888–1894 Vladimir studied at Novorossiysk University under Ivan Vladislavovich Sleshinsky.

For almost a quarter of a century he combined university work with school teaching: from 1891 to 1914 he taught in Odessa’s schools, colleges and gymnasiums. This matters to understanding his role. Zimmerman was a university mathematician who also saw, day after day, how pupils learned mathematics, which mistakes recurred and which explanations actually worked.

## Editing a Mathematical Journal

That experience helps explain his involvement in the *Herald of Experimental Physics and Elementary Mathematics*, commonly abbreviated in Russian as V.O.F.E.M. The journal began in Kyiv as a continuation of V. P. Ermakov’s *Journal of Elementary Mathematics* and moved to Odessa with its editor-in-chief in 1891. It appeared twenty-four times a year and survived until 1917, producing 674 issues of problems, solutions, articles and teaching notes: a remarkable educational forum for its time.

By the late 1890s Zimmerman had joined the editorial staff of V.O.F.E.M., and in 1898–1904 he headed the journal. Sources record that Veniamin Fyodorovich Kagan became involved in the early 1900s, partly from 1902 and fully from 1904. This, too, indicates the editorial team’s calibre: the journal gathered a community in which school mathematics, university research and public explanation of science belonged together.

## Mathematics and the Culture of Proof

Zimmerman’s university career advanced rapidly. He became an extraordinary professor in 1901, then an ordinary professor in the Department of Pure Mathematics in 1903, receiving the degree of Doctor of Pure Mathematics. He held the civil-service rank of State Councillor, a typical link between academic standing and government service among professors of the period.

His publications included the separate work *Euler’s Rule Applied to a Class of Problems on Relative Maxima and Minima* (1899). Yet for readers of his journal, another quality matters more: his articles often unfold as lessons, developing a result carefully rather than announcing it at once. V.O.F.E.M. includes, for example, *On the Volume of a Sphere, Spherical Segment and Spherical Layer* (1908), and a 1913 note on the commutative property of multiplication in the series *From a Teacher’s Notebook*. These subjects were no accident: solid figures, properties of operations and simple methods of proof help teachers build understanding rather than drill.

After the revolution he continued teaching, working at the Odessa Institute of Public Education in 1920–1924 before losing his post in staff reductions. His family history also shows the continuity of an academic environment: his brother Pavel worked in gymnasium education, while his son Nikolai Vladimirovich Zimmerman became a well-known astronomer.

Less is known about Vladimir Zimmerman’s final decades than about his journal and university years. His contribution can nevertheless be read in the tradition of mathematical literacy sustained by publications such as V.O.F.E.M., and in the bridge between school, practice and the university culture of proof. Sometimes it is people like him who keep mathematics alive as a language rather than a collection of rules.
