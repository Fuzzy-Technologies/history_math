The last period of ancient society was the period of Roman dominance. In the third century BC a strong, enduring state took shape in ancient Rome.

Early Roman culture was greatly influenced by the highly developed culture of the Greeks. Yet Greek culture and science began to decline in the Roman period. Alexandria remained a centre of ancient mathematics for a long time, and Rome could not rival it in that respect. Scholars, however, were mainly occupied with commenting on earlier works. The ancient Romans became better known for legislation and legal scholarship.

Roman inheritance laws supplied subjects for arithmetic exercises. Here is one that has come down to us.

“A dying Roman left his wife expecting a child. His will directed that, if a son was born, the son should receive $2/3$ of the estate and the mother $1/3$. If a daughter was born, she should receive $1/3$ and the mother $2/3$. The widow, however, gave birth to twins, a boy and a girl. How should the estate be divided to satisfy the will?”

This is sometimes called the “legal case” problem. The old legal puzzle is considered to have more than one solution.

The $4:2:1$ solution appears under the Roman jurist Salvius Julianus in [Justinian’s *Digest* (D. 28.2.13)](https://droitromain.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/Corpus/d-28.htm). Julianus justified the division by the testator’s intention and considerations of fairness.

## Solution: first interpretation

Julianus took the essential point of the testator’s intention to be the ratio between the mother’s share, $m$, and the shares of the son, $s$, and the daughter, $d$.

According to the will, the mother should receive twice as much as the daughter, and the son twice as much as the mother. The shares should therefore be in this ratio:

$s:m:d=4:2:1$.

The estate must be divided into seven equal parts: four for the son, two for the mother and one for the daughter.

Another interpretation of the will can ensure that the mother receives at least one third of the estate.

Can you find this solution too?

## Notes

The photograph shows Lucius Octavius Cornelius Publius Salvius Julianus Aemilianus (100–170), a Roman jurist. His statue stands at the Palace of Justice in Rome, Italy, in the Prati district. Publius Salvius Julianus belonged to the emperor’s council and held the offices of praetor, consul and urban prefect. As a legal scholar he is chiefly known for his *Digesta*, an outstanding Roman legal collection in 90 books, rich in case law and a literary monument. Julianus combined extensive practical knowledge with wit and ingenuity in settling disputed questions of law.

### Solution: second interpretation

Julianus’s solution can be regarded as unfair to the mother.

The will can also be read as intending the mother to receive at least $1/3$ of the estate, whereas the Roman jurist’s solution gives her only $2/7$, less than $1/3$. So give the mother $1/3$ and divide the remaining $2/3$ between son and daughter in the ratio $4:1$. The son then receives $8/15$ and the daughter $2/15$ of the whole estate, giving:

$s:m:d=8:5:2$.

### Solution: third interpretation

The third interpretation adopts an independent rule: the two cases contemplated by the will are treated equally. Divide the estate notionally into two equal halves and apply the corresponding instructions separately to each half.

- The son receives $\frac12\times\frac23=\frac13$ of the estate.
- The daughter receives $\frac12\times\frac13=\frac16$.
- The mother receives $\frac12\times\frac23+\frac12\times\frac13=\frac12$.

The son receives twice as much as the daughter, as the testator may have wished. He does not receive twice as much as the mother, but she still has two children to bring up.

The shares are then in this ratio:

$s:m:d=2:3:1$.
