*Marking the 230th anniversary of his birth*

The first members of the Kazan school of mathematics were all pupils of the renowned German scholar Martin Bartels. After Nikolai Lobachevsky, Bartels named Ivan Mikhailovich Simonov as the second representative of the University’s mathematical school. For about fifty years, the two scholars and educators worked side by side for the flourishing of Kazan University.

Simonov was a Russian astronomer, one of the discoverers of the southern polar continent, Antarctica, in 1820, and Rector of Imperial Kazan University in 1846–1855. He became a corresponding member of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences in 1829 and belonged to fifteen foreign academies and scientific societies.

## Education and Early Research

He was born on 20 June 1794, or 1 July in the Gregorian calendar, in Astrakhan.

He attended Astrakhan Provincial Gymnasium until 1808, then Kazan Gymnasium in 1808–1809.

He studied at Imperial Kazan University in 1809–1810.

Ivan Simonov had not intended to devote his life to physics or astronomy: he planned to enter the literature division. Professor Martin Bartels, Lobachevsky’s teacher, quite literally persuaded him to take up the natural sciences.

He sat the examinations directly for the degree of magister of physical and mathematical sciences, submitting “On the Attraction of Homogeneous Spheroids”, a work explaining Laplace’s *Celestial Mechanics*.

Simonov’s practical work in astronomy began with observations of the Great Comet of 1811. Lobachevsky, then a magister of mathematics, also took part under Professor Littrow, who had founded the astronomy department. Their results appeared in *Kazan News* in September 1811.

Simonov received his magister degree in 1812 and entered educational service, where he remained for more than forty-two years. In 1814 he was appointed an adjunct in the astronomy department. In 1816, after Professor Littrow left Kazan, he became an extraordinary professor in two departments of Kazan University: theoretical astronomy and practical astronomy.

In 1816–1818 he visited St Petersburg to acquaint himself with the Academy’s observatory.

## The Circumnavigation

In 1819, at the Academy of Sciences’ suggestion, Simonov, who had just completed thorough practical training, was appointed astronomer on a circumnavigation to the Southern Hemisphere aboard the sloops *Vostok* and *Mirny*. The expedition, commanded by Faddey Bellingshausen and Mikhail Lazarev, left Kronstadt in July 1819 and returned in 1821.

Simonov became the first Russian astronomer to circumnavigate the globe. He was the first Russian astronomer to observe southern stars that are never visible from Russia. He was the expedition’s only scientist. From astronomical observations he determined the positions of the ships and newly charted islands; from magnetic data he calculated the position of Earth’s South Magnetic Pole. He also made meteorological, hydrogeological, magnetic, ethnographic and zoological observations and investigations, assembling many kinds of collections.

The natural-history and ethnographic collections he assembled in the southern polar region and on Pacific islands were presented to Kazan University, enriching its geological, zoological and ethnographic museums. Simonov left a detailed account of the voyage. His investigations were of great practical value to navigators and important to several fields of knowledge.

For his part in the voyage, Emperor Alexander I awarded him the Order of St Anne, second class.

## Developing Astronomy in Kazan

After returning to Kazan, he was confirmed as a full professor of astronomy in 1822. He was dean of the University’s Faculty of Physics and Mathematics in 1822–1823 and 1825–1830.

In 1823 he was sent to Europe with Professor A. Ya. Kupffer to purchase astronomical and physical instruments. He visited major scientific centres in the German lands, the Austrian Empire, France, Italy and Switzerland.

In 1828 he travelled through much of Kazan Governorate and parts of Simbirsk and Orenburg governorates, using astronomical observations to establish precise geographical coordinates for many towns.

Simonov was one of the first people in Russia to study terrestrial magnetism.

He played a major role in astronomy in Kazan and Russia. At his initiative, Kazan established two observatories: an astronomical observatory, which he directed in 1838–1855, and a magnetic observatory in 1843. In November 1846 he became the first Russian astronomer to observe the newly discovered planet Neptune. He made meteorological observations and observed star clusters, Uranus and Ceres.

From 1846 until his death in 1855, Simonov was Rector of Kazan University, succeeding Lobachevsky.

## Publications and Commemoration

His publications include these Russian works:

- *Astronomical and Physical Observations Made during a Voyage around the World* (1828);
- *An Essay towards a Mathematical Theory of Terrestrial Magnetism* (1835).

One of the Pacific islands bears his name. The map of Antarctica includes two Cape Simonovs, the Simonov Hills and Mount Kazan.
