*Marking the 155th anniversary of his birth*

Ernst Zermelo (27 July 1871–21 May 1953) was a German mathematician and one of the principal architects of axiomatic set theory. His name stands at the origins of the system of axioms that developed into the modern foundations of mathematics, ZF and ZFC, and is also associated with the axiom of choice and the well-ordering theorem.

## From Physics to the Foundations of Mathematics

He was born in Berlin and studied in Berlin, Halle and Freiburg, with interests extending beyond mathematics to physics and philosophy. In 1894 he earned his doctorate at the University of Berlin with a dissertation on the calculus of variations, and worked in theoretical physics for several years. In 1896 he entered into a scientific debate with Boltzmann about statistical physics and the paradoxes of irreversibility. His path towards the foundations of mathematics thus began far from pure abstraction.

By the early twentieth century, Zermelo was in Göttingen, alongside David Hilbert and a new circle of ideas about the foundations of mathematics. In 1904 he published a short but epoch-making paper, *Proof That Every Set Can Be Well-Ordered* (*Beweis, daß jede Menge wohlgeordnet werden kann*). Within the proof he effectively introduced the axiom of choice, a principle needed to justify selecting one element from each set beyond the assumptions previously accepted. The paper launched a major debate over existence without explicit construction and the limits of proof.

## Axioms and an Academic Career

The next step was more consequential still. In 1908 Zermelo published *Untersuchungen über die Grundlagen der Mengenlehre. I*, offering the first axiomatization of set theory designed to avoid the paradoxes of the naive approach. His idea was simple and powerful: sets cannot be assembled arbitrarily; permissible operations and restrictions must be stated explicitly. This produced a system that, after refinements by Abraham Fraenkel and Thoralf Skolem, developed into standard ZF. In modern notation, ZFC means ZF together with the axiom of choice, which was already included in Zermelo’s 1908 system.

Zermelo’s life reminds us that fundamental mathematics is not made only in a university office. In 1910 he took a chair in Zurich, but tuberculosis forced him to leave it in 1916 and settle in Freiburg. That same year he received the Ackermann–Teubner Memorial Award for his mathematical achievements. In 1926 he became an honorary professor at the University of Freiburg and remained connected with the city for the rest of his life.

## Chess, Navigation and Infinity

He also worked between disciplines. In 1913 Zermelo presented a paper on chess, considering the existence of winning strategies in finite games with perfect information; this became an early source for the future theory of games. Later, in 1930–1931, he formulated the navigation problem—finding the best way to steer in a wind or current—as a problem in the calculus of variations. Today this subject is associated with optimal control and geometry.

Zermelo did not seek sweeping philosophical declarations. His legacy lies in a rigorous account of foundations and rules that helped avoid specific paradoxes while allowing set theory to develop. When we speak of the ZFC axioms, we are continuing the conversation he began in 1908: which assumptions make infinity manageable?
