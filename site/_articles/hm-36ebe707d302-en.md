*Marking the 590th anniversary of his birth*

Regiomontanus, Johann Müller (6 June 1436–6 July 1476), was a German astronomer and mathematician of the early Renaissance who helped make trigonometry an independent and rigorously presented tool. His tables, books and calculations served astronomers, cartographers and navigators for decades.

## Education and Ancient Manuscripts

He was born in Königsberg in Franconia, now in Bavaria, not the Königsberg in Prussia. His education began remarkably early: he entered Leipzig University at eleven, then continued in Vienna, where Georg von Peuerbach became his principal teacher. There he developed a habit unusual in the fifteenth century: testing astronomy through mathematics, and mathematics through observation.

After Peuerbach’s death he completed his teacher’s *Epitome of Ptolemy’s Almagest*. Rather than simply retell the ancient system, it carefully examined difficult passages and pointed out errors in common translations. At the same time, he collected Greek manuscripts, worked in Italy and gradually entered the circle of European humanist scholars.

## Triangles, Observation and Printed Science

His principal mathematical work is *De triangulis omnimodis*, *On Triangles of All Kinds*, one of Europe’s earliest systematic textbooks on trigonometry. It covers plane and spherical triangles, rules for solving them, tables and practical problems. In effect, this was one of the first works in Europe to present trigonometry as a unified language of measurement, from distances on Earth to astronomical calculations. Modern elementary trigonometry includes a law of tangents relating the ratio of the difference and sum of two sides to the ratio of the tangents of the half-difference and half-sum of the opposite angles, also known as Regiomontanus’s formula.

In Nuremberg he took another step that feels strikingly modern: he built an observatory and arranged the printing of scientific texts and tables. His *Ephemerides* supplied the positions of celestial bodies for years ahead, becoming working tools for astronomers and navigators. In the fifteenth century this was close to a revolution: knowledge could be rapidly shared as well as discovered.

In 1475 Pope Sixtus IV summoned him to Rome to help prepare a reform of the calendar. He died soon after arriving. More plans and manuscripts remained than he had managed to publish, yet what he achieved in forty years was enough to secure his place in the history of European mathematics.

He is remembered as a figure of transition: from manuscript learning to printed science, from scattered computational techniques to a rigorous textbook, and from abstraction to a working tool. In that sense, his trigonometry became more than a branch of mathematics: it became a language for a world understood through precision.
