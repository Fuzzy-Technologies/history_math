(On the occasion of his 115th birthday)

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-1" %}

Star formation in the Galaxy, which began several billion years ago, continues today. This fundamentally important discovery, like many other achievements in stellar cosmogony, belongs to the brilliant astronomer and founder of a school of theoretical astrophysics, Academician Viktor Amazaspovich Ambartsumian (1908–1996).

Two years of his life were connected with Yelabuga. In 1981, Ambartsumian wrote: *“I remember with great warmth the kindness of the people of Yelabuga towards the Leningrad group that I had to lead. It was truly a time of arduous feats and relationships of the highest moral quality.”*

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Today's Yelabuga Institute of Kazan Federal University can be proud of the many outstanding scholars who worked within its walls. Academician Ambartsumian was one of them. Already a recognised scholar of world renown, he visited Yelabuga in 1985. In a letter to the pedagogical institute's rector, T. N. Galiullin, he expressed thanks for organising a wonderful journey from Kazan to Yelabuga and for the attention shown by the institute and city authorities: *“I am also very grateful to the city's leaders for their gracious welcome in Yelabuga. I saw that Yelabuga leads an energetic life and that you all recognise its great future.”*

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

He also wrote: *“Unfortunately, my whole life goes like this: either I return from somewhere and make up for what I missed, or I prepare to leave somewhere and finish what I have left undone. In other words, I am a poor organiser even of my own affairs.”* Of course, this was not so: Viktor Amazaspovich was one of the foremost organisers of science. His finest qualities also emerged in his leadership of Leningrad State University's Yelabuga branch.

To save Leningrad State University's most important laboratories and scientific staff engaged in defence research, a research branch was to be established far behind the front. Its leadership was entrusted to Professor V. A. Ambartsumian, the university's vice-rector for research. At the rector's office, he was told: *“Viktor Amazaspovich! You are appointed head of the branch of Leningrad State University in Yelabuga, in the Tatar ASSR. You must immediately evacuate all staff, their family members, instruments and property there. An entire train is at your disposal: twenty-four freight wagons. You are in command of the train.”* They departed on 18 July 1941. About 70 research staff and eminent scholars arrived in Yelabuga, including one full member of the Academy, V. A. Fock; two corresponding members of the USSR Academy of Sciences, V. I. Smirnov and V. A. Ambartsumian; and more than a dozen professors from Leningrad University, among them M. A. Kovalev, V. V. Sobolev, B. P. Nikolsky, M. N. Tverskoy, V. V. Sharonov, E. F. Gross, S. F. Rodionov and V. N. Tsvetkov.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

Despite all the difficulties, the Leningrad scholars began work as early as September. Research was naturally their principal task, chiefly for defence and practical purposes. In particular, the theory of light scattering in turbid media was developed under Ambartsumian's direction, making a major contribution to science. Soviet submariners put it to practical use.

Ambartsumian later wrote: *“I am proud of the remarkable results achieved by the Yelabuga branch of Leningrad University. My warm thanks to you, the people of Yelabuga, for your assistance and attention to Soviet science.”* The government highly valued the branch's defence research. As a result of work carried out in Yelabuga during the war and in the first post-war years, several members of the group became full or corresponding members of the academies of sciences of the USSR and its constituent republics and received State Prizes: V. A. Fock, V. A. Ambartsumian, V. I. Smirnov, B. P. Nikolsky, V. N. Tsvetkov, N. P. Yerugin and others.

Academician V. V. Sobolev later wrote: *“It is interesting that those who had been in Yelabuga continued to work just as successfully in later years, even after being separated from one another <…>. V. A. Ambartsumian said this resulted from the strict selection before evacuation: mainly capable scholars came to Yelabuga <…>. But perhaps we should add that they all passed through the ‘Yelabuga school’ <…>. Yelabuga taught people to work.”* It was Ambartsumian who led these formidable people.

> V. A. Ambartsumian was born on 5 (18) September 1908 in Tbilisi.  
> In 1928, he graduated in astronomy from the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics of Leningrad State University.  
> Doctor of physical and mathematical sciences and professor (1935).  
> Corresponding member (1939) and full member (1953) of the USSR Academy of Sciences.  
> Director of Leningrad State University's Astronomical Observatory (1939–1941).  
> Vice-rector for research and head of the research branch in Yelabuga (1941–1943).  
> Full member of the Academy of Sciences of the Armenian SSR (1943).  
> Director of the Yerevan Astronomical Observatory (1944–1946).  
> Recipient of USSR State Prizes for the theory of light scattering in turbid media (1946), and for discovering and studying a new type of stellar system, “stellar associations” (1950).  
> Director of the Byurakan Astrophysical Observatory from 1946.  
> President of the Academy of Sciences of the Armenian SSR (1947).  
> President of the International Astronomical Union (1961–1964).  
> Hero of Socialist Labour (1968).  
> President of the International Council of Scientific Unions (1968–1972, Paris).  
> Member of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of Armenia from 1948.  
> Elected five times as a deputy to the Supreme Soviet of the USSR.  
> Honorary or foreign member of the academies of sciences of Austria, England, Belgium, Bulgaria, the GDR, Denmark, Italy, the USA, France and Sweden, and of many other academies and societies.

Viktor Amazaspovich's entire life was devoted to theoretical astrophysics and stellar astronomy. He achieved fundamental results and established new scientific fields.

His work is characterised by masterful use of theoretical physics and mathematics and by original interpretations and solutions of the problems considered. He could find the simplest solutions to the most complex physical problems. For example, the theory of light scattering in turbid media had previously led to complex integral equations that could be solved only approximately. Applying a simple invariance principle, Ambartsumian reduced the problem to a system of easily solved functional equations and obtained its exact solution. This principle has important practical applications in physics and geophysics. It was developed in Yelabuga in 1941–1943.

Ambartsumian wrote the first Russian-language textbook of *Theoretical Astrophysics* (1939). At the universities of Leningrad and Yerevan and at the Byurakan Observatory, which he founded and continuously directed, he trained a large group of astrophysicists.

He also wrote mathematical works on inverse problems in the spectral analysis of differential operators.

Ambartsumian's studies of galactic evolution yielded fundamental results concerning manifestations of physical instability, the existence of disintegrating systems of galaxies, and the role of galactic nuclei's activity in their formation. These studies replaced the old conception of a calm, unchanging Universe with that of a real, evolving one. They opened a window into infinity.

A scholar's scientific achievements may receive another form of recognition: coins are minted in their honour, and their image appears on national banknotes. This means that the person represents the whole nation as well as science, becoming one of its leaders. Ambartsumian was one such person.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

In 1998, a 100-dram banknote entered circulation in Ambartsumian's homeland, Armenia. The front depicts the great astrophysicist against a background of space; the reverse shows the Byurakan Observatory, which he founded and directed for many years. It was later named after him. Its grounds contain 10 telescopes, a botanical garden and the Ambartsumian House Museum.

Viktor Ambartsumian died on 12 August 1996 at the age of 88. His remains rest at Byurakan, near the tower of the Great Byurakan Telescope.
