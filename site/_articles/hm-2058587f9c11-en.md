Paul Dirac (1902–1984) was a British theoretical physicist, one of the founders of quantum mechanics and a 1933 Nobel laureate. Scientific folklore remembers him for more than his equations: he had a rare gift for answering in a way that made people want to check the wording of their own question first.

At a seminar, Dirac finished his talk and asked the audience the customary question:

“Any questions?”

Someone in the audience said:

“I do not understand how you obtained this expression.”

Dirac replied unperturbed:

“That is a statement, not a question. Any questions?”

The funny thing is not so much what he said as how perfectly it suited him. His Cambridge colleagues even invented a joking measure of talkativeness, one “dirac”: one word per hour. And when Niels Bohr complained that he did not know how to finish a sentence in a paper, Dirac observed:

“I was taught at school that you should never start a sentence without knowing the end of it.”

And another remark is unmistakably Dirac:

“In science one tries to tell people, in such a way as to be understood by everyone, something that no one ever knew before. But in poetry, it’s the exact opposite.”

These quotations make an almost perfect portrait of a man who treated words as strictly as formulas.
