*Marking the 115th anniversary of his birth*

Mstislav Vsevolodovich Keldysh (1911–1978) was a Soviet scientist working in applied mathematics and mechanics. He became a full member of the USSR Academy of Sciences in 1946.

A leading organizer of Soviet science, he served as president of the USSR Academy of Sciences from 1961 to 1975.

He was three times named a Hero of Socialist Labour, in 1956, 1961 and 1971, received the Lenin Prize in 1957 and two Stalin Prizes in 1942 and 1946, and was awarded the Order of Lenin seven times.

He was one of the architects of the Soviet space programme.

## From School to Applied Mathematics

He was born in Riga, then in the Russian Empire, on 28 January 1911 Old Style, or 10 February in the Gregorian calendar.

From 1919 to 1923 the Keldysh family lived in Ivanovo-Voznesensk, where his father taught at the polytechnic institute. Keldysh began secondary school there. Returning to Moscow in 1923, he attended Experimental Demonstration School No. 7, which specialized in construction. His talent for mathematics was apparent by the seventh and eighth grades, when teachers were already noting his exceptional ability in the exact sciences.

In 1927 he finished school and entered Moscow State University’s Faculty of Physics and Mathematics. During his studies he established scholarly contacts with M. A. Lavrentyev.

After graduating in 1931, he was assigned to the Central Aerohydrodynamic Institute, TsAGI, where he remained until December 1946. He rose from engineer and senior engineer to group leader and, from 1941, head of the Department of Dynamic Strength.

While continuing at TsAGI, he entered postgraduate study under M. A. Lavrentyev at the USSR Academy of Sciences’ Steklov Mathematical Institute in autumn 1934, followed by a two-year doctoral programme. He worked on approximation theory with applications to hydrodynamics and aerodynamics. In 1935 he received a Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences degree without defending a dissertation; in 1937 he received a Candidate of Technical Sciences degree and the title of professor in aerodynamics. In 1938 he defended his doctoral dissertation, “On the Representation of Functions of a Complex Variable and Harmonic Functions by Polynomial Series”.

## Flutter and Shimmy

Keldysh developed a mathematical theory of aircraft flutter that made it possible to determine the critical speed at which it begins and to propose ways of preventing it. During the war against Nazi Germany, aircraft destruction caused by flutter was virtually eliminated.

He also solved the problem of shimmy, oscillations of aircraft landing-gear wheels that became troublesome as aircraft grew larger and faster and often caused accidents. His mathematical solution enabled designers to eliminate these oscillations.

His work to prevent aircraft destruction earned him Stalin Prizes in 1942 and 1946.

## Research Institutes and State Programmes

In 1944 he became head of the recently established mechanics department at the USSR Academy of Sciences’ Mathematical Institute, remaining until 1953. He resumed teaching at Moscow State University, where he had begun in 1932: he lectured in the faculties of mechanics and mathematics and of physics and technology, headed the thermodynamics department and led a seminar on complex function theory. He was a professor at the university from 1942 to 1953.

From 1953 to 1966 he headed the Applied Mathematics Division of the Steklov Mathematical Institute. When it became the USSR Academy of Sciences’ Institute of Applied Mathematics in 1966, he became its director and held the post until 1978.

He took part in developing the Soviet thermonuclear bomb, organizing a special computational bureau at the Steklov Institute in 1946. For this work he received his first Hero of Socialist Labour award in 1956.

His preparations for the first human spaceflight—Yuri Gagarin’s flight on 12 April 1961—earned him the title of Hero of Socialist Labour for a second time.

From 1961 to 1978 he chaired the Committee for Lenin and State Prizes in Science and Technology under the USSR Council of Ministers. He was elected to many foreign academies.

His presidency of the USSR Academy of Sciences was a period of major scientific achievements, creating conditions for the development of many new fields.

The urn containing his ashes is set in the Kremlin Wall on Red Square in Moscow.

## Notes

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Keldysh helped initiate space research and the development of rocket and space systems. From the mid-1950s he led work on the theoretical foundations of placing artificial bodies in Earth orbit and subsequently sending missions to the Moon and the planets of the Solar System.

He headed the scientific and technical council coordinating the creation of the first artificial Earth satellite. He made major contributions to human spaceflight programmes, the formulation of research objectives and investigations of near-Earth space, interplanetary space, the Moon and the planets, and to problems of spaceflight mechanics, control, navigation and heat transfer.

Scientific leadership of the Intercosmos programme occupied an important place in his work. This aspect of his activity remained classified for a long time, and newspapers referred to him as the Theorist of Cosmonautics, although the public knew him as president of the USSR Academy of Sciences.

Keldysh’s name is also associated with the development of modern numerical mathematics in the USSR. He led work on Soviet electronic computers for nuclear and space calculations, beginning with the Strela computer. He not only directed the research team but personally helped create new computational methods and algorithms. In effect, he was the chief state customer for new computing equipment developed in the USSR.

V. I. Arnold recalled that Keldysh considered building Soviet supercomputers unnecessary because outstanding mathematicians such as Kantorovich had calculated everything required for the Soviet nuclear project without computers.

Keldysh bore a substantial share of responsibility for the controversial decision to steer Soviet industry, science and education towards copying IBM System/360 computers, a decision that shaped the future of the Soviet computer industry.

At the end of 1966, a joint meeting of the State Committee for Science and Technology and the USSR Academy of Sciences, supported by Radio Industry Minister V. D. Kalmykov and Academy President M. V. Keldysh, took the historic decision to copy IBM System/360. Many research institutes and factories were redirected towards this enormous programme. Specialists had to retrain, and university curricula increasingly focused on the structure, architecture and software of the ES EVM family of computers. A new technological base was created to manufacture integrated circuits, semiconductor electronics and other computing equipment. As had been predicted, other branches of Soviet computer development gradually contracted through a lack of funding, customers and young specialists, alongside other objective and subjective factors.
