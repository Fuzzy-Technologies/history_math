The surveyor's square (Russian ekker, from French équerre, from Latin quadrare, “to construct a square”) is a topographic instrument for setting out a fixed angle in the field, usually 90°, 60°, 45° or 135°. Its predecessor was the groma (Latin groma), an ancient Roman instrument for setting out perpendicular lines.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The simplest surveyor's square, the “Roman cross”, consists of two mutually perpendicular strips attached to a holder. Sights, or simply pins, are fixed to the strips and used to sight and then mark the required line. The strips may be fixed at other angles, such as 45° or 60°. Enclosed instruments — cylindrical, octagonal, spherical or conical — represent further developments. Reflecting instruments using mirrors or prisms are more complex.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Let us consider the construction of some surveyor's squares.

In the simplest case, one can be a draughting set square fixed horizontally to a stick, or a square board with two mutually perpendicular lines. Another form is a cross mounted on a tripod, with a small weight suspended below on a string: a plumb bob.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

S. Orlov's square is a square box with gaps between its upper and lower covers and sights made of metal wires or pins between them. There is also an octagonal square, with vertical slits and hairs in each face.

Another type is the optical square. Two small mirrors are fixed in a frame at an angle of 45°. The incident and reflected rays make an angle of 90° with each other. One direction is seen through a slit; the other, the required direction perpendicular to the first, is sighted along a line of vision reflected successively by both mirrors. The two-mirror square was invented by the London instrument maker George Adams in the second half of the 18th century.

Modern surveyor's squares use two pentaprisms.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

A pentaprism is a reflecting prism whose cross-section perpendicular to its working faces is a pentagon. Two faces are coated with a reflecting layer; of the three transparent faces, one is not used. Light entering the pentaprism emerges at right angles to its original direction, regardless of its angle of incidence on the entry face. This is achieved by the 45° angle between the reflecting faces. The even number of reflections keeps the image seen through the pentaprism upright.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

Field measurements were once part of the school curriculum, including measurements with the surveyor's square.
