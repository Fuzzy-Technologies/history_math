*Marking the 205th anniversary of his birth*

Arthur Cayley (16 August 1821–26 January 1895) was an English mathematician and a leading figure in nineteenth-century British pure mathematics. An algebraist, geometer and analyst, he produced an immense body of work and supplied a language for new subjects that now seem self-evident.

## St Petersburg, London and Cambridge

Cayley was born in Richmond, Surrey, but spent his early childhood in St Petersburg, where his family belonged to the trading community associated with British companies. In 1828 they returned permanently to England. He attended school in Blackheath and then King’s College School in London.

In 1838 he entered Trinity College, Cambridge, and soon became an outstanding student. He graduated as Senior Wrangler—the highest-ranked mathematics student—in 1842, won the first Smith’s Prize and was elected a fellow of Trinity. He spent several years researching in Cambridge, but no suitable academic post emerged that would allow him to devote his life to mathematics. In 1846 he chose the law.

## Lawyer and Mathematician

Cayley trained at Lincoln’s Inn, was called to the bar in 1849 and spent almost fourteen years practising conveyancing in London. Yet during those legal years he wrote hundreds of mathematical papers. Mathematics was no hobby for him, but a second life to which he gave every free moment.

Recognition came early: he was elected a fellow of the Royal Society in 1852 and received its Royal Medal in 1859. In 1863 he returned to Cambridge as Sadleirian Professor of Pure Mathematics. He remained at the university for the rest of his life, combining research, lectures and university duties.

## The Language of Modern Algebra

Cayley’s principal contribution was to the framework of modern algebra. He helped establish the theory of invariants and covariants, contributed enormously to algebraic geometry and shaped the language of group theory. His 1854 paper *On the Theory of Groups, as Depending on the Symbolic Equation $\theta^n = 1$* marked an important step towards an abstract understanding of a group as a set with an operation.

His role in developing the matrix approach was equally significant. In *A Memoir on the Theory of Matrices* (1858), Cayley systematically advanced the idea of a matrix as an object in its own right and checked the statement now known as the Cayley–Hamilton theorem for small orders. For nineteenth-century mathematics, this was a move from tables of numbers towards structural thinking.

Cayley was also active in scientific institutions. He was president of the London Mathematical Society in 1868–1870 and headed the British Association for the Advancement of Science in 1883. He received the Copley Medal in 1882 and the De Morgan Medal, one of Britain’s major mathematical distinctions, in 1884. His collected works filled thirteen volumes, a remarkable map of his mathematical universe.

Cayley’s name appears today in dozens of terms and theorems, but something else matters more: he could bring algebra, geometry and analysis into a single conversation. That combination of disciplines became his enduring legacy, part of the background from which twentieth-century mathematics grew.
