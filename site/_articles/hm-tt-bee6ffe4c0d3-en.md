An inversor is an instrument for constructing the inverse images of given figures.

In engineering, it converts circular motion into straight-line motion and vice versa.

This property of inversion is used, for example, in the Peaucellier–Lipkin inversor, the first planar hinged mechanism. It was devised independently in the 1860s by Charles Nicolas Peaucellier (1823–1913), an officer in the French army’s engineering corps, and Lipman Izrailevich Lipkin (1846–1876), a student of P. L. Chebyshev. The name “Lipkin’s straight-line linkage” also occurs in the literature. Various inversor designs exist.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

The diagram shows the geometric constructions explaining the Peaucellier–Lipkin mechanism.

Six links have fixed lengths: OA, OC, AB, BC, CD, DA. OA and OC are equal. AB, BC, CD and DA are all equal and form a rhombus. Point O remains fixed. If B is constrained to move along a circle passing through О, shown in red, D must move along a straight line, shown in blue. Conversely, if B is constrained to move along a straight line not passing through О, D moves along a circle through О.

The mechanism realizes inversion in a circle centred on the fixed pivot O, with radius determined by the lengths of its links.

An inversor can serve as a practical teaching aid. The Fibonacci inversor in the photograph at the beginning, for example, can demonstrate inversion in geometry lessons.
