A pantograph is an instrument for drawing homothetic figures, such as drawings, plans and maps. It produces copies that are geometrically similar to the original. Christoph Scheiner invented this mechanical device in 1603 and named it “pantograph” from the Greek πᾶν or παντός, “all” or “every”, and γράφω, “I write, draw or describe”: literally, “that which writes everything”.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Pantographs can have various designs. Their theory is explained in many geometry textbooks. A. P. Kiselev, for example, provides a drawing and explains the instrument’s operation through homothety.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

A typical pantograph contains a parallelogram whose sides are metal rods rotating on hinges at the vertices. One vertex is fixed and serves as the centre of homothety. A tracing pin, which follows the given figure, is attached to the extension of one side. A pencil placed on the segment between these points, at a distance determined by the scale factor of the homothety, then traces a similar figure. Proving this is an elementary geometry problem.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

G. M. Mikhailov described a homemade pantograph that can be built from five equal-length strips from a children’s construction set. The strips have holes drilled at equal intervals. Four form a rhombus; the position of the fifth, adjustable strip is determined by the scale factor.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

Pantographs are also used in everyday life. They can be found in sofa-bed folding mechanisms, adjustable stands that position a microphone in front of a speaker or a spotlight for filming, mirror extension arms, computer keyboard key mountings, and even the current collectors of electric trains and trams.
