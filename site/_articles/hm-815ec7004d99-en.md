He was born on 25 October 1811 in Bourg-la-Reine, France.

The brilliant French mathematician Évariste Galois laid the foundations of modern algebra.

His mathematical gifts appeared very early. The works of Abel, Legendre and Lagrange sparked his interest in algebra. Between sixteen and eighteen, while still a lycée pupil, Galois obtained the principal results in the theory of algebraic equations that would later bear his name: Galois theory. Nevertheless, he twice failed to gain admission to the École Polytechnique, failing its entrance examinations.

Between 1828 and 1830 Galois wrote five papers. The most important include *Analysis of a Memoir on the Algebraic Solution of Equations* (1830), which formulates major propositions of Galois theory, and *On the Theory of Numbers* (1830), in which he effectively constructed the theory of finite fields. He submitted his work to the Academy of Sciences, but even leading mathematicians of the time, including Cauchy, Fourier and Poisson, failed to appreciate it.

In 1829 he entered the École préparatoire, which resumed the name École normale in 1830. Galois was expelled in January 1831 after an open letter criticising the director. As a republican, he spoke publicly against the monarchy and was imprisoned twice. In 1832 he died in a duel whose exact circumstances remain disputed.

On the eve of the duel he sent a friend some manuscripts setting out his main discoveries, with this request: “You will publicly ask Jacobi or Gauss to give their opinion not on the correctness but on the importance of these theorems. After that, I hope there will be people who think it worth deciphering this gibberish.” This gibberish contained group theory, the key to modern algebra and geometry.

Galois’s letter was published soon after his death. The brevity of his presentation and novelty of his ideas nevertheless delayed recognition of his discoveries for many years. In 1846 Liouville published Galois’s principal memoirs on the conditions for solvability by radicals in his journal.

In 1870 the distinguished French mathematician Jordan wrote a book on the theory of substitutions, explaining that his work interpreted Galois’s manuscripts. This book made Galois theory accessible to the whole mathematical community. Over the next twenty years, Cayley and Jordan developed and generalised Galois’s ideas, transforming the face of mathematics.

Galois developed Abel’s result that the general algebraic equation of degree greater than four cannot be solved by radicals and formulated a criterion for a particular equation to be solvable by radicals. The methods were especially important: groups of substitutions became a central tool for studying equations and laid foundations for the later development of group and field theory. Galois used the term “group” systematically for substitutions.

In twenty years of life and four years devoted to mathematics, Galois made discoveries that placed him alongside the foremost mathematicians of the nineteenth century. Group theory is regarded as one of the signs of a new period in the subject: modern mathematics.
