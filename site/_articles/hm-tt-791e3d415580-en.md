(Dedicated to the 220th anniversary of Kazan University)

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-1" %}

In November 1804, Alexander I signed the founding charter and statutes of Imperial Kazan University. The university was established with four faculties or divisions: moral and political sciences; physical and mathematical sciences; medical sciences; and literary studies.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The mathematician and academician Stepan Yakovlevich Rumovsky (1734–1812) was the first trustee of the Kazan Educational District and provided considerable help in organizing Kazan University.

The teaching staff consisted of just two professors and four adjuncts, two of whom were mathematicians: Grigory Ivanovich Kartashevsky, who taught arithmetic, geometry and trigonometry, and Ivan Ipatyevich Zapolsky, who taught experimental physics.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Later, in 1808–1810, the training of mathematicians and physicists was led by prominent European scholars: Professors Johann Christian Martin Bartels, Kaspar Friedrich Renner, Joseph Johann von Littrow, Franz Xaver Bronner and Nikolai Dmitriyevich Brashman. The university's main building was constructed in 1825, followed in 1830 by buildings for the library, chemical laboratory, anatomical theatre, astronomical observatory and clinic.

All the first scholars trained by the Kazan mathematical school were pupils of Bartels, a graduate of Göttingen University.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

Russian mathematicians embarked on independent mathematical research in the 1820s. Among the first outstanding Russian mathematicians was Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky, whose entire life was connected with Kazan. He was both a brilliant mathematician and an eminent educational innovator, training a generation of scholars and teachers for the region. He attended the Kazan Gymnasium from 1802, studied at the university in 1807–1811, and then taught there.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

Besides his inspired non-Euclidean geometry, Lobachevsky left numerous studies in algebra, mathematical analysis, probability theory, mechanics, physics and astronomy. It would be difficult to find another person in the history of Kazan or other Russian universities who so naturally combined scientific genius, teaching talent and administrative ability.

As rector of the university in 1827–1846 and assistant trustee of the Kazan Educational District in 1846–1855, he could directly guide the development of mathematical education in the region. Teaching and methodological work in the Kazan district were better organized than in other Russian districts. Mathematics predominated in the teaching of its gymnasiums. Five mathematician rectors headed the university over fifty years. Places for Tatars and Bashkirs were established at the Kazan Gymnasium and the university. These measures had a positive effect on the education of the local Tatar intelligentsia.

A Pedagogical Institute operated at the university in 1804–1860, admitting university graduates to train as teachers for gymnasiums and other schools.

Kazan University became a major centre of education and science. Scientific schools developed in mathematics, chemistry, medicine, geology and geobotany. The scholarly journal *Scientific Notes of Kazan University* began publication in 1834.

One of the few scholars who appreciated Lobachevsky's discovery during his lifetime was Kazan University Professor Pyotr Ivanovich Kotelnikov (1809–1879). In 1838, he was sent to Kazan University to teach algebra and differential calculus. He founded the Kotelnikov scientific dynasty.

The next stage of the Kazan mathematical school's work saw intensive development of Lobachevsky's ideas in almost every direction. In the second half of the nineteenth century, Alexander Fyodorovich Popov, Vasily Grigoryevich Imshenetsky, Dmitri Matveyevich Sintsov and Alexander Vasilyevich Vasilyev successfully developed questions of mathematical analysis and algebra.

In 1846, Popov was invited to the university and, on Lobachevsky's recommendation, succeeded him in the chair of pure mathematics. His research interests included hydrodynamics, waves on liquid surfaces, elasticity and acoustics.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

Imshenetsky was one of the best-known Russian mathematicians. He developed the theory of first- and second-order partial differential equations.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

Sintsov was one of the country's most prominent geometers and later a full member of the Academy of Sciences of the Ukrainian SSR. He founded the Kharkiv school of geometry.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-8" %}

Vasilyev wrote the first scholarly biography of Lobachevsky and devoted much time and effort to spreading his ideas.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-9" %}

The Kazan Physical and Mathematical Society was founded in 1890, with Vasilyev as its first chairman. The mathematicians who initiated a return to Lobachevsky's geometric ideas at Kazan University were Professor Fyodor Matveyevich Suvorov (1845–1911), in the theory of invariants of Riemannian spaces, and Professor Alexander Petrovich Kotelnikov (1865–1944), in the foundations of mechanics in non-Euclidean spaces. They paved the way for Professor P. A. Shirokov's development of a new direction in geometric research in the early twentieth century. The high scientific standard of that work helped restore Kazan University's reputation in non-Euclidean geometry.
