*Marking the 155th anniversary of his birth*

Nikolai Maksimovich Günther (5 [17] December 1871–4 May 1941) was a Russian and Soviet mathematician, a professor at St Petersburg, later Leningrad, University and a corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences. His research concerned differential equations and mathematical physics. His teaching belonged to a tradition in which mathematical rigour had to serve engineers and physicists rather than stand apart from their work.

## The St Petersburg School and Differential Equations

He graduated from St Petersburg University in 1894 and remained in its academic community, joining the tradition of the St Petersburg school associated with Pafnuty Chebyshev. A characteristic phrase in accounts of his life explains much: “a true scholar works all the time”, in the way he thinks as well as at his desk.

At the university Günther completed both of the principal research qualifications of his period: a master’s dissertation in 1904 and a doctorate in 1915. His topics were at the forefront of early twentieth-century research, ranging from applications of algebraic forms to linear differential equations to the theory of characteristics for systems of partial differential equations. This was a school that valued general theory but required it to deliver precise proofs.

His work covered ordinary and partial differential equations and problems in mathematical physics. In particular, he studied existence and uniqueness in hydrodynamics, while in potential theory he sought careful assumptions, rigorous formulas and justified limiting arguments.

## Potential Theory and the Problem Collection

One major achievement was his book on potential theory, published in a French scientific series in 1934. Contemporaries emphasized that it was among the first truly rigorous and systematic accounts of the subject. It was later published in Russia too.

Generations of engineers and students remember Günther for his contribution to mathematical education. At the Institute of Railway Engineers he helped produce the celebrated *Collection of Problems in Higher Mathematics*. Reprinted many times, it served for decades as a practical standard. Günther was a coauthor and editor, and students nicknamed it the “Seven Sages”.

Günther died in Leningrad in May 1941. His legacy combines rigorous theories of equations and potential with the ability to make mathematics usable in teaching and in scientific and engineering practice.
