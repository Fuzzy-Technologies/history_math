*Marking the 380th anniversary of his birth*

[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](https://teletype.in/@history_math/Universalnyj-genij-Lejbnic-10-18) (21 June [1 July] 1646–14 November 1716) was a German philosopher and mathematician who helped create the language of calculus and sought to bring computation, logic and the structure of the world into a single system.

## Education, Public Service and Scientific Contacts

He was born in Leipzig. His father, a professor, died early, and the future scholar largely educated himself through books in the family library. As a young man he was already devising universal schemes of reasoning. In 1666 he wrote *De Arte Combinatoria*, on the art of combinations, hoping to reduce inference to orderly operations.

He studied philosophy and law at Leipzig University and defended his law dissertation at Altdorf in 1666. Offered a professorship there, he chose a different path: public service that could bring together science, projects and politics. In the 1670s he worked at the court in Mainz on legal and diplomatic assignments while seeking opportunities for larger scientific plans.

His stay in Paris, beginning in 1672, became an education in advanced mathematics. He met leading scientists, studied with Christiaan Huygens and gradually developed his own ideas about infinitesimals. Visiting London in 1673, he demonstrated his calculating machine and was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society. These contacts opened Europe to him as a connected scientific community.

## Hanover and the Language of Calculus

From 1676 his life was tied to Hanover. He served as a court adviser and librarian, travelled widely on official business, collected materials on the history of the House of Welf and maintained an extensive correspondence. He also worked on practical engineering in 1682–1686, including mining problems in the Harz Mountains. In 1691 he took charge of the Herzog August Library in Wolfenbüttel, and in 1696 was appointed a privy counsellor for justice.

His principal mathematical contribution was the infinitesimal calculus. In October 1684 he published *Nova methodus…* in *Acta Eruditorum*, explaining the foundations of differential calculus and establishing convenient notation. The integral sign $\int$, derived from the idea of a sum, soon appeared in the same journal. Clear notation made the new methods readily transferable between problems and easier to teach.

He offered more than formulas: he proposed a way of thinking in which computation became a language for the laws of nature. In mechanics he challenged the Cartesian tradition over what is conserved in motion, introducing concepts that helped shape a new dynamics. In epistemology and logic he pursued a universal scientific language, the *characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator*, anticipating formal systems of reasoning.

## Philosophy, Academies and Computation

His writings combine rigorous mathematics with a philosophical programme. Among his best-known works are *Discourse on Metaphysics* (1686), *Theodicy* (1710) and *Monadology* (1714). *New Essays on Human Understanding* was completed in 1704 but not published during his lifetime. He wrote for specialists and the wider scholarly public, and organized science institutionally: the Berlin Academy of Sciences was founded on his initiative in 1700, with him as its first president.

He also left a mark on the culture of computation. He developed binary arithmetic and published an explanation in 1703, showing how complex operations could be reduced to simple rules. His mechanical Step Reckoner was a milestone in the mechanization of calculation, while the idea of computing by rules eventually became fundamental to programming.

In his final years he continued diplomatic and scientific work, met Peter I and discussed projects for education and academies. Yet his death in Hanover attracted little notice at the court he had served for decades. Today his name returns whenever we write $dy$, $dx$ and $\int$, construct logical formalisms or think of computation as a language of science.
