[Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://t.me/history_math/36) (1777–1855), the “Prince of Mathematicians”, made rigour almost a way of life rather than merely a character trait. He was quite unsentimental about proof: in mathematics, he said, half a proof equals zero.

Against that background, collections of mathematical humour circulate a delightful tale.

A music-loving friend once dragged Gauss to a concert hall to hear Beethoven’s Ninth Symphony. The concert ended. The music died away. The audience was presumably overwhelmed.

His friend turned to Gauss and asked:

“Well, what did you think?”

According to the legend, Gauss replied dryly, almost businesslike:

“And what does all that prove?”

The joke is not so much about music as about a mathematician so accustomed to seeking rigour and the foundations of things that he seemed to expect a theorem from a symphony—or a lemma at the very least. And perhaps that captures Gauss: where others feel wonder, he instinctively asks for the criterion of truth.

P. S. And thank you, Gauss, for your high opinion of our great [Lobachevsky](https://t.me/history_math/806).

## Notes

Source: a late mathematical tale, circulated in this form in humour collections, including one by Bernhard Kutzler. No reliable earlier primary source for the remark has been found.

Further information and sources:

- [Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss) (Wikipedia, biographical article), noting in particular his enjoyment of singing and concert-going.

- B. Kutzler, *Mathematikerwitze & Mathematikwitze* (2005), a modern mathematical humour collection through which the tale circulates in the later tradition.

- [Quotations by Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Gauss/quotations/) (MacTutor), including the quotation about proof, “half proof = 0”.

> I mean the word proof not in the sense of the lawyers, who set two half proofs equal to a whole one, but in the sense of a mathematician, where half proof = 0, and it is demanded for proof that every doubt becomes impossible.

— Quoted in G. Simmons, *Calculus Gems* (New York, 1992).
