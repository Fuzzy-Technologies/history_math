> *“One of the many miscalculations that led to the failure of the fascist campaign against the Soviet Union was underestimating Soviet science.” Academician S. I. Vavilov*

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

There is still no complete and objective study showing how much mathematicians contributed to victory at the front. Ahead of next year's 80th anniversary of victory in the Great Patriotic War, we consider a project entitled “Mathematics and Mathematicians in the Victory” an appropriate educational resource for fostering patriotism among young people. Its educational products are intended for practising and future mathematics teachers, university students and school pupils.

As one area of such research and its educational use, let us consider mathematics directed toward defence. It is also discussed in some academic literature on teaching methods.

An important factor in bringing victory closer was the solution of applied defence problems by Soviet mathematicians before and during the war. These were primarily associated with developing improved military technology. Although applied questions considerably expanded the range of mathematical research, its theoretical level did not decline. The number of institutes and departments of the USSR Academy of Sciences even increased during the war. Soviet scholars in research institutes and laboratories opened a front invisible to outsiders and made personal contributions to victory.

Mathematicians solved many problems in aerodynamics, hydrodynamics and ballistics. Their contribution to aviation was especially impressive.

Academician S. A. Khristianovich (1908–2000) provided a theoretical solution to the problem of changes in aerodynamic characteristics used to calculate aircraft wings in high-speed flight.

> “…I will describe another piece of work that proved useful directly at the front. …The rockets fired by Katyusha launchers had one significant drawback: their firing dispersion was very wide. …Achieving the required density of fire demanded a great many rockets and launchers. …The problem had to be solved immediately; otherwise they would have had to be withdrawn from service and production stopped, because they consumed too much metal. …My colleagues and I established the cause of the dispersion, performed experiments on fairly simple equipment, and proposed a very simple technical solution to improve the rockets' accuracy. Our improved rockets entered service as early as 1943.” S. A. Khristianovich.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Academician M. V. Keldysh (1911–1978) conducted research to eliminate aircraft vibrations — flutter and shimmy — that caused crashes.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

Mathematicians proposed a statistical method of quality control for large quantities of uniform products: cartridges, shells, bombs and mines. During the war, A. N. Kolmogorov (1903–1987), B. V. Gnedenko (1912–1995) and others worked on these problems. Kolmogorov developed a new scientific field: a probabilistic theory for evaluating firing effectiveness.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

N. G. Chetaev (1902–1959), corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences and full member of the Academy of Artillery Sciences, used stability theory to solve problems in automatic control, gyroscopic systems and aircraft control. He also determined the best gun-barrel rifling twist for optimum firing accuracy.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

M. A. Lavrentyev applied mathematical methods to developing theories of explosions, waves and jets.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-8" %}

Under S. N. Bernstein's direction, mathematicians developed tables in 1942 for determining a ship's position from radio bearings.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-9" %}

L. V. Kantorovich proposed methods for efficient cutting of metal.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-10" %}

S. V. Bakhvalov, a noted geometer, developed the theory of artillery fire-control instruments.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-11" %}

Moscow State University professor M. M. Filonenko-Borodich helped create the “Road of Life” across Lake Ladoga by solving the problem of the ice cover's strength.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-12" %}

In Kazan, N. G. Chebotaryov studied the Routh–Hurwitz problem, connected with stability of motion and of practical importance to defence. His work “On the Minimax Problem” also had important technical applications.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-13" %}

Many mathematicians' works on defence topics received State Prizes.

Mathematical knowledge remains important today, when military art has become a contest of “reason, ingenuity and precise calculation.” Creating modern weapons requires not only physical and chemical laws but also new mathematical models and theories.
