A nomogram (from Ancient Greek noµos, “law”, and γραμμα, “written sign, image”, literally “an image of laws”) is a special drawing designed to solve a particular type of computational problem. It makes it possible to find numerical values without calculation by approximately evaluating a mathematical function graphically. Nomograms use a parallel coordinate system invented by Maurice d'Ocagne.

There are, for example, nomograms for solving monic quadratic equations, which can be found in V. M. Bradis' well-known Four-Figure Mathematical Tables.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

A nomogram replaces calculation with very simple geometrical operations specified in its instructions, such as laying a ruler against it, and reading off the answers. Nomograms generally provide answers correct to 2–3 significant figures. Like a slide rule, a nomogram is a graphical analogue computing device, and its accuracy is limited by how precisely physical markings can be drawn, reproduced and read. It can serve as an alternative way to check an answer obtained by another calculation that is more accurate but potentially prone to error.

The principle is that each drawing represents a particular range of variation of the variables, and each value within that range is represented by a geometrical element, a point or a line. Values connected by a functional relationship are represented in a particular correspondence, shared by nomograms of the same type.

Nomograms are divided into three main types, according to their method of representation and the way they specify the functional relationship between the given quantities:

• alignment nomograms;

• grid nomograms;

• overlay nomograms.

Equations in three variables use three scales constructed so that three points satisfying the equation lie on one straight line, hence the name “alignment nomogram”. A multiplication-table nomogram is an example.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Functional grids are used to construct grid nomograms from straight lines; the simplest are logarithmic and semi-logarithmic grids. On logarithmic paper, power functions have straight-line graphs. On semi-logarithmic paper with a logarithmic horizontal axis, logarithmic functions have straight-line graphs.

In their simplest form, overlay nomograms consist of two planes, a base plane and an overlay, with the variables represented on them. The overlay is often made of transparent material. A slide rule is an example of an overlay nomogram.

Constructing a grid nomogram may involve an additional problem, anamorphosis: finding a transformation that turns all three families of lines into families of straight lines, simplifying the drawing.

A nomogram can approximately evaluate certain quantities required for Pearson's familiar chi-squared statistical test. This nomogram demonstrates curved scales with unevenly spaced graduations.

## Historical background {#section-1}

Using simple graphical tables for calculation dates back to antiquity. In Hipparchus' time, such methods were generally accepted for solving spherical triangles. In the modern era, nomograms became widespread in the second half of the 19th century. The Parisian engineer and mathematician L. L. Lalanne introduced them, employing unusual scales on parallel axes and straightening a graph in 1842. He called this transformation “anamorphosis”. Scholars in different countries adopted and used the idea.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

The French mathematician Maurice d'Ocagne is regarded as the founder of the general theory of nomograms. Between 1884 and 1894 he published a series of works on the theory of “calculating drawings”. It was he who called them “nomograms” (Traite de nomographie, 1899).

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

The branch of mathematics studying the theory and practice of constructing nomograms is called nomography. Professors N. A. Glagolev and A. A. Glagolev made major contributions to Russian nomography and to organizing nomographic methods for engineering calculations.

Computer-based nomography has also developed: systems of procedures and standard programs calculate and construct the elements of nomograms using computers. For example, methods have been developed for transferring a graph from an image to Excel and for constructing a diagram or nomogram.
