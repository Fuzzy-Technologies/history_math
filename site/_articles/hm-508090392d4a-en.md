Some old mathematical problems were devised long ago and then travelled from country to country and textbook to textbook. At first they were solved numerically, by arithmetic and logic alone. Later, algebraic methods simplified many solutions, but something of the original beauty of an ingenious argument was lost.

One such traveller is the ancient Chinese problem of the pheasants and rabbits. It was first proposed in the Chinese treatise *Chiu-chang*, the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art. Later it appeared in Leonty Magnitsky’s *Arifmetika* (1703). It still appears in modern mathematics textbooks for pupils in grades 5–7: for example, problem 1148 in the grade-five *Matematika* by S. M. Nikolsky, M. K. Potapov and colleagues.

The statement is simple: “Pheasants and rabbits share a cage. Between them they have 35 heads and 94 legs. How many pheasants and how many rabbits are there?”

There are several ways to solve it. You could even devote a whole lesson to this one problem, comparing solutions or holding a small competition. First try arithmetic, perhaps even mental arithmetic, before devising other methods. That is our invitation to you.

## Solution: first method (two legs)

Imagine that every animal in the cage has two legs on the ground. Show the rabbits a carrot, for example, and they will stand on their hind legs. Think through these steps:

1. How many legs touch the ground?<br>
$35\times2=70$: twice the number of heads, counting the pheasants’ legs and the rabbits’ hind legs.

2. How many front legs do the rabbits have?<br>
$94-70=24$.

3. How many rabbits are there?<br>
$24\div2=12$.

4. How many pheasants?<br>
$35-12=23$.

Answer: 12 rabbits and 23 pheasants.

Try solving the problem yourself by the following methods:

- Method 2: four legs, using arithmetic;

- Method 3: simultaneous equations with two unknowns;

- Method 4: one equation with one unknown;

- Method 5: trial and error, a combinatorial method.

## Notes

### Method 2: four legs (arithmetic)

$35\times4=140$ legs if every animal were a rabbit—replace each pheasant with a rabbit.

$140-94=46$: the number of legs added beyond the required total.

$46\div2=23$ pheasants: the extra legs came from replacing them.

That leaves $35-23=12$ rabbits.

### Method 3: simultaneous equations (algebra)

Let $x$ be the number of pheasants and $y$ the number of rabbits. Together they have 35 heads and 94 legs, giving the system:

$$\begin{cases}x+y=35,\\2x+4y=94.\end{cases}$$

Solve by any familiar method to obtain $x=23$, $y=12$.

### Method 4: one equation (algebra)

Let $x$ be the number of rabbits. There are then $35-x$ pheasants, $4x$ rabbit legs and $2(35-x)$ pheasant legs.

Solve $4x+2(35-x)=94$. It gives $x=12$ rabbits and $35-12=23$ pheasants.

### Method 5: trial and error (combinatorial)

There are no more than 35 of either animal. Start, for example, with 35 pheasants—an underestimate of the number of legs. They have only 70. Then try 34 pheasants and 1 rabbit: 72 legs. Continue until you reach the required answer, 23 pheasants and 12 rabbits.
