Mathematics is not only formulas: it is also play. Archimedes, it turns out, was among the first to bring the two together. His name is associated not only with mechanical and geometric experiments, but also with famous problems—such as the cattle problem concerning the Sun’s herds—and puzzles.

This post concerns Archimedes’ Stomachion, an ancient puzzle game. The Danish historian of mathematics Johan Heiberg identified fragments of the treatise in the famous Constantinople Archimedes Palimpsest in 1906. The original writing had been washed from the medieval parchment and Christian prayers written over it; works by the great Greek scholar survived beneath the later text.

The Stomachion—from the ancient Greek στομάχιον; its exact etymology is uncertain, with “belly-ache” the most likely literal meaning—is a geometric game of fourteen polygons: eleven triangles, two quadrilaterals and a pentagon. They can be assembled into a square. Archimedes did more than play: he sought a mathematical answer to how many ways the pieces could form a square. The problem is now regarded as a forerunner of the seven-piece tangram and modern combinatorics. Its mathematical depth continues to surprise researchers.

Some interesting facts:

- Researchers have counted 536 geometrically distinct solutions. When symmetric transformations and interchanges of congruent pieces are distinguished, these give 17,152 arrangements—32 for each of the 536 solutions.

- Every piece’s area is a rational fraction of the square’s area—try checking this.

Stomachion sets are easy to find today, made in wood or plastic and even sold as board games. The pieces can form more than a square: try animals, robots, buildings or abstract designs of your own.

Have a go! Can you assemble the square, or invent your own pattern? Mathematics sometimes begins with a simple game.

P.S. This “game of Archimedes” proved not only entertaining but scientifically significant: it continues to inspire mathematicians and designers.

## Notes

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The Archimedes Palimpsest, c. 950s, parchment. The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore.

The palimpsest contains these works by Archimedes:

- On the Equilibrium of Planes;
- On Spirals;
- Measurement of a Circle;
- On the Sphere and Cylinder;
- On Floating Bodies;
- The Method of Mechanical Theorems;
- Stomachion.

*The Method of Mechanical Theorems* and the *Stomachion* survive in no other manuscript. For *On Floating Bodies*, the palimpsest is the only surviving source of the Greek text; a Latin translation is also known.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

An excerpt from the Book of Archimedes on the Stomachion, reproduced in Yu. A. Danilov, “Stomakhion”, *Kvant*, 1978, no. 8, pp. 50–63 (in Russian). The excerpt, translated from the Russian facsimiles:

> Since the so-called Stomachion comprises a series of investigations into rearranging the figures of which it consists, I thought it necessary first to explain how many pieces it has and what shape each resembles. I then set out what the angles must be so that, taken in pairs, they can form a right angle. Next I intended to examine thoroughly all conceivable cases in which Stomachion pieces can fit together, and to determine whether the sides of adjoining pieces lie on the same straight line or deviate so little that the eye cannot detect it. These questions require careful proofs; if the deviation from a straight line is small and therefore imperceptible, that kind of complete fit should be considered admissible.
>
> Since one piece may be replaced by another equal piece with equal angles, many different figures can be made… Sometimes two figures taken together are equal and similar to one figure, or two together are equal and similar to two other figures; this allows many figures to be made by rearranging the pieces.
