Many optical instruments — binoculars, sights, surveying compasses, rangefinders and other observing devices — have scales and reticles graduated in units called mils, or angular-scale divisions. Where does this unit come from?

## The angular scale and the mil {#section-1}

An angular-scale division is the central angle corresponding to an arc measuring 1/6000 of any circle's circumference.

For a circle of radius R, the arc length corresponding to one angular-scale division is:

2πR/6000 ≈ R/955 (rounded)

For practical purposes, this arc length is taken as 1/1000 of the circle's radius. This explains the common Russian name for an angular-scale division, “thousandth”. The mil is also a practical angular unit used with optical angle-measuring instruments in artillery, military topography and surveying. In effect, one angular-scale division corresponds to one thousandth of the range.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Artillery has conventions for writing and pronouncing angles expressed in angular-scale divisions. For example, an angle of 450 mils is written 4-50 and pronounced “four fifty”. If an angle contains a whole number of large angular-scale divisions, two zeros follow the hyphen. For example, 90 degrees is written 15-00 and pronounced “fifteen zero-zero”.

Rounding to thousandths produces a relative error of 5%: a thousandth is 5% smaller than an angular-scale division. In practice, this error is disregarded.

The angular-scale division, or mil, makes it easy to convert between angular and linear units. At every distance, the arc length corresponding to one division is one thousandth of the radius, which equals the range to the measured object. An angle of one mil corresponds to an arc, and also its subtending chord, at a distance of 1000 m.

An angle of several mils (У) corresponds to an arc length (В) equal to one thousandth of the range, Д/1000, multiplied by the angle containing У mils: В = (Д/1000) × У.

This gives the “mil formulas”, widely used in angle measurement:

Д = (В/У) × 1000

У = (В/Д) × 1000

Here Д is the range to the object in metres; У is the angle subtended by the object in mils; and В is the object's height or width in metres. These formulas use the chord length rather than the arc length. At small angles, up to 15°, however, the difference between arc and chord lengths does not exceed one thousandth, so they are treated as equal in practical work.

A Russian mnemonic helps people remember the mil formula: “Dunul Veter, Tysyacha Upala” — “The wind blew, a thousand fell.” Its initials recall the formula's symbols.

## Binoculars as an optical instrument {#section-2}

Now let us turn to binoculars. They come in many different forms.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Binoculars, from the French *binocle*, ultimately from the Latin *bini*, “a pair” or “two”, and *oculus*, “eye”, are the most common optical instrument for observing distant objects with both eyes. This gives the observer a stereoscopic image. As a measuring instrument, they are used to measure angles in horizontal and vertical planes and to determine distances to objects whose dimensions are known.

Each binocular telescope has a rotating eyepiece tube and eyepiece at one end, and an objective lens at the other. Two prisms are housed inside the tube. The lenses and prisms are arranged so that the observer sees an upright, magnified image of the objects.

For measurement, binoculars are fitted with an angular and rangefinding reticle etched on a glass plate in the right monocular. It requires additional focusing, so the eyepieces are focused independently.

## The angular reticle {#section-3}

An angular reticle usually consists of two mutually perpendicular scales with divisions and no numerical labels, or labels from which the value of a division can be calculated.

Different binoculars have different reticles according to their purpose. In B-3–B-8 binoculars, for example, the reticle looks as shown below.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

A large reticle division, between adjacent long marks, is 0-10, or ten mils. A small division, between a long and a short mark, is 0-05, or 5 mils. By estimating half a small division by eye, angles can be measured to within two or three angular-scale divisions.

Horizontal angles are measured using the horizontal row of reticle marks. To measure the angle between two objects, align a scale mark with one object and count the divisions to the image of the second. Multiplying that count by the value of one division gives the angle in mils.

There are also several vertical divisions in mils, used in the same way.

## Practical examples of using binoculars {#section-4}

Distance to an object or target can be determined with an angular reticle only when the object's linear dimensions are known, or those of an object immediately beside the target. The “mil formula” is used:

Distance to object = (Object size in metres / Angular size in mils) × 1000

## Example 1 {#section-5}

An enemy tank 2.8 m high subtends an angle of 0-05. Find the distance to the target.

Solution. Д = (2,8 / 5) × 1000 = 560 m.

## Example 2 {#section-6}

A tower is 100 m away and subtends an angle of 2-20 from base to top. Find its height.

Solution. В = (100 / 1000) × 220 = 22 m.

## Example 3 {#section-7}

After the first shot at an enemy machine gun 500 m away, a sniper observed the trajectory passing one figure-width to the left of the target's centre. Find the lateral correction in mils.

Solution. У = (0,75 / 500) × 1000 = 1,5 mils.

Various problems require conversion between degrees and mils.

One angular-scale division, or mil, corresponds to 0-01 = 3,6' = 216".

$$
1° ≈ 0-17, 1-00 = 6°, 360° = 60-00.
$$

## Using a sniper sight {#section-8}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

The angular reticle of modernised binoculars resembles that of a sniper sight. It contains:

- a lateral-correction scale (1);

- the main, upper aiming chevron for firing at ranges up to 1000 m (2);

- additional chevrons below the lateral-correction scale on the vertical line, for aiming at ranges of 1100, 1200 and 1300 m (3);

- a rangefinding scale consisting of a solid horizontal line and a curved dotted line (4).

Below the lateral-correction scale, to the left and right of the chevron, the number 10 denotes ten mils (0-10). The distance between two vertical scale marks corresponds to one mil (0-01). The height of the chevron and of a long lateral-correction mark corresponds to two mils (0-02).

The rangefinding scale is designed for a target height of 1.7 m, an average person's height. This height is indicated below the horizontal line. Above the upper dotted line is a graduated scale whose intervals correspond to changes of 100 m in target range. The numbers 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 correspond to distances of 200, 400, 600, 800, 1000 m. Range can be determined using either the rangefinding scale or the lateral-correction scale.
