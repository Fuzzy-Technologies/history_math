> The crossing, the crossing!

> A. Tvardovsky

Collections of logic problems, both old and modern, contain many river-crossing problems with particular restrictions. The best known is the wolf, goat and cabbage puzzle, [whose author was Alcuin](https://t.me/history_math/341) (Flaccus Albinus, Latin *Alcuinus*, c. 735–804), the Anglo-Saxon scholar, theologian and poet.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

An old Russian collection of recreational problems includes this one:

*“Three jealous husbands came with their wives to a riverbank and found a boat so small that it could hold no more than two people. How can these six people cross the river so that no wife crosses with another woman's husband or remains with him on either bank?”*

The following clarification can be added: *“A wife must not be left on a bank with other women's husbands unless their wives are also there. Wives are allowed to row on their own.”*

Many people have solved such problems successfully by trying different possibilities. You can try your hand too. How the solution is presented, especially visually, also matters. The steps of the algorithm can be described in words, a table, a graph or a schematic drawing. Let us examine one solution.

## Method 1 {#section-1}

For convenience, label the couples Аа, Бб, Вв, using lowercase letters for wives and uppercase letters for husbands. Here is a crossing plan that takes 11 trips. The arrows show the boat's direction.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Can you arrange the crossing in fewer trips? What if there are four couples?

The problem also has other versions:

- Can four couples cross in a boat that seats three?
- Can four couples cross in a two-person boat if the river has an island where people can be left temporarily?
