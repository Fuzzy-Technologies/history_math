> Tatyana Ivanovna Anisimova

## About me {#section-1}

My name is Timur Gilmullin. I graduated in 2006 from Yelabuga's pedagogical institution, then still a university. I completed the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics with an honours diploma, qualifying as a mathematics teacher with an additional specialisation in computer science. I remain very proud of this.

I was asked to recall something from student life. I believe the university gave graduates of my generation a great deal of fundamental knowledge across many subjects: higher mathematics—algebra, geometry and analysis—physics, computer science, methods of teaching mathematics, English and pedagogy. I remember an intensive curriculum throughout all five years. I accumulated several dozen thick, 96-sheet notebooks in mathematical subjects alone. These materials proved very useful later, when I taught in schools and universities in Tatarstan and when I pursued postgraduate study in Kazan.

Yet as students we also found time to have fun, take part in KVN comedy competitions and Student Spring festivals, and organise the famous student competitions in our chosen profession. By then I had also received a musical education, so I wrote the musical accompaniment for our faculty's performances. I also remember our student games of “What? Where? When?”. I captained a mixed team with students from other faculties. We performed quite well at regional games, often travelled to competitions in Naberezhnye Chelny, and even reached the top three. All in all, it was fun and interesting!

## Teachers who influenced my career {#section-2}

Among the teachers and educators who greatly influenced my later career in IT, I would like to acknowledge and thank:

- [Tatyana Evgenyevna Novozhenina](https://ridero.ru/author/novozhenina_tatyana_xbglt/), who taught methods of teaching school mathematics—put simply, she taught people to teach people—and organised student activities and competitions;

- [Mansur Fayzrakhmanovich Gilmullin](https://kpfu.ru/mansur.gilmullin), our teacher of higher algebra and the history of mathematics, who instilled in me a love of mathematics and its applications;

- Taisiya Konstantinovna Yurzanova, who taught subjects involving the use of IT in education and practice, such as automating work in office applications, as well as algorithm design and assessment of algorithmic complexity;

- Igor Alexandrovich Kobelev, our programming-language teacher, whose first classes in the city on algorithms and programming for schoolchildren my friends and I had eagerly attended as boys;

- Anatoly Petrovich Bochkaryov, an expert in teaching the foundations of computer science and information theory, and a cultured teacher respected by colleagues and students.

I believe I owe my entire subsequent career in science and the IT industry to their concentrated knowledge, broad understanding of their fields, patience in explaining material, and invaluable practical classes.

## My career in IT {#section-3}

I successfully applied the knowledge, abilities and skills in mathematics, computer science and teaching methods, and the theoretical and practical material I gained through these teachers, at various workplaces. During and after university, I had the opportunity to:

- work as a schoolteacher of mathematics and computer science in Yelabuga;

- teach cybersecurity-related subjects at the Kazan Aviation Institute, KAI;

- undertake postgraduate study at KAI while helping establish one of Kazan's first departments of Information Security Systems;

- write and defend a candidate of technical sciences dissertation in speciality 05.13.18, “Mathematical Modelling, Numerical Methods and Software Systems”;

- work as an engineer testing application software;

- lead a large department of automation engineers, known as [DevOps engineers](https://habr.com/ru/companies/pt/articles/552104/), Development Operations, at a company developing cybersecurity software;

- work as an architect of [secure development processes](https://habr.com/ru/companies/pt/articles/557142/) at large international IT companies;

- write numerous research, [engineering and applied-science articles](https://scholar.google.ru/citations?hl=ru&user=OL2_1lUAAAAJ&pagesize=1000&view_op=list_works&sortby=pubdate), study guides and books on various topics, create open-source software, and develop a [platform](https://github.com/Tim55667757/TKSBrokerAPI) for my startup in stock-market automation.

In every one of these activities, fundamental knowledge of mathematics, computer science and algorithm design proved useful in one way or another, as did skills directly connected with teaching, communication and coordinating the work of many engineers.

## Training engineers {#section-4}

I shall not describe every job and the specific university subjects that helped in each. Clearly, our course of study was most useful in education. I will focus on one example in which my teacher-training background also helped me in IT.

IT specialists know Habr, a resource where people share knowledge across different fields. When I led an engineering department, I wrote many articles on [automation and managing IT teams](https://habr.com/ru/search/?q=%D0%93%D0%B8%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BC%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%BB%D0%B8%D0%BD&target_type=posts&order=date). One was [“Personal Experience: How to Structure Career Growth in a DevOps Department”](https://habr.com/ru/companies/pt/articles/501766/).

In it, I described our journey from a small team where everyone did everything at once to a large, distributed, specialised team, where we also had to address the training and competency development of automation engineers.

As the department grew, we increasingly understood what our engineers wanted to know: what opportunities did they have to develop as specialists, and what could the department offer new engineers? We began simply, creating a progression table of engineering positions and categories. As the saying goes, “When a society has no colour differentiation of trousers, it has no purpose! And when it has no purpose...” Our internal structure comprised three management positions—department head, deputy head and group leaders—and four engineering positions: junior, regular, senior and lead software engineers. We in turn divided these into three categories.

The categories enabled smoother, gradual staff progression as responsibilities and workloads increased. We then specified the competencies an engineer in each category should possess.

First, we had to define knowledge, abilities and skills, abbreviated ZUN in Russian. Here we drew on Tatyana Evgenyevna Novozhenina's work, which she had introduced in our mathematics teaching-methods classes. In general terms:

- Knowledge consists of the fundamental principles of a subject area that enable a person to solve specific industrial, scientific and other problems: facts, concepts, judgements, images, relationships, assessments, rules, algorithms, heuristics and decision-making strategies in that field. Knowledge also consists of information elements connected to one another and to the external world.

- Abilities are ways of performing an action that a person has mastered, supported by a body of knowledge. An ability is expressed in consciously applying knowledge in practice.

- Skills are automatised human actions developed through consciously performing particular work operations. When an action becomes a skill, practice has enabled a person to carry out these operations without making their execution a conscious aim.

## The engineers' competency map {#section-5}

We then defined knowledge, abilities and skills more specifically in relation to the company's products, producing an overall competency list. Without mastering these competencies, an engineer could not perform well in their position and category.

Such a list becomes very long because it must reflect a particular department's work across all areas, technologies and products. We then described and classified all our requirements in a table, the “Engineers' Competency Map”.

The table has four main sections:

- general engineering competencies needed to solve everyday tasks successfully;

- knowledge: engineers' specific, product-oriented knowledge;

- abilities: applying knowledge in practice to solve product-related tasks, and working with the department's tools and technologies;

- skills: professional command of the tools and technologies used in the department, and learned actions for typical tasks that have become automatic and require little effort.

The table's cells contain qualitative assessments of the approximate level at which an engineer should command each competency.

## The advantages of soft skills in IT {#section-6}

This is just one example of applying the educational approaches I learned as a student at Yelabuga's pedagogical university to engineering work. Many other teaching skills are useful in the IT industry too:

- the ability to teach, for example by creating internal courses for engineers;

- the ability to organise an internal knowledge base with a sound method and clear structure, so engineers can exchange experience and pass knowledge to newcomers;

- the ability to communicate with a large audience and express ideas clearly, especially valuable when defending work projects before a wide audience;

- the ability to plan one's work, closely resembling the methodical lesson planning we learned at university;

- the ability to manage an engineering team, drawing on experience in organising learning and solving problems jointly with pupils;

- most importantly, the ability to learn properly and systematically according to a plan, which greatly helps in every job, including engineering.

I believe graduates in physics and mathematics who also have teacher training enjoy a considerable advantage in today's labour market. University education in general, even specialised IT education at Moscow State University and Bauman University, lags behind practical industrial tasks. We can no longer expect a university to teach every skill and produce someone fully ready for work. Graduates of any university have to keep learning once they start a job.

In today's circumstances, it is more appropriate to speak of continuous education throughout life, or lifelong learning. In this respect, mathematics graduates from teacher-training universities often have an advantage over graduates of purely technical universities, at least in the soft skills listed above.

## Creativity {#section-7}

As I conclude, I want to emphasise that graduates of teacher-training universities gain advantages in their later careers through their interpersonal soft skills and the practical skills acquired during their studies. They can also successfully pursue creative projects of their own outside work.

After university, I never stopped writing articles and books on engineering, mathematics and popular science. The presentation, typesetting and text-processing automation skills I learned during my studies helped greatly, as did presenting material in a modular and methodical way.

For example, Mansur Fayzrakhmanovich Gilmullin and I produced a joint work, the book [*History of Mathematics*](https://ridero.ru/books/istoriya_matematiki/).

It presents a professionally oriented course in the history of mathematics, organised by periods of development. The book is written in an accessible popular-science style for a wide readership and is richly illustrated. Russian teachers will also find a useful section on the history of mathematics in Russia, and teachers in Tatarstan a further section on the region's mathematical history.

For this work, I took responsibility for the entire technical preparation: typesetting, illustration, page design, working with the publisher and publication.

Another joint creative project, still continuing today, is our popular-science Telegram channel [Mathematics with Mansur-abyy](https://t.me/history_math), about mathematics in history, modern science and technology. It offers:

- articles about scholars and experimenters who created science;

- interesting episodes from the lives and work of famous mathematicians, physicists and mathematics educators;

- articles in the series “Discoveries in Mathematics”, “History of Mathematics”, “Mathematicians Joke Too”, and “Mathematical Prodigies”;

- logic and ingenuity problems, quick calculation, mathematical quiz competitions, mathematical tricks and experiments;

- accounts of mathematical instruments, methods and tools and their discovery;

- discussions of mathematics' possibilities and prospects, and mathematics in general.

My part in this project consists of typesetting and illustrating articles and finding historical photographs and documents. We publish many historical, local-history and teaching materials connected in one way or another with the history of mathematics. Subscribe—there is much of interest!

My account ends here. I wish every student in a teacher-training programme success in study and work, growth in soft skills, further creative achievements, and lasting confidence in the path you have chosen!
