Ancient Egypt left an enormous cultural legacy to world history. Among the Egyptian civilisation's landmark achievements were:

- the invention of hieroglyphic writing in the fourth millennium BCE;

- the construction of the pyramids — for example, the Pyramid of Khufu, built in the twenty-sixth century BCE and 146 m high, was counted among the seven wonders of the ancient world;

- the first calendar, adopted as early as the fifth millennium BCE, with a year of 365 days.

Two surviving papyri allow us to assess the state of mathematics in ancient Egypt. Ancient Egyptian mathematics changed little after these papyri were compiled.

The first is known in the history of mathematics as the Rhind Papyrus, or the Papyrus of Ahmes. It is kept in the British Museum in London. Found in 1858, it was acquired by the Englishman Rhind and deciphered in 1870. It is 544 cm long and 33 cm wide, and contains 84 problems. Written in the seventeenth century BCE, it incorporates older material. Its title reads: “Instructions for attaining knowledge of all obscure ... matters, of all secrets contained in things. The work was written in year 33, in the fourth month of the season of inundation, in the reign of King Ra-a-us. From old manuscripts of the time of King ... The scribe Ahmes wrote this.”

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The second is called the Moscow Papyrus and is kept in Moscow's Pushkin Museum of Fine Arts. It is 550 cm long and 8 cm wide, and contains 25 problems. It was written two centuries earlier. The orientalist V. S. Golenishchev acquired it in the late nineteenth century; it was deciphered in 1927 by B. A. Turayev and V. V. Struve.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

The papyri present mathematics through worked problems. Every problem has practical content: quantities of bread, the capacity of storage facilities, or the area of a field. They are grouped by topic rather than by method of solution. Each is solved afresh with numbers, without explanation. Numbers themselves and methods of solving problems were not yet objects of study. Neither statements nor justifications of rules are given.

Numbers were written in a decimal system with special signs — hieroglyphs — for the decimal units of each order. There was therefore no positional principle of notation yet. The hieroglyphs originally took the form of pictures and retained a visual resemblance to particular objects.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

Each sign was repeated as many times as the number contained units of that order. Numbers were written from right to left, from left to right, or in vertical columns.

Egyptian arithmetic was predominantly additive: all calculations were reduced to addition. Multiplication was carried out by doubling and adding.

Fractions also appear in Egyptian arithmetic. All fractions were reduced to sums of so-called basic, or aliquot, fractions, from the Latin *aliquot*, “several”. These are fractions with numerator one, now called unit fractions. The exception was 2/3.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

Decomposing fractions into sums of unit fractions remained in use in mathematics for a very long time, even into the Middle Ages.

The Egyptians also considered algebraic problems reducible to linear equations in one unknown.

> Problem. A quantity and its fourth part together make 15. What is the quantity?

> Here is a traditional solution in the Egyptian manner. Start with 4. You obtain 5. Divide 15 by 5. Multiply the result by 4.

This solution uses the method later called the “rule of false position”. An arbitrary value is chosen for the unknown, taking account of the numbers in the problem and trying to eliminate fractions. If the prescribed operations produce a number other than the one required, the trial “false” value and the values of its parts are corrected proportionally.
