The Pythagoreans studied solutions in natural numbers to the indeterminate equation:

$$
x^{2} + y^{2} = z^{2}
$$

Today this is called the Pythagorean problem, and its solutions, triples of natural numbers x, y, z, are called Pythagorean triples.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

This number-theoretic problem is connected with a geometrical one: find all right triangles with integer legs x, y and hypotenuse z. Particular solutions were known in Egypt and Babylon. The papyri contain a right triangle with sides in the ratio 3 : 4 : 5, still called an Egyptian triangle today. It enabled people in antiquity to construct right angles.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

The Pythagoreans found infinitely many triples of the form:

$$
x = m,
$$

$$
y = 1 / 2 (m^{2} - 1),
$$

$$
z = 1 / 2 (m^{2} + 1),
$$

where m is odd.

The general solution for relatively prime numbers x, y, z appears in Diophantus (3rd century CE):

$$
x = 2pq,
$$

$$
y = p^{2} - q^{2},
$$

$$
z = p^{2} + q^{2},
$$

$$
(p, q) = 1.
$$

These are also known as Euclid's formulas. They imply that infinitely many Pythagorean triples exist.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

The possible values of z in Pythagorean triples form sequence A009003 in the OEIS (On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences).

Pythagorean triples have many remarkable properties that are subjects of specialized research. These properties are also used to solve certain number-theoretic problems, such as indeterminate equations of the second degree. Pythagorean triples are used in cryptography as random sequences for generating keys.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

A generalization of the theory of Pythagorean triples is the problem of finding triples of natural numbers x, y, z such that xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ for some n greater than 2. In 1637 Pierre de Fermat asserted that no such triples exist, and this assertion became known as Fermat's Last Theorem.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}
