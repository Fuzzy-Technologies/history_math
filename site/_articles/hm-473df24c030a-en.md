*Written to mark the 130th anniversary of his birth*

“A Machine Wiser Than Its Maker.”

“The whole problem of man’s use of a machine is that we should know what to ask of these machines and how to ask it; otherwise they may become dangerous.”

“Information is information, not matter or energy.”

Norbert Wiener was an outstanding American mathematician and one of the founders of cybernetics and artificial intelligence.

He was born on 26 November 1894 in Columbia, Missouri, in the United States.

Wiener was a celebrated child prodigy. His education under his father’s guidance began before school age. Leo Wiener was a demanding teacher who immediately set a high standard: a “radical experiment in home education”. He did not hide his intention to raise Norbert and his sisters as geniuses. At four, Wiener was allowed to use the family library; at seven, he wrote his first scientific treatise, on Darwinism. At eleven, he entered the prestigious Tufts College, graduating with honours only three years later with a Bachelor of Arts degree. At eighteen, according to this account, he received a doctorate in mathematical logic at Cornell and Harvard. At nineteen, Dr Wiener was invited to the mathematics department of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. From 1919 he taught mathematics at MIT.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Before the Second World War, the account describes Wiener as a professor at Harvard, Cornell, Columbia, Brown and Göttingen. He held a chair at MIT and wrote hundreds of papers on probability and statistics, Fourier series and integrals, potential theory, number theory and generalised harmonic analysis.

In 1947 Wiener introduced “cybernetics” as the name for a discipline concerned with the laws of information transmission and control. Work on computing, genetics, mathematical statistics and the laws of social development helped prepare the ground for this discipline. Wiener’s *Cybernetics* appeared in 1948. Its full title was *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine*. One of the most influential books of the twentieth century, it was hailed as an original work comparable in importance to those of Galileo and Newton.

Wiener extended Einstein’s formulation of Brownian motion and developed a mathematical model describing the paths produced by Brownian processes. The model is now called the Wiener process in his honour.

He received six scientific awards, including the US National Medal of Science in 1963.

Wiener is also known for his autobiographical books *Ex-Prodigy* and *I Am a Mathematician*.
