“I have created a strange new world out of nothing!”

The Hungarian mathematician János Bolyai, one of the discoverers of non-Euclidean geometry, was born on 15 December 1802 in Kolozsvár, in the Habsburg-ruled Grand Principality of Transylvania, now Cluj-Napoca, Romania. Russian renderings of his surname also include Бояи, Больяи, Бойаи and Бойай.

No account of the beginnings of non-Euclidean geometries would be complete without János Bolyai. He was a military engineer. His father, Farkas Bolyai, a mathematics professor and poet, had studied with Gauss in Göttingen and remained his friend and correspondent.

János spoke several foreign languages: German, Latin, French, Italian and Romanian. He graduated from the military engineering academy in Vienna and mastered mathematical analysis while still young. At the academy he began trying to prove Euclid’s fifth postulate, concerning parallel lines. His father, who had once worked on the problem himself, warned him in a letter in 1823: “You must reject it as you would the most abhorrent liaison; it may rob you of all your leisure, health, peace and every joy in life. This black abyss could perhaps swallow a thousand titans like Newton; it will never be cleared up on earth.”

János became an officer but continued working on geometry. Around 1825 he reached the basic propositions of non-Euclidean geometry. His principal conclusion was that the fifth postulate cannot be proved and is independent of the others. He published his results in 1832 as an appendix to his father’s *An Attempt to Introduce Studious Youth to the Elements of Pure Mathematics*. Its title was *Appendix Explaining the Absolutely True Science of Space*. In the care devoted to every word and sentence, this appendix is among mathematics’ most accomplished works.

Farkas Bolyai wrote to Gauss asking for an opinion. In his letters, Gauss praised János’s work in the highest terms, but gave no public assessment. He said that the ideas in the Appendix had been his own thoughts for many years. János was disappointed, believing that he was losing priority. He was even more shaken when he read Lobachevsky’s work, published in German in 1840.

He also wrote a paper on the theory of imaginary quantities, anticipating Hamilton’s theory, but received a negative assessment.

These setbacks deeply affected his creative work. He never published anything again and died in a state of depression, a few years before non-Euclidean geometry gained general recognition.
