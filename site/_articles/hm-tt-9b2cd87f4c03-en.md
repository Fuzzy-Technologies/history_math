The diagonal scale, also called a transverse scale, is one of the oldest graphical instruments for measuring distances accurately to tenths and hundredths of a unit. It relies on similar triangles, more precisely on the proportionality of segments on parallel lines intersecting the sides of an angle.

Diagonal scales were once widely used in surveying, cartography and architecture. From the XVII to the XIX century, mapping national territories became so precise that older measuring methods were inadequate. The diagonal scale then became an indispensable companion to engineers and cartographers.

The simplicity of its idea is striking: a powerful instrument for those who know how to use it, a mysterious grid for those encountering it for the first time.

We are unlikely ever to know who first devised it. It may have appeared simultaneously in several European schools of applied mathematics.

## The linear scale {#section-1}

First, let us recall the diagonal scale’s older sibling.

A linear scale measures map segments with limited accuracy. It consists of a straight line divided into equal segments, usually 2 cm each.

At the left end, one base interval is divided into ten equal parts to measure tenths.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

## How the diagonal scale is constructed {#section-2}

Construct it as follows:

- Mark 6–10 equal segments, usually 2 cm each, along a horizontal line.
- Divide the leftmost segment into 10 parts.
- Erect perpendiculars at the endpoints of the base intervals.
- Mark equal segments on the outermost perpendiculars.
- Join the resulting points with horizontal lines.
- Draw transversals across the first interval: sloping lines for reading hundredths accurately.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

## How to use it {#section-3}

Measure a line on the map with dividers.

Transfer the opening to the diagonal scale so that the right leg rests on a whole-number mark and the left enters the finely divided area.

If the left leg falls between divisions, move it upwards to the intersection of horizontal and vertical lines.

The final length is the sum of:

- the number of whole base intervals;
- the number of tenths of a base interval;
- the number of hundredths, determined vertically.

## Historical background {#section-4}

The earliest metal diagonal-scale rulers were manufactured in the XIX century for surveyors. Examples can be seen in museums, including the Lomonosov Museum and the Polytechnic Museum.

The diagonal scale’s emergence is associated with the need for accurate field surveying during the development of national cartography in Europe. It was used particularly extensively in constructing roads, fortifications and canals.
