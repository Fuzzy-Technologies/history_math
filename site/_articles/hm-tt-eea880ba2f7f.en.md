A vernier, also called a nonius, is an auxiliary scale fitted to various measuring instruments and tools to determine fractions of a main-scale division more precisely.  
The name “nonius” honours the Portuguese mathematician Pedro Nunes (Latin: Nonius, 1502–1578), who proposed the principle of a scale for an instrument of this kind.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The modern scale design was proposed in 1631 by the French mathematician Pierre Vernier (1580–1637), after whom it is also named. He published the invention in *The Construction, Use and Properties of a New Mathematical Quadrant* (*La construction, l'usage, et les propriétés du quadrant nouveau de mathématique*).

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

In fact, the vernier principle had first been invented by Abu Ali ibn Sina, or Avicenna (980–1037). In his *Book on the Method Preferred to Other Methods for Constructing an Observing Instrument* (*Maqala fi al-tariq alladhi atharahu 'ala sa'ir al-turuq fi ittikhadh al-ala al-rasadiyya*), he described an observing instrument of his own invention intended to replace the astrolabe. This instrument first used the vernier principle to refine measurements.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

The vernier scale works because people can judge the coincidence of marks much more precisely than the relative position of one mark between others. The spacing of its divisions is smaller by a certain amount than that of the main scale. A vernier division may correspond to one main-scale division or to several.

When taking a measurement, the position of the vernier's zero mark gives the whole number of main-scale divisions, Nₘₐᵢₙ. The closest coincidence between vernier and main-scale marks gives the number of vernier divisions, Nₙₒₙ.

The measured value V is found by adding the product of the number of main-scale divisions and the value of each division to the corresponding product for the vernier:  
V = Nₘₐᵢₙ ⋅ Fₘₐᵢₙ + Nₙₒₙ ⋅ Fₙₒₙ  
where Fₘₐᵢₙ is the value of one main-scale division and Fₙₒₙ the value of one vernier division.

Linear, angular, transversal, spiral and other types of vernier are used. An angular vernier, found in a number of optical-mechanical instruments, is read on the same principle as a linear vernier.
