Augustin-Louis Cauchy was born in Paris on 21 August 1789.

A French mathematician and researcher in mechanics, he studied and worked at the École Polytechnique and also worked at the Sorbonne and the Collège de France. He was elected to several European academies of sciences. His work covered many areas of mathematics, and he was enormously productive.

There were periods when Cauchy submitted a new memoir to the Paris Academy of Sciences every week. Later, *Comptes Rendus* (*Reports*) imposed limits on article length. In all, Cauchy published more than 800 works on number theory, algebra, mathematical analysis, differential equations, mathematical physics, and theoretical and celestial mechanics.

His *Course of Analysis* (1821), *Summary of Lectures on Infinitesimal Calculus* (1823) and *Lectures on the Applications of Analysis to Geometry* (1826–1828) systematically used the concept of a limit. In them, Cauchy defined a continuous function, the derivative as a limit of ratios and the integral as a limit of sums; he proved the existence of the integral of a continuous function and established tests for the convergence of series.

The rigour of mathematical analysis also occupied Cauchy. His definitions of limits, continuity, derivatives and integrals marked an important stage in the development of rigorous analysis, although his framework still differed from the modern theory of real numbers. Another major achievement was his development of the theory of analytic functions of a complex variable, founded by Euler and d’Alembert. In differential equations, Cauchy formulated general problems, including the Cauchy problem, and proved fundamental existence theorems. In geometry, he generalized the theory of polyhedra and investigated tangency, rectification and quadrature of curves. In algebra, he developed the theory of determinants and studied permutations, making an important contribution to the emergence of finite group theory. His work also helped establish the terminology of complex analysis.

Cauchy gave a classical proof of the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality; the Cauchy–Bunyakovsky inequality also bears his name.

Despite his achievements, some biographers believe that he did not treat the work of younger colleagues very respectfully. One known episode involved a memoir by Niels Henrik Abel that had been assigned to Cauchy for review. No report appeared, and the manuscript, which could not later be located, resurfaced only after Abel’s death. Abel’s correspondence shows how deeply the delay distressed him.

Cauchy’s name appears in the list of France’s greatest scholars on the first level of the Eiffel Tower.
