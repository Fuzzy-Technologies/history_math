At the All-Russian conference in Dubna in September 2000, Academician V. I. Arnold gave a talk entitled “Does School Need Mathematics?” He sharply mocked the fashion for “proper” mathematics without meaning or pictures, where producing a formal proof matters more than understanding.

He offers an almost comical example of such a school’s “success”.

A French pupil was asked, “What is $2+3$?” The answer was “$3+2$, because addition is commutative” — and that was as far as the pupil got. They did not know that the answer was five, but gave the sort of answer that would earn top marks.

Next comes a typical problem that such pupils supposedly solve with ease:

“Prove that all RER trains on the planet Mars are red and blue.”

The “Martian” solution goes like this: let $X_n(\mathrm{RER})$ be the set of RER trains on planet number $n$. Mars is planet no. 4 and there are no RER trains there, so $X_4(\mathrm{RER})=\varnothing$. Since the set is empty, all its elements have any specified property. Therefore, all RER trains on Mars are red and blue.

The joke is not about Mars, but about a substitution: the proof is formally rigorous, yet empty of substance. Arnold associated this with two troubles in education:

- “Americanisation”: “everything old must be thrown out”, while education becomes a service with no respect for foundations or traditions.

- “Bourbakisation”: mathematics becomes ultra-abstract casuistry based on axioms, while understanding, practical geometry, examples and visual intuition are declared optional. [More about Bourbaki](https://teletype.in/@history_math/Burbaki-04-06).

Formalism without understanding empties mathematics of meaning, but understanding without form soon turns into a conversation based on vague impressions. We therefore need to learn both to think and to prove.
