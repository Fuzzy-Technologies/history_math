*Marking the 120th anniversary of his birth*

Iosif Benediktovich Pogrebyssky, born on 23 February 1906 Old Style, or 8 March in the Gregorian calendar, and deceased on 20 May 1971, was a Soviet mathematician, historian of science and chess player. He received his Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences degree in 1965.

## Education and Wartime Service

He was born in Uman to a physician’s family. At gymnasium he developed an early interest in foreign languages, while chess became a second school for character and logic. After completing a workers’ school, he entered the physics and mathematics division of the Kyiv Institute of Public Education and then undertook postgraduate study in the mathematics department under D. A. Grave. Early in his career he combined teaching at Kyiv institutions with research.

From 1935 he worked at the Ukrainian SSR Academy of Sciences’ Institute of Mathematics. He defended his Candidate of Sciences dissertation in 1940. Volunteering for the front in 1941, he served in cavalry and reconnaissance units and earned military orders and medals. Demobilized as a major in December 1945, he returned to research in Kyiv.

## The History of Science and Books

In the early 1960s he moved to Moscow and joined the USSR Academy of Sciences’ Institute for the History of Science and Technology. There he fully developed his work as a historian of science, investigating mathematics and mechanics, studying primary sources, preparing editions and biographical studies and speaking at conferences. He served on international commissions on the history of science, helped prepare congresses and worked on documentation — the largely invisible labour that gives historical research coherence. He defended his doctoral dissertation in 1965 and soon afterwards became a corresponding member of the International Academy of the History of Science.

He is often remembered at the meeting point of rigorous mathematics and its history. An exceptional polyglot, reportedly knowing about ten languages, he translated classical works into Russian, including writings by Euler and Poincaré, and many books on history and philosophy, among them D. J. Struik’s *A Concise History of Mathematics* (1969). Above all, he could write about science so that readers saw the people, their times and the cost of a scientific idea behind the formulas. His essays follow ideas across periods: how concepts of motion, force and probability change, why one idea survives for centuries while another dissolves into its historical setting.

He left more than a hundred works on the history of mathematics, mechanics and physics. His scientific biographies and books stand out for their respect for sources and their clear teaching style. His books include:

- *Mikhail Vasilyevich Ostrogradsky* (1963), with B. V. Gnedenko;
- *From Lagrange to Einstein* (1966);
- *French Science and Modern Physics* (1967), co-authored;
- *Ferdinand Minding* (1970), co-authored;
- *Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz* (1971);
- *Pascal* (1971), co-authored.

Pogrebyssky was an active member of the team that wrote the five-book *History of Mathematics in Russia*, edited by I. Z. Shtokalo. He also contributed to collective works on the history of mechanics and its development in the USSR. His writing connected biography with the history of an idea, tracing a problem and its method through to the place of scientific thought in culture.

## Chess and Scholarly Strategy

He was also a prominent chess player: a USSR Master of Sport from 1937, Kyiv champion in 1925, joint first in the 1936 Ukrainian SSR championship and a participant in the 1939 USSR championship. His play was described as positional and strategic: unhurried, precisely calculated and capable of unexpected discoveries, winning through a carefully constructed sequence rather than a single blow. This same ability to see several moves ahead helped him fit scattered historical facts into a coherent picture.

His wife, Zinaida Andreevna Artemyeva, was also a strong chess player; she shared first to third place in the Ukrainian SSR women’s championships of both 1936 and 1938. Pogrebyssky died in Leningrad and was buried in Moscow.

Could you have imagined that the history of mathematics is sometimes written by people for whom a game of chess is itself a form of rigorous proof?
