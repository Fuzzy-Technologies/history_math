(On the 300th anniversary of the Russian Academy of Sciences)

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-1" %}

The creation of an academy of sciences in Russia was directly connected with Peter I's reforms. The example of the Paris Academy, to which he had been admitted, his conversations with scholars abroad, G. W. Leibniz's advice and repeated proposals from his associates convinced him that Russia too needed an academy of sciences.

On 28 January (8 February) 1724, Peter issued a decree to the Senate “On the Establishment of the Academy of Sciences and Arts.”

The Academy has borne different names in different historical periods:

> Academy of Sciences and Arts in St Petersburg (1724–1747);  
> Imperial Academy of Sciences and Arts in St Petersburg (1747–1803);  
> Imperial Academy of Sciences (1803–1836);  
> Imperial St Petersburg Academy of Sciences (1836–1917);  
> Russian Academy of Sciences (1917–1925);  
> USSR Academy of Sciences (1925–1991);  
> Russian Academy of Sciences (1991–present).

Initially, members were to be found in the customary way, by inviting foreign scholars from countries such as Switzerland and Germany. The St Petersburg Academy's structure was intended to differ substantially from Western European academies. In practice, it formed a single complex with the Academic University, which existed in 1726–1767, and the Academic Gymnasium, which existed in 1726–1805. Formally these were separate institutions, but the same people were Academy members and university teachers. The Academy was thus to combine research and teaching. Academicians, or professors, had varied duties: following scholarly literature and compiling summaries of results in their fields; attending weekly meetings and annual public assemblies; supplying scientific information and examining discoveries submitted to the Academy; preparing student courses; and giving public lectures. Each academician was to train one or two pupils who could eventually replace him. Peter wished *“that such pupils should be selected from the Slavic people, so that they might more readily teach Russians.”*

After Peter I died on 28 January (8 February) 1725, Empress Catherine I, who succeeded him, took the emerging Academy under her patronage. Peter's personal physician, Lavrenty Lavrentyevich Blumentrost, was appointed its first president.

Catherine provided two buildings on Vasilyevsky Island: the Kunstkamera, still under construction, and the nearby palace of Tsarina Praskovya Fyodorovna Saltykova. From the early eighteenth century, the Kunstkamera became a symbol of the Russian Academy of Sciences.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The foundation of the St Petersburg Academy was a turning point for many sciences in Russia, including mathematics. It gave a powerful impetus to training Russian mathematicians. Of the 23 academicians invited during its first years, seven were mathematicians: Jacob Hermann (1725), a Swiss mathematician and mechanician and the first academician in the chair of higher mathematics; Christian Goldbach (1725), the Academy's conference secretary and councillor; Nicolaus Bernoulli (1725); Daniel Bernoulli (1725); and Leonhard Euler (1727).

The first Russian president was Count K. G. Razumovsky, appointed in 1746 and serving in 1746–1798. The first Russian academicians were M. V. Lomonosov (1745), S. P. Krasheninnikov (1745), author of the first natural-science book, *Description of the Land of Kamchatka*, and the poet V. K. Trediakovsky (1745). Astronomers N. I. Popov (1751), S. Ya. Rumovsky (1767) and P. B. Inokhodtsev (1779) followed.

From 1728, the *Commentaries of the Imperial St Petersburg Academy of Sciences* appeared in Latin, publishing academicians' original mathematical works, including 400 works by Euler.

Academicians were also responsible for teaching the most able students. Some students went to Germany to deepen their knowledge. European pedagogical and methodological ideas thus entered Russia through representatives of Euler's first Russian school of scientific teaching methods.

In 1726–1733, 38 people studied at the university. Later, the best pupils from the Academic Gymnasium, the Slavic-Greek-Latin Academy and other institutions were transferred to it. Among 12 pupils brought to St Petersburg from the Slavic-Greek-Latin Academy were M. V. Lomonosov, D. I. Vinogradov and N. I. Popov.

The university and gymnasium played important roles in science and education. They gave Russia the brilliant Lomonosov and its first Russian mathematical academicians. But a shortage of students prepared for university study led to the university's closure in 1783. The scientific, educational and teaching work of Euler and his pupils and successors — S. K. Kotelnikov, S. Ya. Rumovsky, N. G. Kurganov, N. I. Fuss, M. E. Golovin and S. E. Guryev — helped improve teaching in engineering, military, naval and army institutions throughout the country. The Academy played a major part in preparing and implementing the school reforms of the 1780s and 1790s. Its members developed the reforms' principal provisions, helped train the first professional teachers, and wrote and published around 30 textbooks and study guides.

Mikhail Vasilyevich Lomonosov's scientific, educational and organizational work formed an entire era in the history of the Academy and Russian science. Lomonosov (1711–1765) enriched it with fundamental discoveries in chemistry, physics, astronomy, geology and geography; contributed substantially to history, linguistics and poetics; established the first chemical laboratory in 1748; and actively helped found Moscow University in 1755, which now bears his name.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Lomonosov was rector of the Academic University in 1758–1765. Alongside his scientific work, he devoted great effort to organizing teaching, recognizing the need to bring it into line with European university traditions.

Mathematics and its branches developed within the Russian Academy of Sciences from its creation in 1724. Euler and Daniel Bernoulli were outstanding mathematicians involved in its foundation. Its eminent nineteenth-century mathematicians included P. L. Chebyshev, M. V. Ostrogradsky, A. M. Lyapunov, A. A. Markov the elder, V. A. Steklov and A. N. Krylov. S. V. Kovalevskaya was elected a corresponding member. Not every major Russian mathematician was elected: N. I. Lobachevsky, for example, was not, because his revolutionary non-Euclidean geometry was not recognized during his lifetime.

In 1934 the Academy moved to Moscow.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

In 1991, following the breakup of the USSR, the Russian Academy of Sciences was re-established by decree of the President of the RSFSR.

An Academic University was established in St Petersburg in 2002. It is now the Zh. I. Alferov St Petersburg National Research Academic University of the Russian Academy of Sciences, Russia's only university founded by a Nobel laureate.

In 2013 the Academy was reorganized through merger with the Russian Academy of Medical Sciences and the Russian Academy of Agricultural Sciences.

Mathematical problems facing the Academy are now addressed by its Division of Mathematical Sciences, a structural unit comprising academicians with mathematical research interests and institutions devoted to mathematics.

It is impossible, even briefly, to list all the significant results obtained by Academy mathematicians. Russian mathematics has always had, and still has, many research schools headed by outstanding scholars.

Academician A. N. Kolmogorov (1903–1987) solved numerous problems in probability and analysis. I. M. Vinogradov (1891–1983) obtained major results in analytic number theory. Significant algebraic results emerged from schools in Moscow, led by L. S. Pontryagin and A. I. Kostrikin; Leningrad, D. K. Faddeev; Kazan, N. G. Chebotaryov; and Novosibirsk, A. I. Mal'tsev. Yu. V. Matiyasevich proved the algorithmic undecidability of Hilbert's tenth problem on Diophantine equations in 1970. I. R. Shafarevich and Yu. I. Manin gained worldwide recognition for their work in algebraic geometry. P. S. Alexandrov founded the Russian school of topology, which achieved major results. Complex analysis developed strongly in the schools of M. V. Keldysh, M. A. Lavrentyev and V. I. Smirnov.

Academician L. V. Kantorovich (1912–1986) received the Nobel Prize for mathematical economics in 1975. Academician S. P. Novikov received the Fields Medal in 1970 for topology. Academician L. D. Faddeev (1934–2017) received the international Poincaré Prize in 2006 for his outstanding contribution to mathematical physics. In 2014, Academician Ya. G. Sinai received the Abel Prize, a prestigious international mathematics award, for fundamental contributions to dynamical systems, ergodic theory and mathematical physics.
