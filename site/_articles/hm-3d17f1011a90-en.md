*Marking the 135th anniversary of his birth*

Stefan Vasilyevich Filichev (19 November 1891–1958) was a Soviet mathematics educator and specialist in teaching methods: a person devoted to school mathematics in the fullest sense. He did not build new theories, but helped build what every theory needs in the classroom: clear explanations, well-written solutions, sound problem-solving methods and a coherent school course.

## Arithmetic Problems and Written Reasoning

His chief interest was the arithmetic problem as a tool for thinking. In the 1940s and 1950s, as the country rebuilt its education system after the war, Filichev addressed teachers’ immediate difficulties: how to set out calculations, teach written reasoning, avoid empty formalism and explain a method so that pupils understood its meaning as well as its steps.

In *Mathematics in School* he published practical articles, often with coauthors, on requirements for written work, arithmetic notation, approximate calculations and revisions to the fifth-grade arithmetic curriculum. Such questions often remain behind the scenes, yet they determine whether classroom mathematics is lively and intelligible.

One milestone was *A Guide to Solving Arithmetic Problems* (1948), written with Ya. F. Chekmaryov. The book’s description states its educational aim precisely: solving problems develops “the ability to reason clearly and consistently”. This was a programme rather than a slogan: problems were grouped by topic, different methods were demonstrated, and teachers were offered a range of approaches rather than one prescribed path.

## Problem Books as Historical Sources

His research was substantial too. In 1952 Filichev defended a Candidate of Pedagogical Sciences dissertation on arithmetic problem books in Russian and Soviet schools. In effect, he studied how the content and methods of a problem book changed with its period, the demands of the school and educational ideas.

Historians of mathematics education now return to this work. Filichev is named among the early researchers who saw school problem books as reflections of the social world of their time as well as exercises in skills. Teaching methods thus become part of cultural history through problems, language and classroom experience.
