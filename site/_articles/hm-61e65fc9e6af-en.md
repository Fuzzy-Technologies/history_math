A Russian mathematician whose research on the foundations of geometry led to non-Euclidean geometry.

He was born on 20 November, or 1 December in the Gregorian calendar, in 1792 in Nizhny Novgorod. He attended the Kazan Gymnasium from 1802 to 1806 and the university from 1807 to 1811. His whole life was bound up with Kazan University. He began teaching in 1812 and became a professor in 1816. He was dean of the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics in 1820–1821 and 1823–1825, and rector of Kazan University from 1827 to 1846. University buildings were constructed under his leadership. From 1846 to 1855 he served as assistant trustee of the Kazan Educational District.

Throughout his research career, Lobachevsky investigated the foundations of geometry. At first, like others, he tried to prove Euclid’s fifth postulate. He then became convinced that it was independent of the remaining axioms. Replacing it by a different postulate, under which more than one parallel to a given line can pass through a point in its plane outside that line, produces no contradiction. He first presented his “imaginary geometry” to the Faculty Council on 11 February, or 23 February in the Gregorian calendar, in 1826, in a talk entitled “A Concise Outline of the Foundations of Geometry, with a Rigorous Proof of the Theorem on Parallels”.

His memoir *Imaginary Geometry* appeared in the *Scholarly Notes of Kazan University* in 1835, followed by *New Principles of Geometry with a Complete Theory of Parallels* in 1835–1838. In the latter work he gave a complete, systematic account of the new geometry. All these works met with an extremely hostile reception from his contemporaries.

In Europe, Lobachevsky’s work became known through *Geometrical Investigations on the Theory of Parallel Lines*, published in German in Berlin in 1840. Gauss read it and highly valued the discovery. On his recommendation, Lobachevsky was elected a corresponding member of the Göttingen Scientific Society as one of the “outstanding mathematicians of the Russian Empire”.

Towards the end of his life, having lost his sight, Lobachevsky dictated his final work, *Pangeometry* (1855), dedicating it to the university’s fiftieth anniversary.

The creation of the new geometry was one of the greatest achievements of nineteenth-century mathematics. It placed Lobachevsky among the world’s foremost mathematicians.
