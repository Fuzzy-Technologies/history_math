There are several versions of the well-known puzzle of the three wise men. We shall examine the simplest case: the wise men know the available caps and the rules, each reasons correctly, and each knows that the others do the same.

“A sultan wanted to find out which of his three wise men was the cleverest, so he devised a test.

There are three black caps and two white ones. In a dark room each wise man is given a cap, and the light is switched on. Each can see the others’ caps but not his own. Whoever first names his own cap’s colour will be declared the wisest.

The sultan had the men taken into a dark room, where each was given a black cap. The light came on. After some thought, one said, ‘My cap is black.’

How did he know?”

You may ask why all three were given black caps. One can regard this as the sultan’s idea of fairness: to test them, he should give them equal conditions by putting the same colour—black—on all three. The wise men, however, did not know this. Other conditions would make it a different problem.

## Solution

The solution uses reasoning from another person’s point of view.

Here is the classic solution. It does have critics, who introduce various further assumptions and alternatives.

Seeing both rivals in black caps, a wise man could reason: “Suppose my cap were white. Each rival would then see one black cap and one white cap. If either rival’s own cap were also white, the third man would see two white caps and immediately identify his own as black. After the first silence, both rivals—each observing that no such immediate answer had been given—would therefore realise that their own caps were black and answer at the next stage. But they remain silent at that stage too. My supposition must be false: my cap is black.”

So the winner did not discover his cap’s colour from the arrangement of the stars :)
