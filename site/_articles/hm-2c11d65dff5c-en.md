*Marking the 280th anniversary of his birth*

Gaspard Monge (1746–1818) was a French mathematician and statesman who founded descriptive geometry. His ideas became a language for engineering drawing and technical design: how to turn a three-dimensional body into an accurate flat drawing, and, conversely, how to recover its shape from the drawing.

## Geometry for Practical Work

He was born on 9 May 1746 in Beaune, Burgundy. His talent for geometry emerged early: he is said to have drawn a detailed plan of his home town while still young. In the 1760s he came to the Royal School of Military Engineering at Mézières, where he taught while developing geometrical methods for practical problems in fortification and construction. It was there that his geometry for practical work took shape.

What we now call descriptive geometry was for Monge more than a branch of mathematics: it was a tool for an engineer’s thought. Its central idea is simple and powerful: study space through projections, translating difficult questions about intersections, distances and shapes into clear constructions on a plane. His course was long considered a military asset and guarded as a professional secret.

## Revolution, Schools and a Scientific Legacy

During the French Revolution he took on public responsibilities, serving on the commission that developed the metric system of weights and measures. He was France’s minister of the navy in 1792–1793, working on supply and production. He then helped found new educational institutions, the École Normale and the École Polytechnique in Paris. At the latter he gave his celebrated lectures on descriptive geometry, later published as *Géométrie descriptive* (1799).

He was also interested in pure geometry. His work on surfaces and lines of curvature is often seen as one of the beginnings of differential geometry. The influence of his school can be traced through his pupils and successors, from the polytechnic tradition to nineteenth-century engineering culture.

His life was closely linked with Napoleon. He joined the commission in Italy that selected scientific and artistic treasures, then took part in the Egyptian expedition as a member of its scientific community. For the history of mathematics, however, another point matters more: he showed that geometry could be both a rigorous science and a practical tool for seeing form, constructing and checking.

He died in Paris on 28 July 1818. His descriptive geometry is still recognizable in every precise technical drawing, and in the way an engineer mentally rotates an object before drawing a line.
