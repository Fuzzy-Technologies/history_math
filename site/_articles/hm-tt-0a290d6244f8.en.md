Many problems arising in sport belong, in theoretical terms, to the problems of fair division. Here, what is divided or allocated is the final sporting outcome: prize places, scores, qualification grades, ratings and classifications. Let us consider several problems in sports mathematics.

## Scoring team results at the Olympic Games {#section-1}

The overall team standings at the Olympic Games are unofficial, since the Olympic Charter states: *“The Games are competitions between athletes, not between countries.”* Nevertheless, team standings appeared in some official reports as early as 1906.

The most commonly used system, employed since 1924 and widespread in most countries, awards 7–5–4–3–2–1 points: 7 for first place, 5 for second, 4 for third, 3 for fourth, 2 for fifth and 1 for sixth. The team standings do not count the total number of individual medals handed to athletes in team events. In every event where a team of two or more people receives an award, one medal is counted. If athletes or teams share scoring places, the points in the unofficial team standings are divided equally between them: for example, a tie for first and second gives 6 points each; for third through fifth, 3 points each; for sixth, 0.5 points.

How are places in Olympic team standings allocated at present? The scoring system is: first place, 25 points; second, 17; third, 9; fourth, 5; fifth, 3; sixth, 2.

Under this scoring system, the United States team has most often won at the Summer Olympics, followed by the USSR team, including the CIS. At the Winter Olympics, the USSR team has most often won, followed by Norway.

In 1988, another form of team standings began to be used: medal rankings. These may count either the total number of medals or give priority to gold medals. In the latter case, a country with just one medal, if it is gold, ranks above a country with several silvers and no golds. Where gold-medal totals are equal, the country with more silvers ranks higher. Where gold and silver totals are equal, the country with more bronzes takes the higher place. Various unofficial points-based team standings nevertheless continue to be published today.

All interested parties acknowledge the conventional nature of these unofficial standings, but consider them useful for drawing conclusions about countries' actual position in world sport.

## Judging {#section-2}

In many sports, judging is effectively a form of expert assessment. The most difficult decisions are those made by a panel of expert judges, as in figure skating, gymnastics and diving. An expert's judgement on a question within their speciality may carry more weight than measurements and calculations. The method of expert assessment seeks to establish agreement among experts' opinions. To do so, those opinions must be expressed in some conventional units: marks, points, percentages or ranks.

Several types of information are used when working with an expert panel.

First, the panel can be given a numerical scale for the factor being assessed. Each expert expresses a judgement as a number on that scale. Gymnastics, for example, uses a ten-point scale in increments of 0.1 points.

Second, experts can arrange the objects under assessment in order. This ordered arrangement is called a ranking. To obtain a final aggregate ranking, one defines a distance between the judges' rankings and chooses a median ranking that minimizes the sum of the distances from the originals. Such a solution is mathematically possible but complicated, and is rarely used in practice.

Third, experts can divide the entire collection of objects into separate classes, performing a classification. Classification through a rating system is the most common approach.

Fourth, the objects can be compared in pairs. The expert states which of the two is preferable.

These four types of qualitative information are currently the main forms used in expert assessment.

Expert assessment is now widely used in sports judging by adding the scores awarded by a panel, each judge assessing a performance on some scale. That scale may award points for particular features of the quality of an exercise. The resulting points are then multiplied by coefficients, for example for the exercise's difficulty in figure skating or gymnastics.

The judgements communicated to experts are called relations. On the basis of the experts' relations, the decision-maker selects an aggregate relation. This aggregate relation then determines the fair allocation of sporting results.
