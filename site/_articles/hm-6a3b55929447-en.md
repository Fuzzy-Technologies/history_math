The eighteenth century, the Age of Enlightenment, began with a new crisis in the development of mathematics: infinitesimal calculus had been created and produced excellent computational results, but lacked firm logical foundations.

Joseph-Louis Lagrange, a leading French mathematician, mechanician and astronomer, worked to improve infinitesimal calculus. He belonged to the Berlin, Paris and St Petersburg academies of sciences and the Royal Society of London. He sought to give analysis a rigorous algebraic foundation and remove shortcomings in the approaches of Newton, Leibniz and d’Alembert. His method nevertheless proved incomplete.

Lagrange was born in Turin on 25 January 1736 and became a professor of mathematics at nineteen. When Euler left Berlin for St Petersburg in 1766, King Frederick II of Prussia invited Lagrange to replace him. According to a frequently repeated account, the invitation declared: “It is necessary that the greatest geometer in Europe should live near the greatest of kings.”

From 1766 to 1787 Lagrange directed the mathematical section of the Berlin Academy of Sciences. The Berlin years were the most productive of his life. He obtained important results in the theory of algebraic equations and the calculus of variations. In the paper *On the Solution of Numerical Equations* (1767), he gave methods for isolating the real roots of an algebraic equation and calculating them approximately. He found a method of eliminating variables from a system of equations using the resultant.

He made important contributions to number theory, rigorously proving several of Fermat’s assertions and Wilson’s theorem: for every prime $p$, the number $(p-1)!+1$ is divisible by $p$. In 1770–1771 he published the memoir *Reflections on the Algebraic Solution of Equations*, from which Abel and Galois later drew inspiration. Lagrange’s study of permutations of roots anticipated the later development of group theory. In 1824 Abel proved that the general equation of the fifth degree cannot be solved by radicals, while in 1830–1832 Galois found general conditions for solvability by radicals. Later, in Paris, Lagrange published his celebrated interpolation formula for approximating a function by a polynomial.

Lagrange then moved to Paris. He took part in the reform of weights and measures and worked with Laplace and Monge on the metric system. He also helped organize and run the École Normale and the École Polytechnique.

The work of Lagrange and Euler laid the foundations of a new branch of mathematical analysis, the calculus of variations. Euler often acknowledged the advantages of Lagrange’s methods over his own.

In Paris, Lagrange published his courses in mathematical analysis in two parts: *Theory of Analytic Functions* (1797) and *Lectures on the Calculus of Functions* (1801–1806). He gave the formula for the remainder in a Taylor series, the mean value formula and the interpolation formula. He used triple integrals, developed the method of variation of arbitrary constants and used functions of a complex variable to solve problems in hydrodynamics.

In 1788 Lagrange published *Analytical Mechanics*, developing classical mechanics as a theory of general differential equations governing the motion of material systems. He thus replaced Newton’s geometric approach to mechanics with an analytical one.

Napoleon enjoyed discussing philosophical questions with Lagrange. He granted him the title of count, a seat in the Senate and the Legion of Honour. Lagrange is buried in the Panthéon in Paris. His name appears among the seventy-two greatest scholars of France on the first level of the Eiffel Tower.
