Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) was a mathematician, philosopher and logician who could turn serious subjects into perfect stories for an audience.

Raymond Smullyan’s book contains an episode in which Russell explains a principle of logic: once you seriously accept a false proposition, you can go on to “prove” anything.

One philosopher asks in astonishment:

“Do you seriously believe that ‘two plus two equals five’ implies that you are the Pope?”

Russell replies without batting an eyelid:

“Of course. And I can prove it.”

He immediately offers this proof: from $2+2=5$, he neatly derives $2=1$, then adds a little simple arithmetic:

“The Pope and I are two people. But if $2=1$, the Pope and I are one person. Therefore I am the Pope.”

The laws of logic are unforgiving: anything follows from a false premise.

## Notes

Source: Raymond M. Smullyan, *What Is the Name of This Book?*, the section “Russell and the Pope”.
