A brilliant mathematician and engineer of the Hellenistic age.

Archimedes’ work shaped the course of science for centuries. His contribution is compared with those of Newton and Euler. Some information about his life and character also survives. He was also celebrated for his achievements in mechanics and engineering.

Archimedes was born and lived in Syracuse on the island of Sicily, where he served as an adviser to King Hiero. He was killed when the Romans captured Syracuse.

Archimedes was among the greatest mathematicians. His most important mathematical contribution concerns integral calculus. Although no such calculus existed in his time, he obtained remarkable results with the method of exhaustion. For Italian mathematicians of the sixteenth century, his ideas became a starting point for further development of differential and integral calculus. The well-known Archimedean axiom is now included in axiom systems for the real numbers.

When calculating areas and volumes and determining arc lengths, Archimedes used a method analogous to upper and lower integral sums. In *Measurement of a Circle*, for example, he used inscribed and circumscribed regular polygons to calculate the circumference.

*On the Sphere and Cylinder* gives the ratio of a sphere’s surface area to the area of its great circle, “four times as large”, and the ratio of a sphere’s volume to that of its circumscribed cylinder, “2/3”. Archimedes regarded the latter formula as his greatest achievement and asked for it to be depicted on his tomb. More than a century later, Cicero described recognizing Archimedes’ neglected tomb by its image of a sphere and cylinder.


In his hydrostatic treatise *On Floating Bodies*, he established the existence of the buoyant force of a liquid: Archimedes’ principle concerning the loss of weight of bodies immersed in a liquid. This discovery is associated with his famous exclamation, “Eureka!”

In *On the Equilibrium of Planes*, Archimedes derives the celebrated law of the lever: “Magnitudes are in equilibrium if their distances from the fulcrum are inversely proportional to their weights.” A famous saying traditionally attributed to Archimedes is: “Give me a place to stand, and I will move the Earth.”

Archimedes’ investigations were not developed further in antiquity because the necessary analytical foundations and symbolic algebra were lacking.

Leibniz wrote: “When one reads Archimedes’ works carefully, one ceases to be surprised by all the new discoveries of geometers.” Archimedes’ profile appears on the Fields Medal. Under the current rule, recipients must not have turned 40 before 1 January of the year in which the congress is held.
