*Marking the 115th anniversary of his birth*

Alexey Andreyevich Lyapunov (25 September [8 October] 1911–23 June 1973) was a Soviet mathematician, a corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences and one of the founders of Soviet cybernetics and programming. His career is unusual in the way the rigour of pure mathematics continually led him towards wider questions about knowledge, from set theory to languages, algorithms and research across disciplines.

## From Moscow to Set Theory

He was born in Moscow into a family where the natural sciences were part of everyday culture. In 1928, at seventeen, Lyapunov entered the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics at Moscow University, but soon had to interrupt his studies. From the autumn of 1930 he followed a second path: working in research institutes while developing as a mathematician.

Meeting Nikolai Luzin was decisive. From 1932 Lyapunov entered the orbit of Luzin’s celebrated school, receiving an education in descriptive set theory. In 1934 he began working at the Steklov Mathematical Institute, where he developed as a researcher and published on the properties of sets and the special operations used in set theory.

## Abstraction and Practical Experience

One line of Lyapunov’s work sounds modern even in its name: the convexity theorem for the range of a nonatomic vector measure, often called Lyapunov’s convexity theorem. It shows how a very abstract formulation can produce a tool that later finds applications in optimal control and related fields.

During the war he served in the field army. Afterwards he taught, including at the Artillery Academy. The discipline of calculation, real problems and responsibility for results later bore unexpected fruit in his approach to programming: how can solutions be made testable, repeatable and transferable to others?

## Cybernetics and Programming

In the early 1950s his principal public role began: the struggle to have cybernetics recognized as a serious science. He organized seminars, lectured to many kinds of audiences and helped create the field’s infrastructure, including translated collections, Soviet publications and scientific councils. At the Fourth All-Union Mathematical Congress in 1961, he summed up the history in a famous phrase: “Within a short time, attitudes to cybernetics passed through the following phases: 1) categorical denial; 2) acknowledgment of its existence…”

His contribution to programming as a discipline deserves particular attention. He delivered one of the country’s earliest programming courses to Moscow University students and promoted the operator method as a more expressive and convenient notation than early flowcharts. For Lyapunov, programming was a way of thinking about an algorithm and the structure of a solution, rather than merely serving a machine.

In the 1960s he worked in the Academy’s Siberian Branch and at Novosibirsk University, building research teams, heading departments and helping make Akademgorodok a centre for cybernetics. Machine translation, mathematical linguistics, biology and education all entered his sphere. He organized research through a school, courses, seminars and new connections among mathematicians, linguists, biologists and engineers.

Lyapunov lived for sixty-one years, but achieved the essential task: moving cybernetics and programming from the status of a disputed fashion into the language of scientific work. His legacy includes papers and institutions, but also the habit of treating computation as a rigorous discipline that can be taught, developed and applied far beyond mathematics.
