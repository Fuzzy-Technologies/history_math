*Marking the 910th anniversary of his birth*

Bhaskara II (1114–1185), also known as Bhaskara Acharya, was a major Indian mathematician, astronomer and poet. He headed the astronomical observatory at Ujjain, one of Hinduism’s seven sacred cities.

## The Treatise and Its Mathematics

Bhaskara II wrote the four-part astronomical and mathematical treatise *Siddhanta Shiromani* (“Crown of Learning”, 1150). Its arithmetic part, *Lilavati* (“The Beautiful One”), remained a model textbook for centuries. It was republished many times and translated into numerous languages.

The treatise consists of 1450 verses in Sanskrit.

Bhaskara’s main topics included arithmetic with positive and negative integers and fractions; extracting square and cube roots; summing arithmetic series; finding the greatest common divisor of two numbers; and methods for solving linear and quadratic algebraic equations, including indeterminate equations with several unknowns and systems of algebraic equations. He was one of the first to consider both positive and negative roots when solving quadratic equations.

Bhaskara II also contributed to geometry. He studied the relationships between the sides of a right triangle, presented two proofs of the Pythagorean theorem—already known to earlier Chinese and Greek scholars—and studied methods for measuring volumes. He examined problems in Diophantine analysis and combinatorics.

## An Invention and a Legacy

Bhaskara II also produced one of the earliest designs for a perpetual motion machine, an “ever-turning wheel”.

Today the Leelavati Prize is awarded at the International Congress of Mathematicians for outstanding contributions to the popularization of mathematics. In 2022 the first Russian mathematician to receive it was [N. N. Andreev](https://www.mathunion.org/imu-awards/leelavati-prize/leelavati-prize-2022), creator of the Mathematical Etudes project.

The title *Lilavati* (“The Beautiful One”) is believed to be dedicated to his daughter. The book contains thirteen chapters and 278 verses, mainly on arithmetic and measurement.

The first photograph shows a page from *Lilavati*, the first volume, dated 1650. The use of the Pythagorean theorem is illustrated in the corner.

The second photograph shows a statue of Bhaskara II at Patnadevi, a historic site and tourist destination 18 km southwest of Chalisgaon, Maharashtra, India. He is believed to have written *Siddhanta Shiromani* there.
