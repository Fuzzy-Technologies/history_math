James Clerk Maxwell (1831–1879) was the physicist and mathematician whose name we associate with the equations of electromagnetism. But his letters sometimes suggest that he had theories about domestic life as well as science.

In February 1858, Maxwell tells his aunt Jane Cay that he is engaged — and immediately allays any family anxiety: do not worry, his future wife will not argue with him over formulas and, above all, will not “stop the mathematics”.

“Don’t be afraid; she is not mathematical; but there are other things besides that, and she certainly won’t stop the mathematics.”

— J. C. Maxwell, letter to his aunt Jane Cay, 18 February 1858

About a year and a half later, he is already sharing a joke with his wife Katherine (Katherine Mary Dewar) about a familiar domestic situation: scientists’ wives sometimes find themselves drawn into their husbands’ science, though that is hardly why they married them.

“Mrs. Sabine learnt mathematics of her husband after she was married, so she was not married for it.”

— J. C. Maxwell, letter to his wife Katherine, 16 September 1859

The moral: Maxwell’s ideal family formula is simple — love by default, mathematics optional.

## Notes

Source: Lewis Campbell and William Garnett, *The Life of James Clerk Maxwell*, letters of 18 February 1858 and 16 September 1859.
