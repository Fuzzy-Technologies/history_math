The planimeter, or integrator, is one of the oldest instruments for approximately determining the areas of plane figures enclosed by closed boundaries.

It is a mechanical instrument, now also made in electronic form: an analogue computing device.

Its name comes from the Latin *planum*, “flat surface” or “area”, and the Greek *metrein*, “I measure”.

Planimeters are used in design work, including cutting out material and working with topographic maps, plans and drawings.

There are various planimeter designs: polar, linear, hatchet and circular planimeters, and pantograph planimeters. The automation of area measurement also led to the electronic planimeter.

One technical implementation, the Amsler–Coradi planimeter, came into widespread use.

The instrument works by measuring the lengths of arcs traced on a surface by a special wheel. As the planimeter follows the boundary to be measured, it relates that boundary to a rectangle of known side lengths with the same area as the figure. When a straight segment AB moves in the plane, the area of the figure it “sweeps out”, shown shaded, equals the length of AB multiplied by the length of the arc traced by the segment’s midpoint.

Mechanical planimeters have been known for a long time. They consist of two jointed arms: the pole arm and the tracing arm.

The readings allow the area of the traced figure to be calculated in planimeter divisions. Multiplying the reading by the value of one division gives the area of the measured region.

## The polar planimeter {#section-1}

Polar instruments rotate around one fixed point, the pole, when in use. A polar planimeter consists of a pole arm and a tracing arm. A needle in the underside of a weight attached to one end of the pole arm forms the planimeter’s pole. The other end has a pin with a ball-shaped head. At the end of the tracing arm is a lens marked with a circle and a tracing point at its centre. The carriage contains a counting mechanism. A special device, a [vernier](https://teletype.in/%40history_math/Nonius), provides readings from the measuring wheel.

## The linear planimeter {#section-2}

In use, linear planimeters move over a plan parallel to their initial position. Their tracing arms and guide rules have different lengths, adapted to strip charts of different widths. They differ in how they ensure the straight translational motion of the tracing arm: a groove in a special guide rule, rollers, or movement of the chart paper relative to the tracing arm.

## The hatchet planimeter {#section-3}

One of the simplest planimeters is an iron rod bent into a squared U shape, with one end pointed and the other blunt and flattened.

## The circular planimeter {#section-4}

The Russian inventor P. A. Zarubin proposed an original planimeter design, for which he received the Demidov Prize in 1854.

## Historical background {#section-6}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

In the early 1850s, the polar and circular planimeters were invented almost simultaneously and independently by the Swiss professor Jakob Amsler (1854) and our compatriot Pavel Alekseevich Zarubin (1852). Their designs replaced Cartesian coordinates with polar coordinates, considerably simplifying construction.

Linear planimeters were invented independently by different people at almost the same time in the first third of the nineteenth century. The Bavarian engineer Johann Martin Herman, who proposed the instrument’s design in 1814, is regarded as its first inventor. Independently, the instrument was invented by the Florentine professor Tito Gonnella in 1824 and by the Swiss engineer Johannes Oppikofer in 1827.
