An exceptional German mathematician, Gauss also worked in astronomy, physics and geodesy.

He was born on 30 April 1777 in Brunswick. His extraordinary mathematical abilities were noticed in childhood. He studied at the University of Göttingen. From 1807 until his death, he directed the astronomical observatory in Göttingen and held a university professorship.

Gauss’s scientific interests were extraordinarily broad, bringing theoretical and applied questions together naturally. His work profoundly influenced the subsequent development of higher algebra, number theory, differential geometry, the theory of electricity and magnetism, geodesy, theoretical astronomy and the theory of attraction.

In 1796 Gauss made his celebrated discovery that a regular seventeen-sided polygon could be constructed with compass and straightedge. He achieved astonishing virtuosity in calculation, extending existing tables of prime numbers and quadratic residues. In his doctoral dissertation of 1799, he gave the first widely accepted proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra.

Gauss’s fundamental *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801) is regarded as the beginning of modern number theory. In 1831 he developed the arithmetic of the complex integers that now bear his name. His *General Investigations of Curved Surfaces* (1827) became a foundation of the differential geometry of surfaces.

Gauss’s astronomical work of 1800–1820 was also substantial. He wrote *Theory of the Motion of Heavenly Bodies* (1809), containing methods that still underpin the calculation of planetary orbits. In effect, he created higher geodesy, presenting it in *Investigations in Higher Geodesy* (1842–1847).

The title “Prince of Mathematicians” recognises Gauss’s achievements. The Göttingen scientific society published Gauss’s collected works in twelve volumes between 1863 and 1933.
