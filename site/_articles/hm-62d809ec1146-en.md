*Marking the centenary of her birth*

Yelena Georgiyevna Glagoleva (1926–2015) was a Soviet and Russian mathematics educator, a Candidate of Pedagogical Sciences and one of the organizers of the All-Union Correspondence School of Mathematics, VZMSh, attached to Moscow State University. Her name is familiar to readers brought up on Gelfand’s booklets. The school’s practical work generated texts that explained mathematics rigorously and humanely.

She was born on 8 April 1926 and graduated from the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics at the V. P. Potemkin Moscow Pedagogical Institute. She worked in the USSR Academy of Pedagogical Sciences’ mathematics laboratory, then taught higher mathematics at the Moscow Aviation Technology Institute before joining Moscow State University’s department of mathematical methods in biology. That unusual path, from education to applications and back, helps explain her careful, meaningful style and respect for the reader.

## The Correspondence School of Mathematics

VZMSh responded to a simple but important need: giving pupils in many different towns access to serious mathematics even when no strong mathematics club or teacher was nearby. I. M. Gelfand chaired the school’s academic council, and Glagoleva was its secretary and his assistant for correspondence-school affairs. Behind that dry title lay extensive work: organizing correspondence, shaping teaching methods, checking assignments and texts, and supporting teachers and group students—school mathematics clubs using VZMSh materials under a local teacher’s guidance.

The correspondence school complemented local mathematics clubs with a network for regular study: receiving assignments, sending in solutions, getting written feedback and progressing further. The school has worked for more than fifty years; today its tradition continues through the Open Lyceum All-Russian Correspondence Multidisciplinary School.

## Books and the Art of Reasoning

As part of a team led by Gelfand, she helped produce teaching aids that became classics. Many appeared both as separate booklets and within *Additional Chapters for the Mathematics Course*:

- *Functions and Graphs* (1968), co-authored: constructing and reading graphs using school mathematics, without derivatives, through examples and transformations;
- *The Method of Coordinates* (1973), co-authored: using coordinates to organize geometry and describe lines, circles and distances through formulas;
- *The Correspondence School of Mathematics at Moscow State University* (1973), with N. Kh. Rozov and Zh. M. Rabbot: a short but precise explanation of what correspondence education is and why it matters.

This was fundamental to VZMSh: mathematics was a school of careful writing and reasoning rather than a race. Pupils did more than find an answer: they learned to explain why it was correct and how to check their work. The booklets accordingly contain many small steps, checking questions and problems that require thought rather than guesswork.

These books were repeatedly reissued and translated. Readers value their adult tone: the author neither oversimplifies nor hides the beauty behind formalism. Observation and examples come first, followed by careful reasoning and only then a general method. This order teaches understanding rather than memorization and encourages sound mathematical judgement: checking one’s work, following the logic and refusing to glide past the meaning. Above all, it teaches readers to write solutions that persuade through thought rather than merely an answer.

## Later Writing and Legacy

Later she wrote more broadly for general readers, including “Mathematics with a Human Face” (2013), as well as *Electricity in Living Organisms*, co-authored with Mikhail Borisovich Berkinblit (Moscow: Nauka, 1988; *Kvant Library*, no. 69). The same idea is recognizable throughout: mathematics comes alive when it remains connected to reality and the human question of why.

She died on 20 July 2015. Her memory lives in people for whom VZMSh provided a first serious encounter with mathematics and in books that still feel fresh, neither keeping readers at a reverential distance nor oversimplifying their subject.
