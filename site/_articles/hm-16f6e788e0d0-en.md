*Marking the 170th anniversary of his birth*

The French mathematician, physicist, astronomer and philosopher, who also worked in mechanics, was one of the leading scholars of the turn of the twentieth century. He was president of the French Mathematical Society (1886), a member of the Paris Academy of Sciences (1887) and more than thirty-five foreign academies, including a foreign corresponding member of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences (1895).

He was born in Nancy, France, on 29 April 1854.

He mastered Latin, German and English. He studied at the École Polytechnique, where Charles Hermite was his mathematical mentor, and taught at the universities of Caen and Paris.

Poincaré’s scientific work crossed disciplinary boundaries and encompassed many areas of mathematics and its applications. The Henri Poincaré Papers bibliography lists more than 730 titles by him, while his posthumous collected works appeared in eleven volumes in Paris between 1928 and 1956.

He produced fundamental work in virtually every field of mathematics: algebraic topology, differential equations, probability, non-Euclidean geometry and the foundations of mathematics.

He founded combinatorial topology and laid the foundations of the qualitative theory of differential equations. In developing automorphic function theory, he used Lobachevsky’s geometry and gave it a new interpretation in 1882. He initiated residue theory in multidimensional complex space in 1887.

Poincaré made important contributions to the development of special relativity and devised new methods in celestial mechanics. His works contain ideas that later became fundamental to mathematical chaos theory and the general theory of dynamical systems.

In mathematical physics, he developed potential theory, heat conduction, hydrodynamics and thermodynamics.

In 1889, *Lectures on the Mathematical Theory of Light*, one volume of Poincaré’s *Course in Mathematical Physics*, appeared. The first two volumes of *New Methods of Celestial Mechanics* followed in 1892–1893 and the third in 1899.

In 1900 he chaired the logic section of the First World Congress of Philosophy in Paris. His paper “On the Principles of Mechanics” set out his philosophy: scientific principles are provisional conventions adapted to experience, without direct counterparts in reality.

In books for a general audience, including *Science and Hypothesis* (1902), *The Value of Science* (1905) and *Science and Method* (1908), he developed a conventionalist account of scientific knowledge.

At the Second International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris, he was elected president (1900).

The Institut Henri Poincaré in Paris bears his name. Henri Poincaré University in Nancy became part of the University of Lorraine in 2012.
