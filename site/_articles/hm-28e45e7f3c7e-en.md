*Marking the 410th anniversary of his birth*

John Wallis (23 November [3 December] 1616–28 October [8 November] 1703) was an English mathematician and clergyman who helped prepare the ground for calculus well before Newton and Leibniz. His seventeenth-century work brought methods for calculating areas under curves, new notation and the habit of treating infinity as a working tool.

## From Theology to Cryptanalysis

He was born in Ashford, Kent, and received a classical education with a strong emphasis on languages. At Cambridge he studied theology, took bachelor’s and master’s degrees and was ordained in 1640. Yet, paradoxically, his route into mathematics came through a practical skill rather than a university course: during the Civil War he proved himself as a cryptanalyst, helping the parliamentary side decipher intercepted messages.

In mid-1640s London, Wallis joined a circle that met to discuss natural philosophy and experiments, one of the groups from which the Royal Society would emerge. In his autobiographical account he stressed the discipline of these meetings: “we barred discussion of theology, affairs of state and news”, leaving only science and experiments.

## Oxford and Infinite Processes

In 1649 Wallis became Savilian Professor of Geometry at Oxford, a post he held for more than half a century. Oxford saw the publication of his principal mathematical books of the 1650s: a treatise on conic sections and *Arithmetica Infinitorum* (*The Arithmetic of Infinitesimals*, 1656). He systematically applied algebraic language to geometry and calculation. Rather than treating a curve only as a section of a cone, he described it through coordinates and equations, and sought areas and volumes through infinite processes.

Wallis’s most familiar contribution is his product for $\pi$, known as the Wallis product. It arose from attempts to calculate integrals involving $(1-x^2)^n$ and understand how areas change when one passes from whole-number to fractional powers. Here too appeared a word and technique important to later analysis: interpolation, inserting intermediate values where no direct formula was yet available.

Wallis also contributed to mathematical language. His infinity symbol, $\infty$, first appeared in his treatise on conic sections (1655) and became established through *Arithmetica Infinitorum*. In the later *A Treatise of Algebra* (1685), he developed algebraic ideas and wrote one of the first substantial histories of algebra, combining a textbook with a survey of sources and traditions.

Wallis introduced the terms mantissa, interpretation, interpolation and continued fraction into mathematics, and derived recurrence relations for the convergents of a continued fraction.

## The Royal Society and His Legacy

Wallis helped found the Royal Society of London in 1660 and became one of its earliest members. It serves as Britain’s national academy of sciences.

He was also a true polymath, writing on logic, grammar, theology and mechanics, participating in the scientific institutions of his time and leaving detailed autobiographical accounts. His legacy shows how a new scientific style emerged as calculation, notation and argument began to work together, gradually giving rise to calculus.
