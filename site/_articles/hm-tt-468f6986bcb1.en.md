(On the 370th anniversary of his birth)

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-1" %}

**The Swiss mathematicians and brothers Jacob and Johann Bernoulli were the first generation of the remarkable Bernoulli family of Basel.**

Let us tell the story of one of the brothers, Jacob Bernoulli (1654–1705), a founder of probability theory and mathematical analysis. He was a professor of mathematics at the University of Basel (1687–1705), a foreign member of the Paris Academy of Sciences (1699), and a member of the Berlin Academy of Sciences (1702).

He was born on 27 December 1654 (6 January 1655) in Basel, Switzerland. He studied theology at the University of Basel, but became fascinated by mathematics and taught himself the subject. At university he also mastered 5 languages: French, Italian, English, Latin and Greek. In 1671 he received a master’s degree in philosophy.

He travelled extensively in Europe, visiting France, Italy, the Netherlands and England. He became acquainted with the ideas of Descartes, Huygens and Leibniz. In 1687 he was elected professor of mathematics at the University of Basel, where he worked for the rest of his life.

Jacob Bernoulli’s work marked a stage in the development of probability theory. His *Art of Conjecturing*, published only in 1713, after his death, is a substantial treatise on probability, statistics and their practical applications, bringing together the combinatorics and probability theory of the seventeenth century. The Bernoulli distribution, important in combinatorics, bears Jacob’s name. He formally introduced the classical definition of an event’s probability as the ratio of favourable outcomes to all equally likely outcomes. He proved Bernoulli’s theorem, a special case of the law of large numbers. He constructed a mathematical model for a sequence of independent trials, the Bernoulli scheme. Through his work, probability theory acquired great practical importance.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

In 1687, Jacob came across Leibniz’s first memoir on the foundations of analysis, published in 1684, and enthusiastically began learning the new calculus. He wrote to Leibniz asking him to explain several obscure passages. He received a reply only three years later, in 1690, as Leibniz was away on business in Paris. In the meantime, Jacob Bernoulli mastered differential and integral calculus independently and introduced his brother Johann to it as well. On his return, Leibniz began an active and mutually beneficial correspondence with the Bernoulli brothers. The resulting triumvirate of Leibniz and the two brothers led European mathematics for 20 years and greatly enriched the new analysis.

Jacob Bernoulli applied the new ideas to the properties of several curves: the logarithmic spiral, cycloid, catenary and Bernoulli lemniscate. He determined the area of a spherical triangle, calculated the areas of conoidal and spheroidal surfaces, and carried out many quadratures and rectifications. He was the first to introduce the term “integral” in print.

His book *Arithmetic Applications of Infinite Series and Their Finite Sums* (1689–1704) became the first guide to the theory of series.

In 1690, Bernoulli first published a study of compound interest in which he established the existence of a limiting return, estimating it as greater than 2.5 but less than 3. In effect, he was seeking the limit of a familiar sequence, which equals the number e, known in Russian teaching as the second remarkable limit.

Together with his brother Johann, Jacob laid the foundations of the calculus of variations. He proposed and partly solved the isoperimetric problem and worked on the brachistochrone, or curve of quickest descent, posed by J. Bernoulli.

Jacob taught his brother Johann, his nephew Nicolaus, and Paul Euler, the father of L. Euler.

He was buried in Basel Minster. In accordance with his wishes, a logarithmic spiral was to be carved on his tomb, but the stonemasons carved an Archimedean spiral instead. Around it is the Latin inscription EADEM MUTATA RESURGO, “Changed, I rise again”, expressing the logarithmic spiral’s ability to recover its shape after various transformations.
