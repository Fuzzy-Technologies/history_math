> Truth cannot be consigned to oblivion  
> Once the world has set its eyes upon it;  
> The theorem Pythagoras gave us  
> Is as true today as on the day of its birth.

> Adelbert von Chamisso, translated into Russian by A. Khovansky

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The famous German astronomer Johannes Kepler called it one of geometry's treasures, comparable to a measure of gold. It is hard to find a more substantial or significant proposition anywhere in mathematics: the Pythagorean theorem is unmatched in its scientific and practical applications. It is a theorem of immense importance, simplicity and beauty of statement.

Historians of mathematics report that the relationship between the sides of a right triangle — the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the legs (a² + b² = c²) — was already known in ancient civilizations. Around the twenty-third century BCE, ancient Egypt knew the right triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5, the “Egyptian triangle,” used by the harpedonaptae, or “rope-stretchers.” Similar problems occur in ancient Babylonian texts of the twentieth century BCE and ancient Chinese texts of the fifth century BCE.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

In the Pythagorean school, geometry changed from a collection of recipes for solving area-measurement problems into an abstract science. The systematic introduction of proofs is regarded as Pythagoras's most important scientific achievement.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

Pythagoras is commonly credited with the first proof of this most popular geometrical theorem, now named after him.

Its geometrical statement is: the square constructed on the hypotenuse of a right triangle has the same area as the sum of the squares constructed on its legs.

Pythagoras's proof is surrounded by legends. One says he sacrificed a hundred oxen to the gods for discovering this truth. That is unlikely, however: Cicero observed that the Pythagorean order's rules rejected all bloodshed.

In Euclid, this is called the “nymph's theorem” because the diagram resembles a butterfly, or nymph. Since “nymph” also means a goddess or bride, the Arabs called it the “bride's theorem.”

There are many proofs — about 400 — of this theorem: geometrical, algebraic, mechanical and trigonometric. Pythagoras's own proof has not survived.

Modern school textbooks mostly give simple algebraic derivations of the formula. In doing so, they lose the theorem's original geometrical aura and the path that led ancient thinkers to the truth. Schools should therefore find opportunities to introduce pupils to classical geometrical proofs, including those known from ancient treatises.

An interesting Indian-style proof, associated with Bhaskara II in the twelfth century, says simply: “Behold!”

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

Pupils can also be introduced to the beautiful Arabic “bride's chair” proof.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

One diagram of the proof inspired a popular Russian schoolchildren's saying: “Pythagoras's trousers are equal in every direction.”

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

## Facts {#section-1}

1. The Pythagorean theorem is a special case of the more general law of cosines, which relates the side lengths of any triangle.

2. Its converse is also true: if the square of one side of a triangle equals the sum of the squares of its other two sides, the triangle is right-angled.
