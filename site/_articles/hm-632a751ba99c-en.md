In the USSR, mathematics sometimes had a flavour of its own. In the 1960s and 1970s, many people knew the prices of Moskovskaya Osobaya vodka by heart: a half-litre bottle cost 2.87 roubles, and a quarter-litre bottle 1.49.

One day, someone noticed a remarkable “folk equation”:

$1.49^{2.87}=3.14083\ldots\approx\pi$.

Raise the price of the quarter-litre bottle to the power of the price of the half-litre bottle, and you get almost exactly $\pi$.

Then comes the best part. After the first edition of *Mathematicians Joke Too* appeared, Ilya Abramovich Leenson, an associate professor at Moscow State University’s Faculty of Chemistry, sent the author a comment. Long before calculators, he had checked the formula using tables and obtained 3.1408. Close, but “not perfect”.

And if it was not perfect, the fault must lie with the state’s lack of precision rather than with mathematics. But could that possibly be?!

Leenson even tried to find out how Gosplan, the State Planning Committee, calculated prices “to hundredths of a kopek” before rounding them. At the time, he was refused the exact figures on grounds of secrecy. Only in the early 1990s did he obtain the precise calculated amounts:

- The quarter-litre bottle actually cost 1 rouble 49.09 kopeks (retail price 1.49 roubles).
- The half-litre bottle cost 2 roubles 86.63 kopeks (retail price 2.87 roubles).

And then it came out beautifully:

$1.4909^{2.8663}\approx3.1416\approx\pi$.

One can only marvel: either this was a virtuoso feat of planned arithmetic, or the staff at Gosplan had a fondness for $\pi$ too.
