*For my birthday*

Hello, friends! It’s my birthday, so I’ll turn our usual profile around and tell you about the person who brings you problems, stories and old books every week. My friends call me Mansur. To my grandson and the rest of the family, I’m Papont.

## From School to Teaching

I was born on 4 August 1950 in Izhevsk. I spent my childhood and school years in Tatarstan, at Begishevo station, where teachers and rigorous school mathematics quickly taught me discipline in thought. Then came the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics at Kazan University. Somewhere along the way I fell irrevocably in love with algebra: it teaches us to see structure, not merely to calculate.

I worked in Yelabuga for many decades. On paper, I am an associate professor in the Department of Mathematics and Applied Informatics; in practice, I am someone who cannot live without a blackboard, chalk and a good problem. I have taught mathematics for more than half a century and continue to do so. Every new intake of students convinces me that what matters most is not formulas, but a taste for reasoning.

Over time, my research and teaching interests shifted towards the history of mathematics: how concepts arise, why one notation takes hold while another disappears, and how the past helps a teacher speak to a class in human terms. My dissertation concerned the development of the historical component of future teachers’ mathematical and pedagogical culture. Ever since, I have tried to present history as a tool for understanding mathematics rather than a digression.

## Teaching People to Reason

Over the years I have also been an organizer, from student projects and festivals to intensive educational programmes. For several years I directed the Republican Summer School of Physics and Mathematics. There you can see especially clearly how quickly children’s eyes light up when mathematics stops being frightening and becomes a playful problem.

I hold to a simple teaching principle: first teach people to reason aloud, record their steps honestly and feel no shame in making a mistake. Then formulas cease to weigh them down and begin to help.

## Books, Students and the Channel

In recent years I have gathered into books material that usually stays in the margins of lecture notes. My textbook *History of Mathematics* (2018) grew out of a course and many years of notes. In December 2024, *The Mathematician’s Bible* appeared: a modular course in higher and linear algebra, number theory and polynomials, with historical interludes and concentric returns to its central ideas. I want algebra to sound contemporary and practical, from operations on sets and the basics of number theory to cryptography and data analysis.

There is another thread in my life: student culture. In Yelabuga I am sometimes called the father of KVN, the team comedy competition, and I do not object: humour is oxygen in a university. A student newspaper once said that I had allowed students to call me Mansur-abyi. I do love that form of address: it combines respect without distance with a teacher’s warm sense of responsibility.

And, of course, there is this channel. *Mathematics with Mansur-abyi* is my conversation about mathematics as part of historical culture. We shall carry on: profiles, problems, original sources, little-known episodes, and notes on books and people who contributed to science. I still love poring over old texts, checking dates, finding the original formulation and turning it into a clear story.

## A Parting Thought

Finally, a small piece of advice. Stay curious, do not fear a difficult text, and keep asking yourself why things work as they do. And may algebra, as we wrote in one of our notes, act as the mediator between spirit and matter.

I also often recall Poisson’s words: “Life is adorned by two things: doing mathematics and teaching it.” If a little of that adornment reaches you too, then my efforts are worthwhile.
