When Leonhard Euler was nineteen, the Paris Academy of Sciences announced a competition on a thoroughly practical question: how best to position the masts on a ship. The young Swiss mathematician, who had never yet seen a real seagoing ship, set about solving it as though he had spent his whole life designing fleets.

He did not win the top prize: that went to Pierre Bouguer, a real expert in the mathematics of ships. Euler’s entry, however, received an honourable distinction, an accessit, and for a nineteen-year-old graduate it was a significant contribution to the world of science.

The funny part is not even that Euler solved a “nautical problem” without having seen the sea. What is amusing is how thoroughly Eulerian it all is: given a good problem, the ship, the mast and the waves can wait. Mathematics comes first.

He repaid his debt to the sea later, writing major works on shipbuilding and navigation. So a story that began almost as a joke ended in some very serious science.

Further information and sources:

- [Leonhard Euler](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Euler/) (MacTutor): biographical information on the 1727 Grand Prize problem about masts, Bouguer as the winner, and Euler’s entry as an important early success.

- [Paris Academy of Sciences](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Societies/Paris/): a list of prizes records Bouguer and Camus as the winners of the 1727 mast problem, with Euler receiving a lesser but noteworthy distinction.

- [About Leonhard Euler](https://euler.euclid.int/about-leonhard-euler/) (EFMU): a biographical account, including a YouTube video, with the striking detail that Euler had not yet “seen a ship”.

- Walter Gautschi, [*Leonhard Euler: His Life, the Man, and His Works*](https://www.euler-2007.ch/doc/EulerLec.pdf) (PDF): an account of the young Euler successfully entering the competition despite never having seen a ship.
