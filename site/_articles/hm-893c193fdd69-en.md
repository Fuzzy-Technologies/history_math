Viktor Yakovlevich Bunyakovsky was a Russian mathematician, educator and historian of mathematics. He became an extraordinary member of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences in 1830 and served as its vice-president from 1864 to 1889. He held honorary memberships of Moscow (1858), St Petersburg (1860), Kazan (1875), Kharkiv (1875), Kyiv (1876) and Novorossiya (1878) universities, as well as foreign and Russian learned societies.

He was born on 3 December 1804, or 15 December in the Gregorian calendar, in Bar, Podolia Governorate, in the Russian Empire.

He received his elementary education in Moscow and his higher education in Switzerland and France, at the Lausanne Academy and the Sorbonne. He studied with Laplace, Poisson, Fourier, Cauchy, Ampère, Legendre and other celebrated scholars, working most closely with Augustin-Louis Cauchy. There he defended his dissertation and received a doctorate in mathematics in 1825. He returned to St Petersburg in 1826 and began teaching.

His research focused chiefly on number theory and probability with applications. His *Foundations of the Mathematical Theory of Probability* (1846) gives an original exposition of the theory, a history of its origins and development, and applications to insurance and demography.

In 1853 he published the monograph *Parallel Lines*. It presented the principal existing proofs of the theory of parallel lines, critically examined them and set out his own observations and investigations.

He also compiled a *Lexicon of Pure and Applied Mathematics*, reaching as far as the Cyrillic letter Е. The dictionary played an important role in establishing Russian mathematical terminology.

He wrote secondary-school textbooks: *Arithmetic* (1844) and *A Programme and Synopsis of Arithmetic* (1849). He was interested in practical computation and proposed an improved version of the Russian abacus. Bunyakovsky’s self-counting abacus was a calculating mechanism based on the Russian abacus, designed to add large numbers of two-digit numbers. He also invented the Bunyakovsky planimeter.
