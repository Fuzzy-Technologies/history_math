A surveying compass, or *boussole*, from the French word for “compass”, is a surveying instrument for measuring horizontal angles between the magnetic meridian and the direction of an object: magnetic azimuths and bearings.

Surveying compasses are improved compasses equipped with sighting devices and more finely divided graduated circles, mounted on tripods.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Among technical surveying compasses suitable for school work, the Stefan compass deserves mention. It was designed in the mid-nineteenth century by Gustav Stefan, a military engineer and head of the Nicholas General Staff Academy. It has an outer circle, called the limb, graduated in degrees. A ruler called an alidade rotates around a vertical axis to sight a direction. Its edge is bevelled towards the limb and carries a vernier scale for reading fractions of a degree.

Sighting is performed through diopters on the alidade. These take the form of vertical plates, one called the eye plate and the other the object plate.

A flat cylindrical case sits on the outer limb and houses the compass proper, with its magnetic needle. The inner limb is graduated at one-degree intervals.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

The surveying compass is used in topographic and geodetic work and for orienting a plane table. Its applications include geology, archaeology and forestry.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

In military work, it is used to establish the surveyed positions of elements of troop formations. A periscopic artillery compass is used for artillery fire control, target designation, studying terrain and observing fire.

The marine bearing compass is also a type of surveying compass.
