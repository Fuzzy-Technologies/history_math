You all know Pascal’s triangle, also called the arithmetic triangle. Here is another use for this triangle of numbers.

Joining the digits in its first five rows gives powers of 11:

$1=11^0$.<br>
$11=11^1$.<br>
$121=11^2$.<br>
$1331=11^3$.<br>
$14641=11^4$.

In computing, joining symbols like this is called concatenation.

Can we use this property to calculate other powers of 11?

Later rows contain two-digit coefficients, then three-digit and larger ones. How should concatenation be modified?

Consider the fifth power of 11. Each coefficient with two or more digits produces a carry into the next higher place.

Concatenation gives `15101051`. Put the binomial coefficients of 10 or greater in brackets: `15(10)(10)51`. Then apply what we will call “decatenation”. Keep the units digit of each such number and carry its remaining digits into the next higher place, adding them to the number already there. `1(5 + 1)(0 + 1)051 = 161051`. This is $11^5$.

For $11^6$, use the next row of Pascal’s triangle:

`16(15)(20)(15)61 = 1(6 + 1)(5 + 2)(0 + 1)561 = 1771561` $=11^6$.

Try calculating $11^7$ with this algorithm yourself.

Three-digit binomial coefficients make things more complicated.

$11^{10}$ =<br>
`1(10)(45)(120)(210)(252)(210)(120)(45)(10)1` =<br>
`(1 + 1)(0 + 4)(5 + 12)(0 + 21)(0 + 25)(2 + 21)(0 + 12)(0 + 4)(5 + 1)01` =<br>
`2(4 + 1)(7 + 2)(1 + 2)(5 + 2)424601` =<br>
$25937424601$.

Of course a calculator makes these computations easier. But here we are experimenting with numbers.

P.S. These powers can also be calculated recursively, reducing multiplication to addition:

$11^{n+1}=10\cdot11^n+11^n$.

$11^6=10\cdot11^5+11^5=1610510+161051=1771561$.

## Notes

Here is just the answer: $11^7=19487171$. Try writing out the row of coefficients yourself, following the example above.
