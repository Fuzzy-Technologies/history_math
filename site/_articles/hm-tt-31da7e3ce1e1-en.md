There are several methods for determining whether the results of statistical measurements follow a normal distribution. One visual application of nomograms in mathematical statistics is probability paper, or the Weibull plot. It is named after Waloddi Weibull (1887–1979), a Swedish engineer and mathematician who described the theory of continuous distributions in 1951.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Probability paper, also described as “probabilistic paper” or a “probability plot”, is paper with a specially designed grid. It is constructed so that the cumulative distribution function of a normal distribution appears as a straight line. This is achieved by changing the scale on the vertical axis.

This “straightening” property provides a simple way to test the hypothesis that a given sample comes from a normal population.

If the empirical cumulative distribution function plotted on probability paper is reasonably well approximated by a straight line, there is reason to believe that the population from which the sample was drawn is approximately normal. The advantage of the method is that this conclusion can be reached without knowing the numerical values of the distribution's parameters.

If the points instead follow a curve, the distribution differs from the normal distribution; the curvature and asymmetry of its probability density function can then be assessed.

Probability paper can be made for any distribution. All that is required is to adjust the scale so that the distribution function's dependence on the random variable is represented by a straight line.

Today, normality can be checked in spreadsheet applications such as MS Excel.
