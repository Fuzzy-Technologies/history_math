*Marking the 140th anniversary of his birth*

Hermann Klaus Hugo Weyl (1885–1955) was a German mathematician and theoretical physicist, one of the greatest mathematicians of the twentieth century. He received the Lobachevsky Prize in 1927.

He was born on 9 November 1885 in Elmshorn, a small town near Hamburg.

## Göttingen and Zurich

In 1904 he entered the University of Göttingen and became a student of David Hilbert. He completed his studies and defended his dissertation in 1908, then taught at the university for five years.

From 1913 to 1930 he was a professor at the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology in Zurich. He took a deep interest in physics, met Einstein there and became an early, active supporter of general relativity. He lectured on the new theory of gravitation and considered how it might be developed further. His influential book *Space–Time–Matter* (1918) presented the results. In 1927 he received the international N. I. Lobachevsky Prize for outstanding work in geometry.

## Mathematics, Space and Physics

Weyl made substantial contributions to topology, complex analysis, number theory, mathematical logic, differential equations, differential geometry, continuous groups and their representations and invariants, electromagnetic-wave propagation, general relativity, statistical physics, quantum mechanics, the foundations of mathematics and the philosophy of science.

His *The Concept of a Riemann Surface*, originally *Die Idee der Riemannschen Fläche* (1913), became a classic, providing the first fully rigorous definition of a Riemann surface.

In 1918 he proposed a convenient system of axioms for affine and Euclidean point spaces, known as Weyl’s axiom system.

His work on applied linear algebra helped lay the groundwork for mathematical programming.

*The Theory of Groups and Quantum Mechanics* proved useful in quantum field theory and atomic physics.

## Princeton and the Unity of Ideas

In 1930, on the retiring Hilbert’s recommendation, Weyl returned to Göttingen as his teacher’s successor. After the Nazis came to power in 1933, Weyl, whose wife, the philosopher Helene Joseph, was Jewish, emigrated to the United States. He worked at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. In 1951 he retired and returned to Zurich.

Hermann Weyl’s life and work bridge pure thought and the structure of the universe, rigorous proof and philosophical intuition. His legacy is more than a list of theorems and formulas: it is an idea of unity. He sought—and succeeded—to bring different realms of human thought together in a grand, harmonious landscape.
