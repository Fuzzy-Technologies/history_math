*Marking the 115th anniversary of his birth*

A prominent Soviet mathematics educator, specialist in teaching methods, scholar and educational leader. He wrote school teaching books and books on recreational mathematics and became a professor in 1967.

His name was well known both in the USSR and abroad. Fyodor Fyodorovich made an enormous contribution to higher mathematics education at the Lenin Kirov State Pedagogical Institute, where he worked for forty-three years, progressing from a graduate of the mathematics division of the Vyatka Pedagogical Institute to professor.

## Education and Work at the Institute

He was born on 5 February 1909, or 18 February in the Gregorian calendar, in Karpukhino, Veliky Ustyug District, Vologda Governorate.

He attended Veliky Ustyug Teacher Training College in 1924–1927 and the Vyatka Pedagogical Institute in 1930–1933. He was appointed to teach mathematics and mathematics teaching methods at Sanchursk Teacher Training College. At the end of August 1934 he joined the Mathematics Department at the Vyatka Pedagogical Institute as an assistant. He completed postgraduate study at the RSFSR People’s Commissariat of Education’s Research Institute of Secondary Schools in 1936–1938, supervised by A. Ya. Khinchin and E. S. Berezanskaya.

In 1939 he successfully defended his Candidate of Pedagogical Sciences dissertation, “Questions of Studying Functions in Secondary-School Mathematics”, at the Moscow State Pedagogical Institute. That year, Docent Nagibin became dean of the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics, remaining in the post until November 1943. He was director of the Kirov Pedagogical Institute in 1943–1944 and headed the Department of Mathematical Analysis and Mathematics Teaching Methods in 1947–1969.

He taught courses in mathematical analysis, functions of a real variable, analytic functions, the history of mathematics and mathematics teaching methods.

## Research and Textbooks

His first work, the article “Developing Visual Estimation in Mathematics Lessons”, was published in issue 4 of *Mathematics at School* in 1935.

His scholarly and teaching-method legacy comprises thirty-five books, textbooks and teaching books, and thirty-nine articles in national publications, twenty-nine of them in *Mathematics at School*. For the first thirty years of his career he worked mainly on teaching algebra in secondary school. From 1965 his research interests shifted towards geometry in the eight-year school.

In the 1964 All-Union competition for geometry textbooks for the eight-year school, *Geometry: A Textbook for Grades 6–8*, by F. F. Nagibin, A. F. Semenovich and R. S. Cherkasov, was judged the best. It was published by Prosveshchenie in 1967.

Nagibin wrote five secondary-school problem collections, alone or with coauthors: one on geometry, two on algebra and two for extracurricular work.

## Training Researchers and Recreational Mathematics

He made a major contribution to training researchers and teachers for higher education. In 1947 the Department of Mathematical Analysis and Mathematics Teaching Methods at the Kirov State Pedagogical Institute began offering postgraduate study in mathematics teaching methods, with Docent Nagibin as supervisor. Twenty-three students completed postgraduate study under him. His supervision gave rise to a distinctive school of research, “The Theory and Methods of Teaching Mathematical Problem Solving”.

His best-known collection of engaging and unconventional problems for secondary-school pupils is *A Mathematical Treasure Chest* (*Matematicheskaya shkatulka*, in Russian; Uchpedgiz, 1958). After E. S. Kanin revised it in 1984, the book carried two authors’ names, F. F. Nagibin and E. S. Kanin. It was republished in 1988 and 2006 and translated into nine languages. Its total print run exceeds two million copies. *A Mathematical Treasure Chest* has lived on for more than fifty years!

## Notes

Five principal directions can be distinguished in Nagibin’s work:

1. Teaching mathematical problem solving.
2. Teaching algebra and introductory mathematical analysis in secondary school.
3. Teaching geometry.
4. General approaches to mathematics teaching.
5. Developing pupils’ thinking and creativity through learning mathematics.
