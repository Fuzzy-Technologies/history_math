The **proportional compass** is a remarkable instrument for enlarging or reducing line segments in a chosen ratio. It also helps divide them into equal parts, quickly and accurately, without calculation.

## History of the invention {#section-1}

In the late sixteenth century, mathematicians, engineers and artists faced the problem of scaling drawings. How could proportions be transferred from one object to another? How could dimensions be converted without complicated arithmetic?

Geometry supplied the answer. The proportional, or dividing, compass emerged as an instrument combining mechanics and mathematics. It proved indispensable to architects, cartographers, military engineers and sculptors.

Key dates:

- 1567: Fabrizio Mordente, an Italian mathematician from Salerno, published a treatise on an “eight-point compass.”
- 1595–1598: Galileo Galilei improved the instrument. Together with the craftsman Mazzoleni, he created a “military compass” used in artillery and surveying.

## Construction and operation {#section-2}

A proportional compass consists of:

- two graduated legs joined by a pivot;
- a movable slide that sets the scale factor;
- scales with fixed ratios, including 1:2, 2:3, the golden ratio, π and others.

How it works:

1. Set the slide to the desired division, for example 1:2.
2. Measure a segment between the tips of one pair of legs.
3. The other pair automatically gives the proportional length.

The instrument implements geometrical similarity: proportions remain unchanged regardless of scale.

## Development and use {#section-3}

- Seventeenth century: compasses with artillery scales and navigation markings appeared.
- Eighteenth century: brass models with precision adjustment.
- Nineteenth century: folding versions for fieldwork.

## The proportional compass in education {#section-4}

In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, these compasses were used in school geometry. They appear in textbooks by:

- A. P. Kiselev (1960);
- A. F. Malinin and F. I. Egorov (1886);
- N. N. Nikitin (1969).

They were included in sections on similarity and scaling as a practical illustration.

## In sculpture and design {#section-5}

A compass with curved legs—the proportional caliper—is popular with sculptors. It lets them quickly take measurements from life and transfer them to their work in the required proportion.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

## Modern significance {#section-6}

The principle of proportional division underlies digital tools:

- “virtual compasses” in CAD programs;
- scaling and transformation algorithms;
- cryptographic methods, such as RSA, involving division into proportional blocks.

The proportional compass is an ingenious physical embodiment of similarity. It aided Renaissance mathematicians, serves sculptors and engineers today, and laid a foundation for algorithms of the future digital age.
