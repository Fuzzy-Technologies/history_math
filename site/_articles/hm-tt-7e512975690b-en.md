The overhead projector, known in Russian as a kodoskop — an abbreviation of “classroom optical board” — is a type of projector widely used in mathematics teaching. Any teaching material, whether text, a diagram or a drawing, transferred photographically or graphically onto a transparent support can be projected onto a screen or classroom board. These materials are called transparencies. They can be made photographically or with an easily erasable felt-tip pen used to draw on transparent film.

Unlike other types of projector, the overhead projector has a large condenser, a mirror-and-lens optical system. It lies horizontally, allowing transparencies up to the size of a typewritten page to be placed on it. Several transparencies can also be superimposed, creating the effect of watching a construction as it develops — the “fold-out” method.

The overhead projector is convenient for explaining and consolidating new material, working on mathematical exercises and problems, setting independent work and tests, organizing individual work and displaying homework assignments.

The first prototypes appeared in 1927 and were improved by the engineer Walther Bauersfeld, who built the Belsazar projection apparatus in 1931.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Overhead projectors became widespread thanks to the American company 3M, which began mass-producing them for educational institutions in 1957. From the late 1970s, they appeared in most Soviet and Russian schools and universities, where they remained in use until the early 2000s. They then began to be displaced by document cameras, interactive whiteboards and modern digital multimedia presentation systems. Some institutions still use overhead projectors because they are simple and convenient to operate.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

The USSR produced several types specifically intended for educational institutions, such as the Lektor-2000. Various types of domestically manufactured Orion overhead projector continue to be produced today.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

An overhead projector has an illumination system similar to a slide projector's, but with a vertical optical axis. The transparent originals, changed manually, are placed horizontally on a flat square Fresnel lens that serves as the condenser. The image on the screen is formed by a projection lens positioned vertically above the Fresnel lens and fitted with a mirror at 45° to the illumination system's optical axis. As a result, the light changes direction from vertical to horizontal and reaches the screen, which can be any evenly coloured white surface. A special white classroom board or a light-coloured wall was usually used.
