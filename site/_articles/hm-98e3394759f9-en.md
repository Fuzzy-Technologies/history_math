A Persian philosopher, astronomer and mathematician, Khayyam is best known as the poet of the *Rubaiyat*.

He was born on 18 May 1048 in Nishapur in northern Iran, where he is also buried. He received an excellent education in Bukhara and Samarkand, studying astronomy and mathematics. Many rulers of the East invited him to their courts. At the invitation of Sultan Malik-Shah and his vizier Nizam al-Mulk, he moved to Isfahan, capital of the Seljuk Empire. From 1074 Khayyam led the scholars working at the observatory established there; this period lasted about eighteen years, until Malik-Shah’s death in 1092.

He compiled the *Astronomical Tables of Malik-Shah* and helped reform the solar calendar introduced in 1079. The Jalali calendar became an important part of the Persian tradition of solar time-reckoning; Iran adopted the modern official Solar Hijri calendar in 1925. In 1976 the country changed the calendar era rather than abolishing the solar calendar, and the earlier era was restored in 1978. The estimate of the tropical year associated with Khayyam’s work was exceptionally accurate for its time.

Khayyam systematically classified equations of degree at most three within the algebraic tradition of his time. He developed the algebraic method for solving quadratic equations, following al-Khwarizmi, and geometric methods for solving cubic equations by means of conic sections. He did not reach explicit algebraic formulas for cubic equations, of the kind later associated with Cardano. In *On the Demonstration of Problems of al-jabr and al-muqabala* (1069), he also defined the subject of algebra: finding unknown quantities related to known quantities by equations. Algebra was thus treated as the science of equations.

Khayyam also took up Euclid’s fifth postulate, a problem scholars before him had tried unsuccessfully to resolve. In 1077 he completed his *Commentary on the Difficult Postulates of Euclid’s Book*. Without knowing it, he came close to problems that would later be solved by the brilliant Russian geometer Lobachevsky.

By studying ratios of magnitudes and solving algebraic problems geometrically, Khayyam developed connections between algebra and geometry, though he did not create analytic geometry in its later sense. Khayyam also worked in geography, natural science and philosophy. His work in these fields made him a polymath.
