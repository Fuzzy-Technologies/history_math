*Marking the 525th anniversary of his birth*

Girolamo Cardano (24 September 1501–21 September 1576) was an Italian Renaissance mathematician, physician, engineer and philosopher. In the history of algebra he is remembered for a book after which cubic and quartic equations ceased to be a secret craft and became a subject of public scholarship.

## Physician and Author of Ars Magna

Cardano was born in Pavia, studied there and in Padua, and trained in medicine. He became well known as a physician in Milan and beyond, and historians of medicine still discuss his clinical observations. Mathematics was no secondary hobby for him, but part of a single undertaking: understanding the world through number, measure and mechanism.

His principal mathematical monument is *The Great Art, or the Rules of Algebra* (*Ars magna, sive de regulis algebraicis*, 1545). In it Cardano first brought together and published methods for solving cubic equations, received through the tradition of del Ferro, Fiore and Tartaglia. Equally importantly, he presented the solution of the quartic associated with his pupil Lodovico Ferrari. He did more than record recipes: he showed how particular cases could form a general picture of algebraic transformations.

## Numbers, Mechanisms and Chance

*Ars magna* also contains an episode that would later open the way to complex numbers: for some cubic equations, the formulas lead to roots of negative quantities. Cardano treated them cautiously, but their appearance within a valid procedure mattered more than his reservations. Mathematics was already demanding an enlarged concept of number.

Cardano was a man of books and of devices. His name is associated with the universal joint, which transmits rotation at an angle, and the Cardan grille, a stencil-based cipher. For him, these inventions expressed the same reasoning: form and rule could work in algebra, mechanics and cryptography alike.

Chance formed another thread. His *Book on Games of Chance* (*Liber de ludo aleae*) was written in the sixteenth century and published after his death. It is now often seen as an early step towards probabilistic thinking: counting possibilities, fair play and the price of risk rather than fate and luck.

## A Dramatic Life

Cardano’s life was dramatic. He endured family tragedies, disputes and persecution. In 1570 the Inquisition arrested him, in part over his astrological writings. After his release he moved to Rome, where he received support at the papal court and continued writing. He died there, leaving a legacy that still works: algebraic formulas, but also the idea that mathematics should be published, tested and discussed.
