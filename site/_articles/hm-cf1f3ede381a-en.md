An outstanding Norwegian mathematician.

He was born on 5 August 1802 in Finnøy, Norway.

His brief life was marked by hardship. His family lived on the verge of poverty. At school he became fascinated by mathematics; his teachers noted his exceptional ability and predicted a great future for him.

Abel studied at the University of Christiania, now Oslo. In 1824, while still at university, he published his proof that the general quintic cannot be solved by radicals. The work earned him a scholarship to continue his education abroad. He visited Berlin, Paris and Italy. After returning, he lived in severe poverty; in 1828 he obtained temporary teaching work at the university. He was elected to the Royal Norwegian Society of Sciences and Letters. He died of tuberculosis.

During his short life, Abel made mathematical discoveries of the greatest importance.

A well-known saying traditionally attributed to Charles Hermite holds that Abel left mathematicians enough work for five hundred years.

Independently of Jacobi, Abel developed the theory of elliptic functions, for which the Paris Academy of Sciences awarded them a prize in 1830. The integral equation known as Abel’s equation bears his name. His name survives in terms such as abelian differentials, integrals, equations, functions and varieties, as well as in convergence tests.

In 2002, to mark the bicentenary of Abel’s birth, the Norwegian government established the Abel Prize in mathematics. It quickly gained a reputation as the “Nobel Prize in mathematics”, becoming one of the most prestigious awards in the field. Its founders aimed to encourage mathematicians of international standing and to promote modern mathematics. Nobel had not planned an award for mathematics.
