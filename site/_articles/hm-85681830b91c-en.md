*Marking the 435th anniversary of his birth*

Girard Desargues (1591–1661) was a French geometer, architect and engineer. He is regarded as a founder of projective geometry, where perspective becomes more than an artist’s technique: it becomes a rigorous mathematical language.

He was born in Lyon on 21 February 1591. Well educated, he took an early interest in practical geometry: perspective, construction problems and engineering calculations. He served in the army as a military engineer.

In the 1620s and 1630s he worked in Paris and joined Marin Mersenne’s circle, where the latest developments in mathematics and mechanics were discussed. Descartes, the elder and younger Pascal and other scholars of the time met there.

## A New Language for Geometry

Desargues thought boldly and in new ways. He proposed studying figures through the properties preserved by projection rather than through lengths and angles. A crucial step was to regard parallel lines as meeting at a point at infinity. A circle, ellipse, parabola and hyperbola could thus be seen as relatives: different projections of the same conic.

His principal mathematical work, *Brouillon project…* (1639), concerned conic sections and projective properties. Only a very small number of copies—reportedly about fifty—were printed, and the book soon became rare. Desargues introduced the language of pencils of lines and ranges of points, carefully examined situations involving infinite distances and showed how to discuss conics through properties unchanged by projection. In effect, it was a draft of the projective geometry that would be reborn much later.

The book’s history became something of a detective story. Leibniz later tried unsuccessfully to obtain a copy, and for a long time the work was known chiefly through manuscript copies, one of them made by Philippe de La Hire. Desargues’s ideas consequently circulated among a small group of specialists rather than through textbooks.

## A Theorem, Pascal and Rediscovery

His name is also attached to the famous theorem on two triangles. If the lines joining corresponding vertices meet at a point, the triangles are in perspective from a centre; the intersections of corresponding sides then lie on one line, giving perspective from an axis, and conversely. The theorem first appeared in print later, in a work on perspective prepared by Desargues’s pupil Abraham Bosse.

Desargues had a real influence on his contemporaries. In 1640, while proving his mystic hexagon property of conics, the sixteen-year-old Blaise Pascal explicitly acknowledged his debt to Desargues’s ideas and his efforts to follow that method. Yet a paradox followed: after Desargues’s death, his geometry almost disappeared from view. His language and style were ahead of their time, while mathematicians’ interests shifted towards analysis.

Only in the nineteenth century, as projective geometry became a discipline in its own right, was his contribution rediscovered and appreciated. Projective thinking became a bridge between drawings, perspective, conics and rigorous proof.

## Engineer and Geometer

Desargues remained a practical man throughout his life. He wrote on perspective in 1636, stonecutting and stereotomy in 1640 and sundial construction that same year, and proposed engineering projects including water-lifting systems. This connection between workshop and mathematical study is one of the most striking features of his legacy.

He died in Lyon in October 1661. His name lives on in projection theorems and problems and in the lunar crater Desargues, reminding us that a familiar perspective drawing rests on a deep mathematical idea.
