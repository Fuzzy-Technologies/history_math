*Marking the 135th anniversary of his birth*

Ivan Ivanovich Privalov (1891–1941) was a Soviet mathematician, a corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences from 1939 and a Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences from 1935. An outstanding member of the Moscow school of mathematical analysis, he is associated above all with complex function theory, although his interests also included trigonometric series and functions of a real variable.

## Education and Teaching

He was born in Nizhny Lomov, Penza Province, on 30 January 1891 Old Style, or 11 February in the Gregorian calendar; some sources give 1 February Old Style, or 13 February. After graduating from the Nizhny Novgorod Gymnasium with a gold medal in 1909, he entered Moscow University and graduated in 1913. In summer 1911, while still a student, he attended lectures by David Hilbert, Edmund Landau and Felix Klein in Göttingen. This early encounter with a rigorous European style remained evident throughout his writing. Impressed by his ability, D. F. Yegorov recommended that he remain at the university to pursue research.

Privalov joined the Moscow Mathematical Society in 1915 and became its vice-president in 1930. He became a professor at Saratov University in 1918, returned to Moscow in 1921 and became a professor at Moscow State University in 1922. From 1923 he headed the function theory section of the Research Institute of Mathematics and Mechanics and taught at the N. E. Zhukovsky Air Force Academy.

## Analytic Functions and Their Boundaries

His first major work was *The Cauchy Integral* (1918). Appearing during the civil war and blockade, it remained little known abroad for a long time but became a classic, systematically applying Lebesgue measure and integration to complex analysis. In joint work with N. N. Luzin, Privalov showed that a conformal map from a disk onto a domain with a rectifiable Jordan boundary sends boundary sets of zero length to sets of zero length. They also proved that a holomorphic function on the disk is uniquely determined by its finite nontangential limits on a set of positive length on the boundary circle. Radial limits alone on such a set do not suffice for an arbitrary holomorphic function. These results addressed a deceptively simple question: when do boundary values determine a function inside a domain, and when do identical boundary values fail to guarantee agreement everywhere?

Subharmonic function theory occupies a special place in his legacy. Privalov showed how naturally it arises where complex analysis, potential theory and problems concerning harmonic functions meet. An extensive body of research led to *Subharmonic Functions* (1937), which quickly became an essential reference for function theorists. His last major work, *Boundary Properties of Single-Valued Analytic Functions* (1941), brought together years of research on how analytic functions approach a boundary: where angular limits exist, how mappings behave and how subtle the relationship between a domain’s geometry and analysis can be.

## Lectures, Textbooks and Legacy

He devoted great energy to teaching and was a brilliant lecturer. His lectures were valued for their exceptional clarity and for showing the idea behind each formula, rather than treating calculation as magic. His textbooks—*Introduction to the Theory of Functions of a Complex Variable*, *Fourier Series*, *Integral Equations* and *Analytic Geometry*—went through many editions. *Analytic Geometry* alone appeared dozens of times: its thirtieth edition was published in 1966 and its thirty-first in 1991. P. S. Alexandrov explained why Privalov had few students and scarcely established a school of his own: he “knew too much and therefore demanded a great deal”.

On 29 January 1939 he was elected a corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences. In 1940, for Moscow University’s anniversary, he received the Order of the Red Banner of Labour.

Privalov’s name remains familiar in modern complex analysis courses. His theorems and lemmas provide much of the framework for discussing limiting values and boundary uniqueness.

He died in Moscow on 13 July 1941 and was buried at Novodevichy Cemetery. A street in Nizhny Lomov has borne his name since 1964, and material about him is held in the local history museum.
