> “But providence is not algebra. The human mind, as the common saying goes, is no prophet, but a guesser…”  
> A. S. Pushkin

Words such as mystery, hope, destiny, fate, providence, chance, accident and fortune occur in many forms in Pushkin's writing. His poems often return to chance events in life and the prediction of destiny.

Readers of *Eugene Onegin* can hardly overlook a stanza in Chapter VII that attempts to predict Russia's distant future:

> When we push farther back the bounds  
> Of beneficial enlightenment,  
> In time—by the calculations  
> Of philosophical tables,  
> In some five hundred years—our roads  
> Will surely change beyond all measure:  
> Highways, linking Russia here and there,  
> Will cross the country everywhere.  
> Cast-iron bridges over waters  
> Will stride in broad, sweeping arcs;  
> We shall move mountains, under water  
> Bore daring vaulted passageways,  
> And the Christian world will establish  
> A tavern at every station.

Not everyone knows that in January 1833 Pushkin was admitted to the Imperial Russian Academy. Initially he attended its meetings fairly regularly, but his visits gradually became less frequent and ceased altogether at the beginning of 1834.

At the end of 1835 Pushkin asked A. Kh. Benckendorff, chief of the gendarmes and head of the Third Section, for permission *“in the coming year, 1836, to publish four volumes of purely literary articles—stories, poems, etc.—historical and scholarly articles, and critical reviews of Russian and foreign literature.”* The journal *Sovremennik* (*The Contemporary*) was authorized as a literary collection appearing four times a year. It resembled an almanac, with just two sections, “Poetry” and “Prose.” It contained fiction, criticism, bibliography and articles on literary history and theory, but also articles touching on contemporary politics, economics, Russian history, culture, education and the scientific and technical achievements of the time. Four volumes appeared during Pushkin's lifetime.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

The first volume of *Sovremennik* published “A Review of the Paris Mathematical Annual for 1836” by Prince Pyotr Borisovich Kozlovsky (1783–1840), diplomat, poet and popularizer of the exact sciences. The third volume carried his probability-theory article “On Hope.” This was the first popular account of probability theory in Russian literature; its author called it the “theory of likelihoods,” using the old Russian word *udobosbytnosti*. Pushkin had commissioned Kozlovsky's mathematical articles. He valued the collaboration highly and, on the eve of his duel, asked Kozlovsky not to delay an article on the theory of steam engines intended for the first issue of *Sovremennik* in 1837.

Pushkin's library contained two works on probability theory. One was the famous *A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities* by the great French mathematician and mechanician Laplace (1749–1827), published in Paris in 1825. Pushkin scholars suggest that this interest in probability was connected with his deep concern for the relationship between necessity and chance in historical processes.

Here is what Kozlovsky wrote in the introduction to “On Hope.”

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

> “I venture to ask each reader to add the fruits of his own experience and recollections to what I have said, so as to convince himself of the disastrous and bitter consequences of deceptive hopes. But how can we protect ourselves from them? How can the means of such protection be spread among the people? I know no other way than to spread that philosophical mathematics called the calculus of probabilities (calcul des probabilités), or, better in my view, the science of calculating likelihoods, so that, together with the first concepts of algebra, it becomes clearly and deeply impressed upon even the most ordinary minds. This does not seem to me as difficult as many imagine in their fear of algebraic formulas. Here I shall try to set out the elementary principles of this science without assuming any knowledge of higher mathematics in my reader. My reward will be if, after reading the article, the reader says: any child can understand it.”

> “I see no greater difficulty in understanding that, when I know that three of the four roads before me lead to Moscow, without knowing which ones, then by taking one of them I have the likelihoods in my favour—that is, I shall make no mistake and arrive in Moscow—whereas I may make a mistake, or the probability of my being mistaken is as 1 to 4. From this simple and obvious example we conclude that likelihoods are expressed by a fraction whose numerator contains the cases favourable to me and whose denominator contains all possible cases.”

Today, authors of probability problem books sometimes draw on Pushkin's works for exercises on the classical definition of probability. One example uses the plot of his famous *The Queen of Spades*: *Hermann draws three cards at random from a deck of 52 cards. Find the probability that they are a three, a seven and an ace.*
