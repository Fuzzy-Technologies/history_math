*Marking the 130th anniversary of his birth*

Pavel Sergeyevich Aleksandrov (25 April [7 May] 1896–16 November 1982) was a Soviet mathematician and one of the founders of modern topology. He became a full member of the USSR Academy of Sciences in 1953, having been a corresponding member since 1929, and a member of the RSFSR Academy of Pedagogical Sciences, later the USSR academy, in 1945. A professor at Moscow State University from 1929, he was president of the Moscow Mathematical Society in 1932–1964 and vice-president of the International Mathematical Union in 1959–1962. He received the first-class Stalin Prize for 1942 under a decree dated 22 March 1943 and the title Hero of Socialist Labour in 1969.

## The Moscow Mathematical School

He was born in Bogorodsk, now Noginsk. Entering Moscow University in 1913, he soon joined the Moscow mathematical school through D. F. Egorov’s seminar and later close ties with fellow students and colleagues, including P. S. Urysohn. By the 1920s he was in active contact with leading European mathematicians, helping strengthen international links in Soviet science.

## The Language of Topology

His main fields were general and combinatorial topology. Aleksandrov helped make topology a rigorous language for twentieth-century mathematics, studying continuity, compactness and the structure of spaces. His name is attached to concepts and results familiar from textbooks: the development of compactness, metrization theorems, the compactification of locally compact spaces by adjoining one point, and combinatorial descriptions of spaces through complexes and their invariants.

He was both a researcher and a builder of a mathematical school. At Moscow State University he headed the Department of Higher Geometry and Topology for many years, ran seminars and worked on leading journals, including as editor-in-chief of *Uspekhi Matematicheskikh Nauk*. *Topologie* (1935), written with Heinz Hopf, became a classic course for its time and helped shape the discipline’s language.

## School Education and the Mathematical Community

His work for schools deserves a separate mention. Aleksandrov took part in discussions of school mathematics and coauthored a school algebra textbook with A. N. Kolmogorov. For him, mathematics remained a culture of thought rather than a collection of techniques, a culture he sought to pass on to students and future teachers.

His life brought together research, the university and the mathematical community. For decades he led the Moscow Mathematical Society, sustaining the contacts and traditions of the Moscow school. His legacy lies in results, people and a style of mathematical conversation in which a demand for rigour leaves room for the beauty of an idea.
