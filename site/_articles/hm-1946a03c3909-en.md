One of the first major independent achievements of European Renaissance mathematics was the solution of cubic equations by radicals.

Niccolò Tartaglia, also known as Niccolò Fontana and nicknamed Tartaglia, the stammerer, was an Italian self-taught mathematician, teacher and fortification engineer. He entered the history of mathematics through this problem.

He was born around 1499, or 1500, in Brescia, Lombardy.

He taught himself to read and mastered mathematics, then passed an examination and began teaching it to others. He later became a well-known mathematician of his day and taught mathematics in Verona, Brescia and Venice; in Verona he worked at an abacus school of practical mathematics.

In 1534 he received a challenge to a contest between scholars, as was customary at the time. In 1535 he rediscovered methods of solving cubic equations already known by then. He defeated his opponents at the mathematical tournament, publicly demonstrating his results while keeping the method secret.

In 1539, the learned Milanese physician Gerolamo Cardano pleaded with him for the rule, swearing not to publish it. Tartaglia disclosed it in verse. After reconstructing the rule from its somewhat obscure wording and proving it, Cardano felt entitled to include it in *The Great Art* (*Ars Magna*, 1545), acknowledging Tartaglia’s authorship. Nevertheless, it became known as Cardano’s formula.

Tartaglia was outraged, and a controversy followed. The quarrel produced a series of documents: Tartaglia’s *Quesiti et inventioni diverse* (1546), Ferrari’s six printed *Cartelli* (1547–1548), and Tartaglia’s replies. They made the history of the discovery widely known.

His surviving writings consider mathematics and practical questions in mechanics, ballistics and topography. Alongside artillery, Tartaglia studied the strengthening of towns and fortification.
