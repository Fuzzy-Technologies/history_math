*Marking the 185th anniversary of his birth*

A well-known Russian mathematics educator and public figure in education. He developed the first methodology for teaching algebra (1868). Trained as a naval officer, he became a prominent theorist and practitioner of naval education and a rear admiral. He was a recognized advocate of women’s education.

His name is often mentioned in connection with Sofya Kovalevskaya, whom he tutored in mathematics at home for two years, in 1867–1868.

## A School Without Coercion

He was born in Kamchatka on 29 January 1839, or 10 February in the Gregorian calendar.

He studied at the Naval Cadet Corps and its officers’ classes. In 1856 he was commissioned as a midshipman. In 1858–1861 he also attended university lectures. During that period, together with a circle of students and officers, he founded the Vasilyevsky Island Free School for boys from poor families. He became its elected inspector and was one of St Petersburg’s best-known educators in the 1860s.

He advocated teaching without coercion or rewards: no punishments, prizes, marks or examinations at all. He introduced new teaching practices, including manual work, learning crafts and visits to workplaces, factories and museums.

## Algebra and Naval Education

In 1868 he published *A Course in Algebra Based on the Gradual Generalization of Arithmetic Problems: Teaching Guidance for the Teacher of Elementary Algebra* (the Russian title translated here). This was the first methodology for teaching algebra in Russia. In the first half of the nineteenth century, questions about how to teach algebra had not been raised.

In 1866 he entered the Naval Academy. He continued his teaching career at the Naval Cadet Corps, where he taught mathematical subjects from 1867 to 1894. He taught mathematics to the outstanding scientist, naval architect and academician Alexey Krylov during Krylov’s studies at the Corps in 1878–1884.

## Women’s and Vocational Education

From the late 1860s he worked on questions of women’s education. When plans arose to establish higher courses for women in St Petersburg, Strannolyubsky helped design their organization. He lectured to numerous women’s study circles formed to help women prepare for higher general education and specialist medical education. Deeply devoted to women’s education, he inspired his many students with a love of science.

He also took part in the first congresses of Russian workers in technical and vocational education, held in St Petersburg in 1889–1890 and Moscow in 1895–1896. He presented extensive reports on women’s vocational schools.

In the last years of his life, he carried out scholarly work on technical education at the Imperial Russian Technical Society.

His principal naval publications include the following (Russian titles translated here):

- “The Present State of Certain Questions Relating to the Rigging and Sailing Characteristics of Ironclad Ships”, *Morskoy Sbornik* (*Naval Collection*), 1867, nos. 10 and 11.
- *The Organization of Higher Naval Education in Russia*. St Petersburg, 1878.
- “A Survey of Systems of Naval Education in Foreign Countries”, *Morskoy Sbornik*, 1882 and 1883.

The breadth of Alexander Strannolyubsky’s personality enabled him to bring these diverse interests together into a single educational system that offered pupils wide-ranging opportunities.
