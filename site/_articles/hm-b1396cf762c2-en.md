*Marking the 110th anniversary of his birth*

An American mathematician and one of the founders of linear programming, alongside the Soviet mathematician Leonid Kantorovich and the American mathematician John von Neumann. He was elected to the US National Academy of Sciences in 1971 and to the US National Academy of Engineering in 1985; he was also a member of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences.

## Education and University Work

He was born on 8 November 1914 in Portland, Oregon, USA.

George Dantzig received a bachelor’s degree in mathematics and physics from the University of Maryland in 1936 and a master’s degree in mathematics from the University of Michigan in 1938. He received his doctorate in mathematics from the University of California, Berkeley, in 1946.

He became a professor in the Department of Industrial Engineering at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1960, where he founded a research centre that he subsequently directed. In 1966 he moved to Stanford University as a professor of operations research and computer science. In 1974 he co-founded the Systems Optimization Laboratory with Richard Cottle.

## Linear Programming and Recognition

He planned a multivolume work on linear programming; two volumes co-authored with Mukund Thapa were published in 1997 and 2003. Dantzig introduced the term “linear programming” in 1949. He is known as the developer of the algorithm used to solve problems by the simplex method.

Dantzig was the first recipient of the John von Neumann Theory Prize in 1975. He received the US National Medal of Science in 1975 and an honorary doctorate from the University of Maryland, College Park, in 1976. He was awarded the Harvey Prize in Israel in 1985.

In 1979 the Mathematical Programming Society (MPS) and the Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics (SIAM) established the Dantzig Prize, awarded every three years since 1982 for original research making a major contribution to mathematical programming.

A celebrated story from Dantzig’s life is often told in accounts of mathematical history.

In 1938 he entered the doctoral programme in mathematics at the University of California, Berkeley, where he studied statistics under Jerzy Neyman. One day in 1939 he arrived late for class and mistakenly assumed that the equations on the board were homework. The assignment was difficult, but George nevertheless managed to complete it. They turned out to be two unsolved problems in statistics that established scholars had been unable to solve for many years.

Sometimes you can do the impossible — provided you have not already convinced yourself that the impossible is impossible.
