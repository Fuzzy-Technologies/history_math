Kirik of Novgorod holds a special place in the history of science in Rus’. He is the first Russian mathematician known to us by name, a twelfth-century theologian and philosopher, and the author of a remarkable treatise on numbers.

The precise dates of his life are unknown (c. 1110–1158), but a problem he set in 1136 gives us his year of birth: “How many months, weeks, days and hours has a person lived if he turned twenty-six in 1136?” For more, see [“The Problem of Kirik of Novgorod”](https://teletype.in/@history_math/The-Kirik-of-Novgorod-Story).

Kirik spent most of his life at St Anthony’s Monastery in Novgorod. He was a deacon and a domestikos, or choirmaster, and some researchers believe that he also kept the archbishop’s chronicle until 1156.

His knowledge was extraordinarily broad:

- mathematics, including fractions and large numbers;
- astronomy and calendar cycles;
- philosophy, theology and canon law;
- music theory.

## Reckoning Time and Fractions of the Hour

Kirik left behind remarkable scholarly works. His principal treatise, *Instruction by Which a Person May Know the Number of All Years* (1136), is a masterpiece of medieval Rus’ scholarship. It includes:

- calculations from the Creation to his own time in years, months, days and hours;
- the indiction, solar cycle and lunar cycle;
- an original system of subdividing the hour into seventh-order fractions;
- calendar tables, an autobiographical passage and references to contemporaries, including Emperor John II Komnenos and Archbishop Nifont.

His subdivision of the hour is especially unusual: first into fifths, then through seven successive levels, ultimately yielding 78,125 parts. This system has no parallel in Byzantium or Europe.

## Questions about Life and a Lasting Legacy

His second major work, *Questions of Kirik*, is a dialogue with Archbishop Nifont, taking the form of questions and answers about life, morality, marriage, birch-bark documents and everyday dilemmas in the twelfth century. It is a valuable source on the daily life and customs of medieval Rus’. Its full title is “These Are the Questions of Kirik, Which He Asked Nifont, Bishop of Novgorod, and Others”; it was composed in the 1140s–1150s.

His works form an important part of our cultural heritage, convincingly challenging the myth of medieval Rus’ mathematical backwardness. They show that scholarly thought in Rus’ has deep historical roots. Kirik was more than a priest: he was a philosopher who sought to understand and measure time, numbers and the order of the world.

## Notes

Simonov, R. A. *Kirik of Novgorod: A Twelfth-Century Scholar*. Russian edition. Moscow: Nauka, 1980. 112 pp.

*Instruction by Which a Person May Know the Number of All Years* (1136) comprises twenty-seven numbered sections in surviving sixteenth- to nineteenth-century copies. These can be grouped into several parts:

### 1. Basic Units of Time, Sections 1–5

- Time from the Creation to 1136, year 6644 in the Byzantine era, calculated in years, months, weeks, days and hours.

### 2. Theoretical Foundations of the Calendar, Sections 6–15

- The indiction, a fifteen-year cycle.
- The solar cycle, twenty-eight years.
- The lunar cycle, nineteen years.
- The renewals, or cyclical regeneration of the elements:
  - the heavens every eighty years;
  - the earth every forty years;
  - the sea every sixty years;
  - water every seventy years.
- Leap years.
- The Great Cycle of 532 years, after which Easter dates repeat.

### 3. Fractional Divisions of the Hour, Sections 21–27

- The hour is divided into five first-order fractional hours.
- Each first-order fractional hour is divided into five second-order fractions.
- This continues to seventh-order fractional hours.
- Thus one hour contains 78,125 seventh-order fractional hours.
- Kirik writes: “Beyond this there are no more; that is, none arise from the seventh-order fractions.”

### 4. Autobiographical Part

- The date of composition.
- Easter calculations for 1136.
- Information about the author.
- References to contemporary rulers: Byzantine Emperor John II Komnenos, Prince Svyatoslav Olgovich of Novgorod and Archbishop Nifont.

His successive division of the hour into fifths is particularly impressive: an original Russian system with no direct parallel in either the Byzantine or Western European tradition.

Points of interest in *Questions of Kirik* (1140–1150):

- References to birch-bark documents.

- Questions about mixed marriages between Orthodox Christians and Latin Christians.

- Discussion of conduct in various everyday situations.

- Dialogues about tensions between church prescriptions and real life.

- Discussion of when it is best to take the Great Schema, the highest degree of monastic vows.

Kirik’s legacy is studied from several perspectives:

1. History of mathematics: R. A. Simonov and T. S. Polyakova analyse his computational methods.

2. Cultural history: V. V. Milkov and others examine Kirik’s place in the intellectual tradition of medieval Rus’.

3. History of the book: the manuscript transmission of his works.

4. Philosophy: the worldview expressed in his writings.

Further points of interest:

- Kirik did not use Arabic numerals, which had not yet come into use in Rus’.

- His calendar cycles were based on the Byzantine era.

- A conference marking Kirik’s nine-hundredth anniversary was held in 2010. Its proceedings were published as *Kirik of Novgorod and the Culture of Medieval Rus’*.
