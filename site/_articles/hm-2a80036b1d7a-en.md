A Russian mathematician, mechanics researcher, writer and essayist, and a corresponding member of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences (1889). She was the world’s first woman professor of mathematics. Her most important research concerned the rotation of a rigid body.

Sofya Vasilyevna Kovalevskaya, née Korvin-Krukovskaya, was born in Moscow on 3 January 1850, or 15 January in the Gregorian calendar. She showed exceptional mathematical ability early in life and took lessons in higher mathematics from the well-known teacher Alexander Strannolyubsky. At the time, women were not admitted to Russian universities. To pursue science, she entered a marriage of convenience with Vladimir Kovalevsky, later an outstanding Russian palaeontologist, and went to Germany. At Heidelberg University she attended lectures unofficially, with her lecturers’ permission; in Berlin she studied privately with Karl Weierstrass.

On the basis of her work on partial differential equations and Abelian integrals, the University of Göttingen awarded her the degree of Doctor of Philosophy in 1874.

She returned to Russia in 1874 but had no opportunity to apply her knowledge. In 1883 she was invited to teach at Stockholm University College, now Stockholm University, where she spent eight years. In 1888 she wrote *On the Rotation of a Rigid Body about a Fixed Point*, which won a prize from the Paris Academy of Sciences and later another from the Swedish Academy of Sciences.

Kovalevskaya gained worldwide recognition as a scientist, but because of the unfair treatment of women in tsarist Russia, she did most of her research abroad. Nevertheless, progressive Russian intellectuals secured recognition of her scientific achievements. Her election as a corresponding member of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences, on Chebyshev’s nomination, was an exceptional event in Russia.

She also wrote literature. Her works include the novella *A Nihilist Girl* (1884), the family chronicle *Recollections of Childhood* (1889), the novel *The Vorontsov Family* and *The Struggle for Happiness: Two Parallel Dramas* (1892). In her literary works she even tried to give a mathematical justification for people’s actions.

Kovalevskaya sympathized with the revolutionary struggle and the ideas of utopian socialism. In 1871 she went to besieged Paris and cared for wounded Communards.

She died at forty-one and is buried in Stockholm.

Since 1992, the Division of Mathematical Sciences of the Russian Academy of Sciences has awarded the Sofya Kovalevskaya Prize for outstanding results in mathematics.
