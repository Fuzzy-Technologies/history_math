*Marking the 120th anniversary of his birth*

Kurt Friedrich Gödel (1906–1978) was an Austrian logician, mathematician and philosopher of mathematics, and one of the twentieth century’s major thinkers. His work changed discussions of mathematical foundations and of what it means to prove something and justify a theory.

He was born on 28 April 1906 in Brünn, now Brno in Czechia, then part of Austria-Hungary. Childhood rheumatic fever left him unusually attentive to his health throughout his life. At the University of Vienna he initially hesitated between mathematics and theoretical physics, but Philipp Furtwängler’s lectures finally drew him to mathematics. He also attended Moritz Schlick’s seminar, where Russell and the philosophy of mathematics were discussed.

## Completeness and Incompleteness

In 1929 he completed his dissertation under Hans Hahn and proved the completeness theorem for predicate calculus: in this logic, truth and provability coincide. In 1931 he published the incompleteness theorems, often ranked among the century’s most important mathematical results.

Put very briefly, any sufficiently rich, consistent formal theory of arithmetic inevitably contains true statements that cannot be derived from its axioms. To show this, Gödel devised a way of encoding formulas as numbers, now called Gödel numbering, and constructed a statement that says within the system, “I am not provable here.” A consistent system cannot prove that statement and is therefore incomplete. The second theorem adds a further limit: such a system cannot prove its own consistency using its own resources.

These theorems are often extended to almost everything imaginable, but Gödel was concerned specifically with formalized mathematics. That is where their power lies.

His arithmetization of syntax — treating formulas and proofs as objects that can be manipulated like numbers — became an important bridge between logic and computability theory, alongside the work of Church and Turing.

## Princeton, Sets and Cosmology

From the early 1930s he taught in Vienna while making several visits to the Institute for Advanced Study, IAS, in Princeton. In 1938 he married Adele Porkert. They emigrated to the United States in 1940, and he spent the rest of his career at the IAS.

In set theory, Gödel constructed the constructible universe $L$. His result shows that if the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms (ZF) are consistent, the axiom of choice and the generalized continuum hypothesis can be added without contradiction. In 1963–1964, Paul Cohen showed that the axiom of choice does not follow from ZF and that the continuum hypothesis does not follow even from ZF with the axiom of choice, assuming the respective systems are consistent. This helped establish a way of thinking in terms of independence: sometimes asking whether a statement is true becomes a question of which axioms we are prepared to accept.

His interests extended beyond logic. In 1949 he found an unusual solution of Einstein’s equations in general relativity: a rotating-universe model containing closed timelike curves. He was a friend of Albert Einstein, and their walks in Princeton became part of local academic lore. His honours included the Albert Einstein Award in 1951 and the US National Medal of Science in 1974. He published relatively few papers, but almost every one made an impact.

His later years were troubled by severe anxiety and fear of poisoning. He died in Princeton on 14 January 1978. His ideas live on in mathematics, philosophy and the culture of proof itself. Whenever we ask about the limits of formalization and what can in principle follow from axioms, we continue, knowingly or otherwise, the discussion he made so precise.

At the beginning of the twentieth century, David Hilbert proclaimed the aim of axiomatizing all mathematics. Completing that programme required proving the consistency and logical completeness of natural-number arithmetic. On 7 September 1930, at a conference on mathematical foundations in Königsberg, the twenty-four-year-old Gödel first announced two fundamental incompleteness theorems. These showed that Hilbert’s programme could not be realized: whatever arithmetic axioms are chosen, there are propositions that can neither be proved nor refuted by Hilbert’s elementary, finitary means, and a finitary proof of arithmetic’s consistency is impossible.

The unscheduled announcement had an overwhelming effect. Gödel immediately became world famous, and Hilbert’s programme for formalizing mathematical foundations required urgent reconsideration.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

In 1940, fearing military conscription, Gödel left for the United States with his wife and his brother Rudolf. The war made an Atlantic crossing dangerous, so they travelled via the Soviet Union, then on friendly terms with Germany, using the Trans-Siberian Railway and continuing through Japan. In the United States he readily obtained a position at the recently founded Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton and became a professor there in 1953. In 1958 Gödel published [a paper on extending the finitary standpoint](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1746-8361.1958.tb01464.x).

In 1948 he became an American citizen. During the interview he tried to demonstrate that the US Constitution was formally and logically incomplete and did not prevent the establishment of a dictatorship, but was politely interrupted. The supposed flaw he identified became known as Gödel’s loophole.
