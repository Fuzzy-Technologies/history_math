*Marking the 135th anniversary of his birth*

Ivan Matveyevich Vinogradov (2 [14] September 1891–20 March 1983) was a Soviet mathematician, an academician of the USSR Academy of Sciences and the founder of a powerful school of analytic number theory. For almost half a century he headed the Steklov Mathematical Institute, combining the organization of research with personal mathematical work that became a classic.

## Universities and the Institute

He was born in Milolyub, a village in the Velikiye Luki district of Pskov province, to a rural priest. In 1910 he entered the Faculty of Physics and Mathematics at St Petersburg University. After graduating in 1914, he remained there to prepare for an academic career.

He spent the Civil War and the first post-revolutionary reforms working in universities around the country: in 1918–1920 he worked at Perm and Tomsk. Returning to Petrograd in 1920, he taught at the Polytechnic Institute and the university; by the mid-1920s he was a professor and a leading figure in number theory. His election as an academician in 1929 marked the growing prominence of his work as an organizer of science.

In the early 1930s Vinogradov took part in restructuring the Academy’s institutes. In 1934 he became the first director of the newly established Steklov Mathematical Institute. When the Academy moved to Moscow, the institute acquired a permanent base on Vavilov Street. Vinogradov led it in 1934–1941 and, after an interruption, again from 1944 until his death. Administration did not eclipse his research: contemporaries noted the dozens of papers he published in the 1930s and the influence of his seminars on the whole field.

## Trigonometric Sums and Prime Numbers

The principal mathematical instrument associated with his name is the method of trigonometric sums. Put simply, it is the art of estimating complicated oscillating sums that arise in problems about primes and representations of numbers. It also developed particular techniques, including bilinear forms and mean-value estimates, that became standard tools of analytic number theory.

His most famous result concerned the weak Goldbach problem. In 1937 Vinogradov proved that every sufficiently large odd integer can be expressed as a sum of three primes. This was a decisive advance, showing that careful analytic estimates could move problems that appear almost purely arithmetical.

## Books, Honours and Legacy

His books trained generations of mathematicians. They include *The Method of Trigonometrical Sums in the Theory of Numbers* and *Elements of Number Theory*, a rigorous textbook with a clear line of reasoning and a lively tradition of problem solving. He also supervised editorial work for the Academy’s publications for many years, took an interest in school education and headed the commission on school mathematics reform until his death.

His achievements brought the highest honours: the Stalin Prize in 1941, Lenin Prize in 1972, USSR State Prize in 1983, two Hero of Socialist Labour awards in 1945 and 1971, and the USSR Academy of Sciences’ Lomonosov Gold Medal. Internationally, he was recognized as a leading number theorist, becoming a foreign member of the Royal Society in 1942 and an honorary member of the London Mathematical Society.

Vinogradov’s name lives on in theorems and methods, but also in institutional memory: the Steklov Institute’s traditions, his school of pupils and the language of modern analytic number theory. Here, mathematical results and the research community built around them carry equal weight.
