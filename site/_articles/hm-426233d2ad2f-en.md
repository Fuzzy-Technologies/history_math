They say that a new professor, appointed by the ministry, once arrived at the University of Königsberg. The students were less than enthusiastic: some earlier discreditable act had already damaged his reputation.

Apparently the whole university attended his first lecture. Naturally, the new professor took this as a success and a sign of popularity to come. Yet no sooner had he opened his mouth than the audience rose noisily and almost everyone walked out.

The university council then discussed whom to punish for disrupting the lecture. Formally, the offence was plain enough, but the culprits were an entire crowd.

[Carl Gustav Jacobi](https://t.me/history_math/232) (1804–1851), a professor at Königsberg and one of the leading mathematicians of his time, took the floor.

He recalled the old university rule:

*Tres faciunt collegium*: “three make a collegium”.

Three listeners were enough for a lecture. Everyone who left before the last three had therefore left lawfully. Only the final three had disrupted the lecture. And since nobody knew who those three were, strictly speaking there was nobody to punish.

Mathematics has little to do with this, of course. But the logic is university logic at its finest.
