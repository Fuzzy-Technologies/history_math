Brahmagupta (c. 598–c. 668), an Indian mathematician and astronomer, was among the first to give rigorous rules for working with zero in his *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* (628). In his rules, zero is the result of subtracting a number from itself; adding or subtracting zero leaves a number unchanged, while multiplying by it turns everything into zero.

“The product of zero and a negative, or of zero and a positive, is zero; and that of two zeros is zero.”<br>
“Zero divided by zero is zero. A positive or negative number divided by zero is a fraction with that denominator.”<br>
(Modern wording based on H. T. Colebrooke’s 1817 translation, chapter XVIII, section II, pp. 339–340, rules 34–36, §§ 22–24.)

Here we encounter a small “error of the age”: Brahmagupta held that $0/0 = 0$ and left the fraction $a/0$ with zero in its denominator. Today, division by zero is regarded as undefined. Nevertheless, his rules for zero and negative numbers helped lay the foundations of school arithmetic.

## Notes

The rules for working with zero appear on pp. 339–340 of H. T. Colebrooke’s 1817 translation.
