*Marking the 210th anniversary of his birth*

August Wilhelm Grube (16 December 1816–28 January 1884) was a German educator and a kind of “school engineer” whose name became a byword in the teaching of elementary arithmetic. His approach became known as the monographic method: study numbers one by one, rather than operation by operation, exploring each number through all its relationships and operations.

## From Schoolteacher to Writer

Grube was born into a craftsman’s family in Wernigerode, Saxony, Germany. He attended the local lyceum and then the teacher-training seminary in Weissenfels. He began as an assistant teacher in Merseburg, but soon left school service for private tutoring in several families. From the late 1840s he lived near Lake Constance, first in Hard and then in Bregenz. In 1866 he retired and devoted himself to writing on education and teaching methods.

## The Monographic Method

His key book was *Leitfaden für das Rechnen in der Elementarschule…* (*A Guide to Arithmetic in the Elementary School*, 1842). Grube proposed an idea that then sounded bold: within the first hundred numbers, do not divide teaching into the four operations. Instead, treat each number as an object in its own right, exploring its composition, comparisons, multiples and differences so that addition, subtraction, multiplication and division grow from an understanding of the number. He distinguished his *Denkrechnen*, calculation through thinking, from customary *Regelrechnen*, calculation by rules.

This gave Grube’s method the practical pattern later described in many countries: work intensively within a small range, often 1–20, building each number, decomposing it, measuring it with earlier numbers and expressing its relationships in words before moving on. Its strength lay in rich mathematical meaning and attention to pupils’ speech; its weaknesses were the risks of overload and monotony. These drew considerable criticism in the late nineteenth century, and the method was often softened.

## Criticism and International Influence

Leo Tolstoy strongly opposed Grube’s method in *Otechestvennye Zapiski* in 1874. Sergei Rachinsky was another opponent.

Grube’s influence extended far beyond Germany. English-speaking educators discussed his method as a distinct school of arithmetic teaching, and published manuals analysing lessons. In Russia, his ideas were known and adapted by specialists in teaching methods, including Vasily Yevtushevsky. A nineteenth-century educational technique thus became an international language of elementary mathematics.
