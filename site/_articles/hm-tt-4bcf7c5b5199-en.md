A rangefinder determines the distance to an object remotely, without direct measurement. Rangefinders are widely used in topographic surveying, military work, astronomical research and photography.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

By operating principle, rangefinders are divided into geometric, or passive, and physical, or active, instruments.

A geometric rangefinder determines the altitude h of the isosceles ∆ABC from the known side AB = l, the rangefinder’s baseline, and the opposite acute angle 𝛽, the parallactic angle. One quantity, 𝑙 or 𝛽, is usually constant and the other variable and measured. On this basis, rangefinders are classified as having a constant angle or a constant baseline.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Physical rangefinders — using light, radio or sound — measure the time taken by a signal sent from the instrument to travel to the object and back. Echo sounders and sonar, for example, operate this way.

A stereoscopic rangefinder determines a target’s distance, flight altitude and angular coordinates — azimuth and elevation — for medium- and large-calibre anti-aircraft artillery.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

The world’s first instrument for accurately determining the distance to a target was invented in the late 1860s by the Russian officer and military engineer V. F. Petrushevsky. It was initially used in coastal artillery. In 1868, Petrushevsky’s rangefinder passed successful trials at batteries in Kronstadt.
