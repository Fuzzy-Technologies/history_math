## From the Author {#author-note}

How can theoretical mathematics be applied in practice? Sometimes the wealth of formulas in textbooks seems impossible to connect to real problems arising here and now. Yet we can start with basic definitions, try to relate them to the facts we have, and gradually introduce more sophisticated mathematics. Let me show you an example.

Some time ago, my son Timur Gilmullin and I carried out practical experiments to investigate long numerical sequences for outliers, or anomalies. Implementing the algorithms we devised drew, in particular, on algebra, mathematical analysis and statistics. We first tried to apply familiar basic theoretical concepts and formulas to our experimental results. Only afterwards did we adapt them and introduce our own concept of an anomaly in a numerical sequence and an anomaly-filter function suited to a particular range of our tasks.
## Abstract {#abstract}

This article discusses and formally introduces the concepts of an anomaly in a numerical sequence and an anomaly-filter function. The work is motivated by the lack of a unified approach to the concept of an anomaly, despite its central role in solving many practical problems.

We use the breakdown point to describe an estimator’s resistance to outliers and apply the filter in sliding windows. The method of filtering a numerical sequence for outliers combines the median and the median absolute deviation. We propose a modification of the Hampel method for identifying outliers in a sample, suitable for a wide range of IT automation tasks.

We have developed Python functions that filter a numerical sequence for anomalies and determine the index of its first anomalous element. As an example, a Jupyter Notebook demonstrates how the modified Hampel method can quickly identify anomalies in stock-market prices.

To obtain a sample containing outliers, we use our own library for generating test stock-market data. The experimental results confirm that the proposed algorithms can clearly identify anomalies under different parameter settings.

We discuss the method’s advantages and limitations. The Hampel filter is readily optimised and parallelised. The material offers a practical way to automate the identification of anomalies in numerical sequences.

## Introduction {#introduction}

In many practical problems we need to find anomalies in numerical sequences. An anomaly is a value that departs in some way from the rest of the sequence. Such problems arise in many areas:

- cleaning noisy data in [data science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_science);

- filtering outliers from neural-network training samples in [machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning);

- detecting anomalous network activity by hackers while monitoring traffic and events in [cybersecurity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyber_security);

- identifying outliers or tails in market-data streams in [algorithmic trading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_trading);

- and any other anomaly-detection problem in which the data can be represented as a numerical sequence.

A numerical series means different things in mathematical analysis and statistics. Here we use the statistical meaning: a finite sequence of numbers, comparable to a sample.

There are various interpretations of anomalies in numerical sequences, most of which concern time series [1, 2, 3, 4]. A time series consists of statistical observations, collected at different times, of parameters of the process under study. Anomalies are data patterns that do not conform to an expected, clearly defined notion of normal behaviour. What counts as normal or abnormal can change over time.

Anomalies can be detected using various prediction-based approaches, including statistical methods and neural networks [1].

Individual studies define different measures of how anomalous an observation is. Some address the problem by constructing algorithm ensembles: combining the results of several methods whose different errors can compensate for one another [2].

Anomaly detection is often treated as an unsupervised-learning problem. Time-series analysis is commonly understood as the application of statistical and machine-learning methods to identify patterns in a series and predict the future behaviour of the system it describes [3].

Anomalies can, for example, serve as diagnostic indicators [4]. Different fields use different names for them: anomalies, outliers, discordant observations, exceptions, aberrations, surprises, peculiarities or contaminants [5]. The lack of a unified approach to the concept is therefore the motivation for this work.

These approaches were partly brought together in [6, 7]. We developed our own definition of an anomaly for practical use across a wide range of tasks. We will also define and implement an anomaly filter that is as independent as possible of the particular problem and offers flexible settings [8].

The following sections use market data to show how a modified Hampel filter detects anomalies in numerical sequences [9, 10].

## Theory {#theory}

Outlier assessment for one-dimensional data has been extensively studied in the statistical literature. The [sample mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean_and_covariance) estimates location, and the sample [variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variance) estimates spread. Both are sensitive to outliers. Even a single observation that differs greatly from the rest can make the sample mean differ substantially from the mean calculated without that observation.

To measure a statistical estimator’s resistance to outliers, Hampel introduced the [breakdown point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robust_statistics#Breakdown_point): the smallest proportion of observations whose replacement by arbitrarily large aberrant values can drive the estimator arbitrarily far from its original value [6].

Intuitively, the breakdown point cannot exceed 50%: if more than half the observations are contaminated, the main distribution cannot be distinguished from the contaminating distribution. The maximum outlier proportion at the breakdown point is therefore 0.5. Some statistics attain this maximum. For example, the [median](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median) has a breakdown point of 0.5, whereas the sample mean has a breakdown point of 1/n, where n is the sample size.

A higher breakdown point is generally taken to indicate a more reliable statistical estimator. A statistic with a high breakdown point is sometimes called a robust statistic.

The median and the [median absolute deviation (MAD)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median_absolute_deviation) give robust estimates of a sample’s location and spread. These two statistics are the basis of the Hampel filter.

MAD is calculated as follows:

$$
\operatorname{MAD}(X)=\operatorname{Median}\bigl(\lvert x_1-\operatorname{Median}(X)\rvert,\ldots,\lvert x_n-\operatorname{Median}(X)\rvert\bigr)\tag{1}
$$

where X is a sample of n observations x₁, …, xₙ.

In Hampel’s approach, an [outlier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outlier) is a number x in a sample X whose absolute difference from the sample median exceeds the sample’s MAD, calculated using equation (1), multiplied by a distribution-dependent coefficient k, the [scale factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_parameter#Estimation). For a normal distribution, this coefficient is approximately 1.4826 [7].

In practice, the definition is modified to construct a Hampel outlier filter using [sliding windows](https://www.geeksforgeeks.org/window-sliding-technique/) and a parameter s ([sigma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_deviation)), a threshold number of standard deviations. The median and MAD are calculated for every sliding window of sample elements. The absolute differences between sample elements and the median are then compared with MAD multiplied by both k and s [8].

A higher standard-deviation threshold s makes the filter more permissive; a lower threshold identifies more numbers as outliers.

We can now define the central concept: what we mean by an anomaly in a finite numerical sequence for practical purposes.

Definition 1. An anomaly in a numerical sequence is a number a from a series X whose absolute difference from the sliding-window median exceeds s times the window’s MAD multiplied by the coefficient k.

To make the anomaly filter as general as possible and independent of the particular practical task, we propose the following set-theoretic interpretation of this definition.

By Definition 1, all anomalies in the series form a subset A:

$$
A=\bigl\{i\mid\lvert x_i-\operatorname{Median}(W_i)\rvert>s\cdot k\cdot\operatorname{MAD}(W_i)\bigr\}\tag{2}
$$

where the set X is the original numerical sequence and Wᵢ is the series of numbers in the i-th sliding window. There are n − w + 1 windows in total, where w is the window size.

Definition 2. An anomaly filter for a numerical sequence is the function:

$$
\begin{aligned}
F&:X\to\{\mathrm{True},\mathrm{False}\},\\
F(x_i)&=\begin{cases}\mathrm{True},&i\in A,\\\mathrm{False},&i\notin A\end{cases}\tag{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

where X is the original numerical sequence of n elements xᵢ, and A is the set of indices of anomalous elements defined by equation (2).

To filter a numerical sequence for anomalies, we propose our Python implementation of [HampelFilter()](https://github.com/Tim55667757/TKSBrokerAPI/blob/develop/tksbrokerapi/TradeRoutines.py#L586-L665). It can detect anomalies among the values of any numerical sequence using the modified Hampel filtering method. The filter detects all anomalies defined by expression (2).

The filter function has the following adjustable parameters:

- window (variable w): the sliding-window size, with a default of 5;

- sigma (variable s): the threshold number of standard deviations, with a default of 3;

- scaleFactor (variable k): the coefficient determined by the series distribution, with a default of 1.4826.

HampelFilter() returns a new series F consisting of True and False values, as in expression (3). True indicates an anomalous element at the corresponding position in the original series.

In practice, it is often sufficient to find only the first anomalous element, or the first of the largest elements, in a numerical sequence. For this purpose, we propose our Python implementation of [HampelAnomalyDetection()](https://github.com/Tim55667757/TKSBrokerAPI/blob/develop/tksbrokerapi/TradeRoutines.py#L668-L730). It returns the smallest index among the detected anomalies, or the index of the first maximum element in the input series if that index precedes the anomaly’s index. If the series has no anomalies, or if all elements are equal and there are no maxima, it returns None.

## Examples {#examples}

Concrete examples of numerical sequences make it easier to understand how HampelFilter() works and what it treats as an anomaly. We present them as a Python script.

First, consider filtering numerical sequences with the default parameter values.

HampelFilter(window=5, sigma=3, scaleFactor=1.4826):

```python
import pandas as pd
from tksbrokerapi.TradeRoutines import HampelFilter

testData1 = [
    # All elements are equal; there is not a single
    # suspicious element, so
    # there should be no anomalies:
    pd.Series([10, 10, 10, 10, 10]),

    # The first element clearly differs
    # from the rest of the series and is anomalous:
    pd.Series([1, 10, 10, 10, 10]),

    # Most elements in this series equal 10,
    # while the first two differ from the overall pattern,
    # so both are anomalous:
    pd.Series([1, 5, 10, 10, 10]),

    # As in the second example,
    # the second element is anomalous because
    # it differs from most elements in the series:
    pd.Series([1, 5, 1, 1, 1]),
]

for i, test in enumerate(testData1):
    print("Input {}: {}".format(i + 1, list(test)))
    print("--> {}".format(list(HampelFilter(test))))
```

To check our expectations about which numbers in the series are anomalous, we run the code above and obtain the expected result:

```text
>>> Input 1: [10, 10, 10, 10, 10]
>>> --> [False, False, False, False, False]
>>> Input 2: [1, 10, 10, 10, 10]
>>> --> [True, False, False, False, False]
>>> Input 3: [1, 5, 10, 10, 10]
>>> --> [True, True, False, False, False]
>>> Input 4: [1, 5, 1, 1, 1]
>>> --> [False, True, False, False, False]
```

The default sliding-window size is 5, which equals the length of each series in this example. Let us see what changes if we set the window size to 3, first using the same series and then adding some new ones.

HampelFilter(window=3, sigma=3, scaleFactor=1.4826):

```python
testData2 = [
    # Nothing should change,
    # because there are no anomalies:
    pd.Series([10, 10, 10, 10, 10]),

    # No change, because there is
    # one anomalous element:
    pd.Series([1, 10, 10, 10, 10]),

    # Here, the first elements should no longer
    # be treated as anomalies when sigma = 3:
    pd.Series([1, 5, 10, 10, 10]),

    # No change, because there is one
    # clearly anomalous element:
    pd.Series([1, 5, 1, 1, 1]),

    # New number series:
    pd.Series([1, 10, 10, 1, 10, 1]),
    pd.Series([1, 10, 10, 10, 10, 1]),
    pd.Series([1, 1, 1, 10, 10, 10]),
]

for i, test in enumerate(testData2):
    print("Input {}: {}".format(i + 1, list(test)))
    print("--> {}".format(list(HampelFilter(test, window=3))))
```

Running the code above gives the following result:

```text
>>> Input 1: [10, 10, 10, 10, 10]
>>> --> [False, False, False, False, False]
>>> Input 2: [1, 10, 10, 10, 10]
>>> --> [True, False, False, False, False]
>>> Input 3: [1, 5, 10, 10, 10]
>>> --> [False, False, False, False, False]
>>> Input 4: [1, 5, 1, 1, 1]
>>> --> [False, True, False, False, False]
>>> Input 5: [1, 10, 10, 1, 10, 1]
>>> --> [True, False, False, False, False, False]
>>> Input 6: [1, 10, 10, 10, 10, 1]
>>> --> [True, False, False, False, False, True]
>>> Input 7: [1, 1, 1, 10, 10, 10]
>>> --> [False, False, False, False, False, False]
```

The first, second and fourth series show no change from the previous example. Notice the third series: its first two elements, previously considered anomalous, are now ignored. This follows directly from equations (1) and (2).

Similarly, the fifth series has only one anomalous element, although at first glance we might expect more. Also, with too small a sliding window, the anomalies in the seventh series could not be identified.

To understand HampelAnomalyDetection(), consider several examples. An important difference from HampelFilter() is that this function also takes account of the presence and positions of maximum elements in the series.

HampelAnomalyDetection(), with the default parameters:

```python
import pandas as pd
from tksbrokerapi.TradeRoutines import HampelAnomalyDetection

testData3 = [
    pd.Series([1, 1, 1, 1, 111, 1]),
    pd.Series([1, 1, 10, 1, 1, 1]),
    pd.Series([111, 1, 1, 1, 1, 111]),
    pd.Series([1, 11, 1, 111, 1, 1]),
    pd.Series([1, 2]),
    pd.Series([1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1]),
]

for i, test in enumerate(testData3):
    print("Input {}: {}".format(i + 1, list(test)))
    print("--> {}".format(HampelAnomalyDetection(test)))
```

Running the code above gives the following result:

```text
>>> Input 1: [1, 1, 1, 1, 111, 1] --> 4
>>> Input 2: [1, 1, 10, 1, 1, 1] --> 2
>>> Input 3: [111, 1, 1, 1, 1, 111] --> 0
>>> Input 4: [1, 11, 1, 111, 1, 1] --> 1
>>> Input 5: [1, 2] --> None
>>> Input 6: [1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1] --> None
```

## Practical Application {#application}

To interpret our findings, consider Hampel filtering in the practical task of identifying anomalies in a numerical sequence of market prices. To obtain a series with outliers, we use our own [PriceGenerator library for generating test market data](https://github.com/Tim55667757/PriceGenerator/blob/master/README_RU.md).

Traders and analysts commonly work with OHLCV time series. Each row gives the opening date, opening, high, low and closing prices, and trading volume for the interval. The four prices can be plotted as a Japanese candlestick.

PriceGenerator can be used as a Python module or run from the command line to generate random price data that closely resemble real prices while having predefined statistical characteristics. It lets us set the overall price trend, candle timeframe, maximum and minimum of the price range, maximum candle size, probability of the next candle’s direction, probability of price outliers, number of generated candles and many other parameters.

For this experiment we developed the Jupyter Notebook [HampelFilteringExample](https://nbviewer.org/github/Tim55667757/TKSBrokerAPI/blob/develop/docs/examples/HampelFilteringExample.ipynb). It demonstrates the use of HampelFilter(), the modified Hampel filter, to identify anomalies in market prices quickly.

The notebook generates a price series with the following characteristics:

- integer prices on the chart, for simplicity;

- candle interval and generation horizon: 1 day and 75 candles;

- minimum and maximum close prices: 40 and 140;

- starting close price: 50;

- maximum candle-tail outlier: 35;

- maximum candle-body size: 25;

- probability of the next candle moving upwards: 51.5%;

- probability of the next candle containing an outlier: 10%;

- overall trend: falling first, for 40 candles, then rising for 35 candles.

To obtain these characteristics, run PriceGenerator with the following parameters:

```sh
PriceGenerator --debug-level 10 --ticker "TEST_DATA_OF_OHLCV" --precision 0 --timeframe 1440 --horizon 75 --max-close 140 --min-close 40 --init-close 50 --max-outlier 35 --max-body 25 --max-volume 4000000 --up-candles-prob 0.515 --outliers-prob 0.1 --trend-deviation 0.005 --split-trend "down-up" --split-count 40 35 --generate --render-google index.html
```

The generated OHLCV candles are stored in a Pandas DataFrame and are ready for further analysis in Python.

To see the series generated by PriceGenerator, a static or interactive chart is plotted and selected summary statistics are calculated automatically, as shown in Figure 1. The source figures come from a separate generator run with the parameters displayed on the figures themselves, so they do not reproduce the command above exactly.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-1" %}

The price chart visibly contains outliers above and below some candles. We are interested not in every such outlier, but only in excessively long tails, or candle shadows, and excessively large candle bodies.

Next, standard Pandas tools remove unnecessary data from the OHLCV DataFrame, leaving only price columns. We then calculate quantities derived from the OHLCV values:

- candle-body sizes: $\mathrm{body}=\lvert\mathrm{close}-\mathrm{open}\rvert$;

- upper candle shadows: $\mathrm{upper}=\mathrm{high}-\max(\mathrm{open},\mathrm{close})$;

- lower candle shadows: $\mathrm{lower}=\min(\mathrm{open},\mathrm{close})-\mathrm{low}$.

The resulting series of body sizes and shadow lengths are added to the DataFrame. HampelFilter() can then check each series for anomalies.

Let us see how well the modified Hampel filter identifies anomalous price outliers and candle sizes with its default parameters: window = 5, sigma = 3 and scaleFactor = 1.4826.

As the earlier examples showed, both very small and very large elements can be classified as anomalous relative to other elements in the sliding window. We mark anomalous candle bodies with ⮾, lower shadows with ↑ and upper shadows with ↓. Figure 2 shows the result.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

At first glance, the filter seems to have done a good job: anomalies have been found and marked. To an observer unaware of the small window size, however, these results are not obvious. Some marked elements appear unnecessary. Let us widen the sliding window to cover the entire numerical sequence, setting window = 75 in our example, and identify the anomalous elements again, as shown in Figure 3.

Now the chart marks only those elements that most observers looking at the whole picture would probably call anomalous relative to the other elements in the visible series.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

The Hampel method has limitations. For example, when implementing the algorithm, we may find that anomalies at the first or last position in a series are ignored by the original algorithm because it uses a sliding window. To work around this in our HampelFilter() implementation, we first extended the series at both ends by the window size. Only then could we detect anomalies in the first and last elements of the original series.

In practice, however, the Hampel filter is extremely effective. With modern libraries such as [Pandas](https://pandas.pydata.org/), it processes fairly large series of tens of thousands of elements in seconds. It is also readily optimised and parallelised, for example using [CUDA Python](https://developer.nvidia.com/cuda-python), Numba’s [@cuda.jit decorator](https://numba.readthedocs.io/en/stable/user/jit.html#compiling-python-code-with-jit), or a [Python multiprocessing ThreadPool](https://superfastpython.com/threadpool-python/).

Filtering with the modified Hampel method therefore provides an effective solution to anomaly detection in a numerical sequence. It gives fast results and is straightforward to understand and implement.
