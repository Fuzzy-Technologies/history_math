For several centuries in the Middle Ages, a fierce struggle took place between abacists—advocates of calculating with an abacus, who used Roman numerals—and algorists, who supported the emerging algorithmic methods of arithmetic using the new decimal place-value notation. The abacists and the Church fiercely resisted the spread of the new system. The abacus itself, however, was not to blame.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

The abacus, from Greek ἄβαξ, ἀβάκιον and Latin *abacus*, “a square tablet covered with dust,” is a counting board used for arithmetic in ancient Eastern cultures from approximately the fifth century BCE. It is believed to have first been used in Babylon. It was a wooden board sprinkled with sand, in which grooves were drawn. Pebbles representing digits were placed in these grooves. In Egypt, rods and wires threaded with stones replaced the lines and depressions. The first abacuses used a base-five number system.

Later, devices resembling the abacus came into use among almost all peoples, including those of ancient China and India and the Eastern Arabs. Their base-five systems were replaced by decimal ones.

In ancient Greece and then ancient Rome, the abacus replaced the older method of counting on the fingers. Its development made calculations quick, accurate and easy. A pebble on an abacus could represent units, tens, hundreds or thousands.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

In Western Europe, methods of calculation were influenced by the Western Arabs, who conquered Spain at the end of the eighth century. In the tenth century, the French monk and scholar Gerbert of Aurillac learned abacus calculation there, wrote a book about it and encouraged its use through his pupils. His influence as Pope Sylvester II helped the abacus spread widely in Europe. It remained in use until the eighteenth century.

Over the centuries, the abacus changed its form, but the principle stayed the same: ruled columns or otherwise designated positions for recording numbers by place value.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-5" %}

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-6" %}

In Eastern countries, the Chinese suanpan and Japanese soroban are widespread counterparts of the abacus. Their construction follows the same principle and uses the decimal number system, although the Japanese version is somewhat more economical. Both have meticulously developed sets of algorithms that mechanically—that is, without additional mental or written calculations—perform the four arithmetic operations and extract square and cube roots.

Like other tools, the abacus eventually ceased to be universally used, but it still serves educational purposes in some places. In Japan, for example, soroban use is part of the primary-school curriculum for teaching calculation.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-7" %}

The decimal abacus, or Russian *schoty*, which uses the decimal system and allows operations with quarters, tenths and hundredths, appeared in Russia around the turn of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. It remained in active commercial use until the final decade of the twentieth century. Calculating on counting frames was taught in schools in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. In the USSR, instruction in their use was removed in the early 1980s.

The mental arithmetic popular today also draws on the working principles of the abacus.
