One sign of the crisis in ancient Greek mathematics was the discovery of incommensurability. This was connected with the concept of infinity. Different scholars approached the problems of infinity in different ways.

One approach to constructing mathematics was proposed by the great philosopher Democritus.

Democritus of Abdera (Δημόκριτος, c. 460 BCE – c. 370 BCE) was an ancient Greek philosopher and one of the founders of atomism and materialist philosophy.

He was the first Greek scholar of encyclopedic breadth, working in philosophy, mathematics, physics, astronomy, medicine, philology, ethics and music theory.

Ancient authors mention some 70 different works by Democritus, none of which survives today. His extraordinarily rich legacy has come down to us in only 300 fragments.

At the heart of Democritus' philosophy is the atom: something indivisible, which neither comes into being nor passes away and forms the fundamental basis of everything that exists.

Democritus carried this philosophical conception over into mathematics. A point is an atom. A collection of points arranged in a particular way makes a straight line. A collection of straight lines makes a plane, and a collection of planes makes space. A collection of exceedingly thin circles makes a cylinder or a cone. Solids consist of a finite number of elementary parts, and the volume of a solid is obtained by adding the volumes of these parts. Democritus rejects infinite divisibility and incommensurability.

Democritus' atomistic theory is unsuitable for studying continuous quantities, because understanding them requires a passage to the limit.

For example, suppose a line segment, a continuous quantity, is an infinite collection of “indivisible” parts. If their magnitude is zero, the magnitude of the whole segment is also zero. If each “indivisible” part has some magnitude, the segment's magnitude will be infinite, since a small quantity repeated infinitely many times can become infinitely large. Thus the measure of a segment cannot be defined as the sum of the measures of its “indivisibles”. The measure of a set is not equal to the sum of the measures of its elements.

Nevertheless, Democritus' ideas were fruitful and were later developed by Archimedes. Democritus is believed to have been the first to establish that the volumes of a pyramid and a cone are respectively one-third the volumes of a prism and a cylinder with the same height and the same base area.

Democritus' approach later became the basis of the medieval “method of indivisibles” (B. Cavalieri), which in turn foreshadowed the theory of infinitesimals and integral calculus.

Even today, we use analogues of the concept of the “atom” when formulating problems in mathematical physics.
