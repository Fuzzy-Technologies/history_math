*Marking the 170th anniversary of his birth*

Andrei Andreyevich Markov (2 [14] June 1856–20 July 1922) was a Russian mathematician and a member of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences. His work spanned number theory, mathematical analysis and probability, and his name is inseparable from Markov chains and Markov’s inequality.

## St Petersburg University

He was born in Ryazan. Often ill as a boy, he used crutches for a long time, yet showed an early talent for mathematics. In 1874 he entered St Petersburg University, where he studied under P. L. Chebyshev and graduated in 1878.

Almost his entire life was linked with the university. From the late 1870s he taught and conducted research in St Petersburg, becoming a professor in 1886. He was elected an academician of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences in 1896. As a teacher, he combined unusual clarity with demanding standards of proof: rigorous logic always sustained the theory in his lectures.

## Numbers, Approximation and Dependence

In number theory he studied quadratic forms and extremal problems that led to the celebrated Markov numbers and equations. In analysis he worked extensively on continued fractions and best approximation, and investigated polynomials of least deviation from zero. These topics may now seem separate, but they meet in a single concern in his work: how precision is structured, and how to control it.

His widest fame came from probability. He was among the first to study sequences of dependent random variables systematically and show how limit laws could be extended to dependent cases. Markov chains became a language for processes in which the next step depends on the present state rather than the whole past.

To explain dependence through familiar material, he analysed the alternation of vowels and consonants in Pushkin’s *Eugene Onegin*. It was a powerful move for its time: the mathematical model arose from a real text and observed statistics rather than abstraction alone.

## A Textbook and a Public Stance

His textbook *Calculus of Probabilities* (1900) was another milestone, strongly influencing the development of Russia’s probability school. In the 1910s he publicly debated the place of probability in school mathematics, insisting on rigorous explanation rather than attractive words standing in for meaning.

In 1905 he resigned in protest against the government’s actions. In 1912 he petitioned the Holy Synod to secure a formal break with the Church, an episode remembered as an example of his principled stance.

His name is heard whenever dependence is discussed, in mathematics, statistics, physics, biology, linguistics and machine learning. Within mathematics, it remains a sign of precision and disciplined thought: even chance requires a rigorous language.
