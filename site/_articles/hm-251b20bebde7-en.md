How would you establish this property of simple quadrilaterals: all simple quadrilaterals with the same side midpoints have equal areas? School geometry could include another named theorem: this is a familiar property, but it is not usually called by a name.

Varignon’s theorem: “The quadrilateral whose vertices are the midpoints of the sides of any quadrilateral is a parallelogram.”

This parallelogram is called the quadrilateral’s Varignon parallelogram, though as yet unofficially. Its sides are parallel to the original quadrilateral’s diagonals and half as long.

Remarkably, the theorem holds for any quadrilateral: convex, nonconvex or self-intersecting. The picture shows quadrilateral ABCD and its Varignon parallelogram EFGH.

The Varignon parallelogram is a valuable aid in many plane-geometry problems, including olympiad problems. Here are some experiments to try for yourself.

## Experiments

1. Construct the Varignon parallelogram of a nonconvex and a self-intersecting quadrilateral.

2. Prove that the Varignon parallelogram’s perimeter equals the sum of the original quadrilateral’s diagonal lengths.

3. For a simple (non-self-intersecting) quadrilateral, prove that the Varignon parallelogram’s area is half that of the original quadrilateral.

## Generalisations

Exploring the properties of the Varignon parallelogram leads to important generalisations.

- A quadrilateral’s midlines, or bimedians, are the segments joining the midpoints of opposite sides. They are the diagonals of its Varignon parallelogram and bisect one another.

- A generalised Varignon parallelogram of a quadrilateral is any parallelogram whose vertices lie on its sides and whose sides are parallel to its diagonals. For a simple quadrilateral, its area does not exceed half the original quadrilateral’s area.

### Question

How would you establish this property: all quadrilaterals with the same side midpoints have equal areas?

### Answer

All these simple quadrilaterals share the same Varignon parallelogram. Their areas are twice its area.
