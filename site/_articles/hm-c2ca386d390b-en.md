*Marking the 225th anniversary of his birth*

Joseph Ludwig Raabe (1801–1859) was a Swiss mathematician and a notable figure in nineteenth-century mathematical analysis. His name is most often recalled through Raabe’s test for the convergence of series, which refines and strengthens the classical ratio test associated with d’Alembert.

## From Brody to Zurich

He was born on 15 May 1801 in Brody, Galicia, now in Ukraine, into a poor family. He began earning money from private lessons at an early age and largely taught himself mathematics. By nineteen he was able to move to Vienna, where he studied at the Polytechnic Institute while continuing to tutor. He also began publishing, including in *Crelle’s Journal*, one of the leading mathematical periodicals of the time.

After the cholera epidemic interrupted his private lessons in Vienna in autumn 1831, Raabe moved to Zurich in 1832. He taught mathematics at a Gymnasium and, from almost the beginning of the new University of Zurich, founded in 1833, gave courses there as a Privatdozent. He later became a professor, and in 1855 was invited to join Zurich’s newly established Federal Polytechnic School, the future ETH.

## Series, Integrals and Textbooks

His reputation rested above all on a paper on the convergence and divergence of series, published in 1834, which presented Raabe’s test. It is especially useful when the usual ratio test nearly works but does not settle the question, for example in series involving factorials and expressions with similar rates of growth.

His interests were broader, however. He worked extensively on definite integrals, Bernoulli numbers and polynomials, and asymptotic estimates, including those arising around the gamma function and factorial approximations. One intriguing detail is that he introduced the term “Bernoulli polynomials” in one of his papers.

His textbooks also became an important part of his legacy. He wrote a three-volume course on differential and integral calculus, published in 1839–1847, and issued *Mathematische Mittheilungen* in 1857–1858. For generations of students and engineers, these were tools for mathematical work: rigorous, yet directed towards practical applications.

He died in Zurich in January 1859. His memory survives in more than the name of a test: his work illustrates how nineteenth-century mathematics learned to be both precise and practical.
