Ancient Rome had an interesting system of fractions. It divided the basic unit of weight or money, and later any quantity, into 12 parts called *unciae*. A Roman might say that he had travelled seven unciae of a journey. Thus an uncia meant 1/12 of any quantity.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

The Romans charged debtors interest: money beyond the amount originally lent. They might say, for example: *“For every 100 sestertii of debt, you will pay 16 sestertii in interest.”*

In such calculations, fractions serve as units of measurement. Such units led to the concept of the percentage. Its history is discussed in I. Ya. Depman's *History of Arithmetic*.

## Historical teaching situations and percentage problems {#section-1}

1\. We use the word “percent” very often. It has entered every school subject and everyday practice in every sphere of human activity. Has this always been so? From textbooks, literature and television, we know that bankers, businesspeople and economists have used, and still use, this term particularly often. Give examples of percentages in everyday life and science. Where are they most common?

2\. Mark true statements with “+” and false ones with “–”:

- The word “percent” comes from the Latin words *pro centum*.
- Percentages allow parts to be compared with one another.
- The idea of percentages originated with the Babylonians.
- The Romans called the money they received as profit on each hundred “percentages”.
- In practice, it is more convenient to use thousandths, or “per mille”.
- The % sign comes from the operation of division by one hundred.
- The curious word “scrupulously” comes from the Roman term *scrupulus*.

3\. Read and analyse the following passage about the history of percentages from G. I. Gleyzer's *History of Mathematics at School: Grades IV–VI*. Mark facts that interest you with different symbols: “V”, “I know this”; “+”, “This is interesting or unexpected”; “–”, “I thought otherwise”; “?”, “I want to know more”. This approach is called an interactive system for effective reading and reflection.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

> “The word ‘percent’ comes from the Latin words *pro centum*, literally ‘for a hundred’ or ‘out of a hundred’. Percentages are very convenient in practice because they express parts of whole numbers consistently in the same hundredths. This simplifies calculations and makes it easy to compare parts with one another and with the whole.

> The idea of consistently expressing parts of a whole in the same fractions arose from practical considerations among the ancient Babylonians, who used sexagesimal fractions. Several problems on cuneiform tablets concern the calculation of interest, but Babylonian moneylenders reckoned ‘out of sixty’, rather than ‘out of a hundred’.

> Percentages were especially widespread in ancient Rome. The Romans called the money a debtor paid the lender on each hundred ‘percentages’. From Rome, percentages passed to the other peoples of Europe.

> For a long time, percentages meant exclusively the profit or loss on every hundred roubles. They were used only in commercial and monetary references. Their applications then broadened to economic and financial calculations, statistics, science and technology. Today, a percentage is a particular kind of decimal fraction: a hundredth of a whole taken as one unit.

> In some questions, smaller fractions — thousandths — are also used. These are called ‘per mille’, from Latin *pro mille*, ‘out of a thousand’, and written ‰, by analogy with the percentage sign %. In practice, however, thousandths are usually too small and tenths too large. Hundredths, or percentages, are therefore the most convenient.”

4\. Write three questions about percentages that you would like answered. For example, what should we call Babylonian “percentages”, since they are calculated out of sixty rather than a hundred?

The questions may be factual — Who? What? When? — or creative: Explain why; Why do you think so? What is the difference? What would happen if…?

5\. Explain the solution of the problem quoted by Gleyzer.

The celebrated Roman poet Horace gives a conversation between teacher and pupil in a Roman school of the period:

> *“Teacher: Let Albinus' son tell us what remains if one uncia is taken away from five. Pupil: One third. Teacher: Correct; you will know how to look after your property.”*

6\. Return to activity 2. Check your answers. Were your ideas correct? What have you learned? Which question remains unanswered?

## Answers and historical background {#section-2}

1\. Interest calculations were widely practised in Babylon, as surviving interest tables show. The term “percent” does not suit Babylonian moneylending operations, because the accumulated capital was calculated out of sixty units, not a hundred. Annual interest was 12 shekels on 1 mina, equal to 60 shekels: in our notation, 20%. This “pro-sexagesimal” rate is used in all the problems.

From the thirteenth to the sixteenth century, every textbook devoted considerable space to interest calculations, because merchants made up a large proportion of their readers, as in the works of Leonardo of Pisa and Luca Pacioli. Arabic arithmetic and algebra textbooks, which served as sources and models for European authors, were filled with property calculations under the highly complex Islamic inheritance laws. Simon Stevin published the first printed interest tables in 1584 and included them in his *Arithmetic* in 1585.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

2\. The term “capital” comes from Latin *caput*, “head” or “chief”: it was the amount entered in account books at the head of the calculations. “Interest” comes from the Latin phrase *id quod inter est*, what lies between the capital lent and the amount received back.

Sometimes we say, “He studied the question scrupulously”. This means it has been examined thoroughly, leaving not even the smallest uncertainty. The word comes, it turns out, from the Roman name for 1/288 of a unit, *scrupulus*, which is 1/24 of an uncia.

L. F. Magnitsky's *Arithmetic* contains almost no percentage problems. This is explained by the fact that bills of exchange were not permitted in merchants' practice until 1729.

3\. The % sign is believed to come from the Italian *cento*, “hundred”, often abbreviated *cto* in percentage calculations. Further simplification in handwriting, turning the letter t into a slash, produced the modern percentage symbol.

5\. $\frac{5}{12} - \frac{1}{12} = \frac{1}{3}$.
