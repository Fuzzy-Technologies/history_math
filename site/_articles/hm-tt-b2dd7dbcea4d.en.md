The discovery of incommensurability prompted Greek philosophers to seek a way out of the crisis in mathematics. The Greeks began to build mathematics on geometry rather than on the arithmetic of rational numbers. They created what is known as geometric algebra. Its basic objects were line segments and rectangles, for which addition and subtraction were defined.

It made no sense to add quantities of different kinds, such as rectangles and line segments, so the system of calculation defined in geometric algebra was graded. All rules, theorems and problems were formulated in terms of relationships between the lengths of segments and the areas of rectilinear figures.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-2" %}

Geometric constructions used straight lines and circles: Greek mathematics thus became a theory of constructions with compass and straightedge. This raised a question: could every geometric problem be solved in the same way? As early as the fifth century BCE, problems appeared that resisted compass-and-straightedge solutions. These are the famous three classical problems of antiquity, which were finally settled only in the nineteenth century.

1. Doubling the cube. Construct a cube with twice the volume of a given cube.

2. Trisecting an angle. Divide an arbitrary angle into three equal parts.

3. Squaring the circle. Construct a square equal in area to a given circle.

The problem of doubling the cube is called the “Delian problem”, after a well-known legend. Plague broke out on the island of Delos. Its inhabitants asked an oracle for advice. The oracle told them to double the golden altar to Apollo, which was shaped like a cube. The Delians cast another cube, but the plague continued.

They had solved the problem incorrectly. Algebraically, doubling the cube reduces to a cubic equation, whose solution requires constructing a segment equal to the cube root of 2. The Greeks were unable to do this.

Hippocrates of Chios, in the fifth century BCE, reduced the problem to finding two mean proportionals, or “insertions”, forming a continued proportion.

Archytas of Tarentum (428–365 BCE) solved it using a certain spatial curve, so his solution was not a classical construction. Other mathematicians who attempted it, including Plato, Eratosthenes, Heron and Nicomedes, also used mechanisms other than compass and straightedge.

Trisecting an angle also reduces to a cubic equation. In antiquity, however, such an equation could not be obtained. Mathematicians of the East did so in the Middle Ages. Only in 1837 did the French mathematician Pierre Wantzel (1814–1848) prove that the cubic equations arising from doubling the cube and trisecting an angle are not, in general, solvable by square radicals, and therefore cannot be solved with compass and straightedge. Certain angles can nevertheless be trisected in particular cases, including a right angle.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-3" %}

Hippias of Elis, in the fifth century BCE, gave the first solution to angle trisection by nonclassical means. He provided a way to construct a special curve, the trisectrix, which Leibniz called the quadratrix in a particular case; this curve supplied a solution to the trisection problem.

{% include article-figure.html id="fig-4" %}

The quadratrix can also be used to square the circle, as Hippias’ pupil Dinostratus did in the fourth century BCE. Since the problem reduces to an equation involving π (pi), it is equivalent to constructing a segment equal to π.

In 1882, the German mathematician Carl Lindemann (1852–1939) rigorously proved that π is transcendental: it cannot be a root of an algebraic equation with rational coefficients. Squaring the circle with compass and straightedge is therefore impossible.

While tackling these ancient problems, mathematicians encountered others, such as constructing regular polygons. These problems stimulated the further development of mathematics.

You can learn more about the classical mathematical problems of antiquity in the book [History of Mathematics](https://ridero.ru/books/istoriya_matematiki/).
